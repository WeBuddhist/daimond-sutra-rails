# Extensive Commentary on the Noble Diamond Cutter Perfection of Wisdom by Kamalaśīla ^0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^0-1]]

An Extensive Commentary on the Noble Prajñāpāramitā, the Diamond Cutter. ^0-1

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^0-2]]

In the Indian language: Āryaprajñāpāramitāvajracchedikāṭīkā. In the Tibetan language: An Extensive Commentary on the Noble Prajñāpāramitā, the Diamond Cutter. Homage to Mañjuśrīkumārabhūta! Although whatever is renowned to all beings appears clearly before them, she perfectly reveals that they are not found to possess the essence of real entities; she is the stable foundation by which all the Victorious Ones attain the genuine, holy, eternal, and supreme fruit; and she gives birth to all the buddhas abiding in the three times—to her I pay homage with deep devotion! Because familiarizing oneself with her even slightly for but a single instant accumulates vast virtue, and completely eradicates all exceedingly great misdeeds from their very root, therefore, in order to cultivate familiarity with her, having examined the path taught by the gurus, I shall compose this according to the capacity of my intellect. ^0-2

## First, Showing the Purpose and Subject Matter of the Method of Explaining the Sūtra ^1-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^1-1]]

Here, one who wishes to explain the sūtra should first state its purpose, in order to engage the listeners in taking it up earnestly and so forth. Without realizing the purpose, intelligent people will not engage in anything at all. Next, in order to show the method for accomplishing the purpose of the sūtra, the subject matter should be stated. A sūtra empty of subject matter does not accomplish the desired purpose, just like words such as 'ten pomegranates'. Next, in order to make that subject matter easy to grasp, the summarized meaning should be stated. Next, in order to understand the summarized meaning, the meaning of the words should be stated. Next, in order to show that the sequence is not contradictory, the connections among the words should be shown. Next, in order to clear away logical contradictions between earlier and later parts, replies to objections should be stated. The procedure is like that. In this regard, the very name of this sūtra, the Diamond Cutter, indicates its subject matter and purpose. Thus, it is the Diamond Cutter in two ways. Because it cuts the subtle afflictive obscurations and cognitive obscurations that are difficult to destroy like a diamond, this indicates that its purpose is the relinquishing of the two obscurations. Alternatively, 'cutter' means it is like a vajra because it resembles the shape of a vajra. A vajra is made thick at its two ends and thin in the middle. Similarly, this prajñāpāramitā also thoroughly teaches as vast the level of action through aspiration at the beginning, and the level of a buddha at the end. By being thin in the middle, it indicates the level of pure extraordinary resolve. Therefore, this is like the shape of a vajra; this indicates that these three levels are its subject matter. ^1-1

## Showing the Summary ^2-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^2-1]]

The summarized meaning, in brief, has five aspects. They are as follows: the setting, the initiation of the discourse, ensuring the uninterrupted lineage of the buddhas, the characteristics of practice, and its locus. ^2-1

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^2-2]]

In this regard, the setting is the cause for the sūtra to arise, and that is shown starting from "Thus have I heard" up to "Then the venerable Subhūti". Therefore, it is stated by the authentic compiler in order to inspire trust in himself, and it should be stated for all sūtras. The initiation of the discourse is the connection to the context in order to explain the meaning of the sūtra. Because if spoken out of context, everything would become confused, in order to clear away confusion, the connection to the context should be made in all cases. That is shown by "Then the venerable Subhūti rose from his seat" and so forth. Ensuring the uninterrupted lineage of the buddhas is also shown by that very passage. Since the noble Subhūti knew that this prajñāpāramitā makes the lineage of the buddhas uninterrupted, the elder Subhūti first laid out exactly how the lineage of the buddhas becomes uninterrupted. The characteristics of practice are shown by "How should one who has genuinely entered the bodhisattva vehicle abide?" and so forth. The locus of those characteristics of practice is whichever object a bodhisattva should abide in, practice, and perfectly control the mind in—that is called its locus. That, moreover, is shown starting from "Subhūti, here, one who has genuinely entered the bodhisattva vehicle should think thus, 'I...'" up to the final verse, "A star, a visual aberration," and so forth. These two, the characteristics of practice and its locus, completely show the entirety of a bodhisattva's activities; this is the summarized meaning for now. ^2-2

## Explaining the Meaning of the Words ^3-0

### Explaining the Setting ^3-1-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-1]]

The meaning of the words and so forth should also be stated; those will be stated. In this regard, although the term 'Thus' appears in many meanings such as acceptance and so forth, here, by the power of the context and so forth, it should be understood as a presentation. This presents the entirety of the meaning of the sūtra occurring below. 'By me' excludes others and indicates that the meaning was heard directly. The implication is: 'I heard it myself; it did not come solely through transmission.' 'Heard' negates realization, because other than a buddha, there is no one else who realizes such dharmas by themselves. This clears away lack of trust. 'At one time' means at a single time; it indicates that such rare and supreme dharmas are difficult to hear at all times. Alternatively, it shows that he himself has heard much. It indicates: 'At one time I heard this, but at other times I heard other things as well.' Alternatively, connecting it with what follows as 'at one time the Blessed One was dwelling,' it establishes that for the purpose of benefiting those immeasurable trainees, he was dwelling elsewhere at other times. 'Blessed One' means that because he possesses the conquering of the four māras, which have the characteristics of the afflictions, the aggregates, the lord of death, and the child of the gods, he is called 'Blessed One' by way of etymology. ^3-1

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-2]]

Alternatively, because he is endowed with sovereignty and so forth, he is called 'Glorious'. As it is said: 'Complete endowment with sovereignty, physical beauty, glory, and renown, wisdom, and diligent exertion—these six are called "glory".' 'Śrāvastī' means that because it is the hermitage of the sage Śrāvasta, it is the city of Śrāvastī; because the particular region characterized by it is jointly connected with it, it is called Śrāvastī. The place is mentioned in order to declare that the place has become a stūpa; thus, even in this very text it is stated, 'that place becomes a stūpa.' Alternatively, the authentic compiler states all these in order to establish that his words are worthy of being grasped. Thus, if they are spoken along with the location, along with the circumstances, and along with the master, for the purpose of inspiring trust in the world, others will grasp the words; otherwise, the words would not be worthy of being grasped. Since the region of Śrāvastī is very vast and it would not be known exactly where he was dwelling, 'the grove of Prince Jeta' is mentioned; because it is the grove of that one called Prince Jeta, it is the grove of Prince Jeta. Since it is possible that even that might not be renowned to anyone at all, 'in Anāthapiṇḍada's park' is mentioned. The householder 'Anāthapiṇḍada' was renowned as such because out of compassion for the protectorless he gave them alms; since he bought the park from Prince Jeta by laying down precious jewels side by side for the sake of the Blessed One, and since Prince Jeta also had some pavilions and so forth adorned for the Blessed One from that, it is renowned as the grove of those two. If only any one of these were mentioned, the desire to express the specific place would not be understood, so all three phrases are mentioned. Alternatively, this mention of 'Śrāvastī' indicates the perfection of benefiting others, because he dwells among the multitudes. ^3-2

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-3]]

This mention of 'the grove of Prince Jeta, Anāthapiṇḍada's park' indicates the perfection of benefiting oneself, because he dwells in solitude. Thinking, 'Who possesses mastery in this?', 'a great saṅgha of monks' is mentioned; because they are free from afflictions, they are 'monks'. Because they cannot be divided by opponents such as māras and so forth, those multitudes are called a 'saṅgha'. Those of the 'great saṅgha of monks' have two greatnesses: multitude and power; this indicates greatness in power. Thinking, 'What is the greatness of multitude?', 'one thousand two hundred and fifty' and so forth is mentioned, because at that time there were that many. That the bodhisattvas were exceedingly more numerous than the saṅgha of monks is because the sūtra is taught for their sake. Because they possess intentions with great courage for awakening, they are 'bodhisattvas'. Since śrāvakas also have courage for awakening, in order to exclude them, 'great beings' is mentioned. Because they are endowed with seven kinds of great characteristics—dharma, generation of the mind, inclination, intention, accumulation, time, and perfect accomplishment—they are 'great beings'. In this regard, 'great dharma' is because the teaching of dharma to bodhisattvas, such as the prajñāpāramitā in one hundred thousand lines and so forth, is extensive. ^3-3

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-4]]

'Great generation of the mind' is because they generate the mind toward unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening. 'Great inclination' is because they themselves are inclined toward the profound and great dharma. 'Great intention' is because they possess the intention to generate benefit and happiness for all sentient beings. 'Great accumulation' is because their accumulations of merit and wisdom are immeasurable. 'Great time' is because they attain awakening over three countless great eons. 'Great perfect accomplishment' is because they perfectly accomplish unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening. 'Dwelling' means dwelling by means of the four postures, or rather, dwelling by means of the unsurpassed abodes of Brahmā, the gods, and the noble ones. The particle 'te' indicates that the dwelling was already established. In this regard, having understood that the saṅgha of monks stays right at the feet of the Tathāgata at all times because they have done what was to be done and are objects of compassion, they are mentioned first in order to indicate their constancy. Bodhisattvas are mentioned later because, not having fully completed the accomplishment of the welfare of immeasurable sentient beings, they possess great compassion for those who have not done what is to be done, and roam throughout the worldly realms in order to accomplish the welfare of transmigrators, so they do not stay at the feet of the Blessed One at all times. ^3-4

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-5]]

Therefore, bodhisattvas are not mentioned in the sūtras of the śrāvakas, because those are not for their sake, and because they are not constant. 'Then' means after that. 'Time' means certainty; this determines the time for food. Determining the time means determining the time as 'at this time one should certainly eat'. Furthermore, not knowing what that is, it is to be explained by mentioning 'morning'; this is compounded as 'it is both the morning and the time of the occasion'. The characteristic of the morning is that it becomes the time for those observing fasting vows and those who have gone forth to obtain food; in order to understand that it was conclusively determined by the Blessed One, 'the time of the occasion' is mentioned. Otherwise, mentioning only 'morning' would not be understood as conclusively determined; because it is possible that the Blessed One might act casually, therefore all three words are mentioned. 'Under-robe' refers to putting on robes in a way conforming to householders; and that is done in order to establish reverence for the trainings among the śrāvakas. Otherwise, how could one who is untamed himself tame others? Regarding 'putting on the dharma robes', putting on the dharma robes should be seen as having the characteristic of donning them; otherwise it is not the context. 'Great city' is a specific region, meaning the specification of Śrāvastī. ^3-5

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-6]]

If one asks, 'Why did the Blessed One enter for alms?': It is to guide the śrāvakas to having few desires, to benefit housewives, the poor, the sick, the elderly, and so forth, to cause the blind and so forth to obtain eyes and so forth, and to generate faith in the Blessed One in others by continually showing various marvelous things; thus one should understand that the purposes are immeasurable. 'Having done the activity of food' means that it is spoken thus regarding one who has done and accomplished the activity of food, such as food and so forth. If even those born from small roots of virtue, such as Brahmā and so forth, do not perform the activity of food because they are free from the attachment of eating food by mouthfuls, would the Blessed One, born from inconceivable roots of virtue, do so? Likewise, it is also said in the noble Dharmasaṃgīti: 'The Tathāgata does not possess hunger and thirst, does not defecate and urinate; His body is not frail.' That is true, but nevertheless, if that body of the Blessed One were not ultimate, then there would be occasion for all criticisms; but when this is the emanation body of the Blessed One, displaying such kinds of behavior and engaging in accordance with the intentions of the sentient beings to be tamed, at that time how could there be occasion for criticism? In order to encourage them by saying, 'Even a human being who exerts effort attains such an unsurpassed state,' He displays a human body to those to be tamed, and thus displays the manner of taking a meal. ^3-6

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-7]]

By showing the complete purification of alms to whatever donors and benefactors, it generates joy in them; and those who have the fortune but, due to some karmic obscuration, have been born among the hungry ghosts and so forth, if touched by the nectar-like fingernails of the Blessed One's hand, it becomes the supreme taste of the gods, and when they eat the inexhaustible food carried by majestic yakṣas and so forth, their minds become satisfied with supreme happiness, and since they also obtain supreme samādhi, the activity of food of the Blessed One is immeasurable. If one asks, 'With what kind of aspect did He perform the activity of food?', therefore 'at the end' and so forth is mentioned; having received everything, whatever meal is eaten at the end is 'eating the alms meal at the end', and this is preceded by properly taking it up in order to guide the śrāvakas toward the qualities of purification. 'Ate' is a word meaning that He had already eaten. Alternatively, regarding 'at the end', whatever is received a second time is called 'subsequent food'; that subsequent food is compounded in words as 'it is both the food at the end and alms'. Alternatively, whatever alms it is that consists of eating that afternoon food is spoken of in that way. Therefore, because of possessing the qualities of purification and abandoning food at the wrong time, 'ate' is a word meaning that He is free from those two. 'Having put away' means putting away. ^3-7

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-8]]

Furthermore, although those who exert themselves in taking orders, such as Śakra and so forth, constantly remain in the presence of the Blessed One, the Blessed One Himself put away the alms bowl and dharma robes in order to show that those who have gone forth do it themselves. It is also in order to eliminate the mental displeasure of those with small fortune who lack servants. Although the appearance of impurity is constantly abandoned far away and He places His feet upon lotuses, one should regard washing His feet as being in accordance with the intention of sentient beings who are inclined toward the Action Tantras and so forth, or in order to show reverence for the trainings such as 'one with filthy feet should not trample on bedding and seats'. 'On the arranged seat' refers to it being arranged by gods and so forth. 'Straightening the body' is a word meaning that He acts without bending down too much or leaning back too much. If one asks, 'Why did the Blessed One dwell in the posture of abandonment at the time of teaching the dharma?': It is in order to guide the world into generating exertion in abandonment, saying, 'Only those of abandonment are capable of teaching, knowing, and hearing this dharma, whereas others are not.' 'Establishing mindfulness in front' is a word meaning that He actualized the mindfulness of the prayer He had made previously when He was a bodhisattva, 'When I attain the unsurpassed state, I shall act in such a way that the lineage of the Buddhas will not be interrupted.' Although the Buddha Blessed Ones constantly rest in equipoise and thus always abide in mindfulness everywhere, mindfulness at the wrong time achieves nothing, whereas mindfulness of the prayer at the right time is accompanied by its result. Since mindfulness at the right time also shows that the Blessed One is constantly resting in equipoise, there is no contradiction. ^3-8

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-9]]

If one asks, 'Why is "Then many monks" mentioned?': It is in order to show that, of however many in number the saṅgha of monks with whom the Blessed One was dwelling in Śrāvastī, not all of them assembled there at the time of teaching the dharma, but rather that 'many'—three or more—assembled there. Furthermore, it was spoken by the compiler in order to generate confidence in others. 'Assembled' merely means drawing near. 'Sat down' refers to sitting on a seat. Because the world mostly establishes the specific preceded by establishing the general, both are mentioned in accordance with worldly convention. Otherwise, by just 'sat down' alone, both would be mentioned. The meaning of the words of the prologue and so forth has been explained. ^3-9

### Explaining the Initiation of the Discourse and the Uninterrupted Lineage of the Buddha ^3-2-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-10]]

Now, the raising of the topic and making the lineage of the Buddhas uninterrupted will be explained. In order to show that it is extremely wonderful, it is mentioned twice. 'Sugata' is a vocative. He has properly gone via the path of the two kinds of selflessness. Because He has gone well, He is Sugata, just like saying 'well-praised'. Alternatively, having abandoned the afflictive and cognitive obscurations along with their latencies without exception, because He has gone, He is Sugata, just like a completely full vase. Alternatively, because He has gone without returning, He is Sugata, just like a completely cured infectious disease. 'With whatever' is a specific detail of the benefit to be given; it is a word meaning that He has benefited with however many benefits there are. If one suspects, thinking, 'If even kings and so forth care for subjects and so forth who rely on them as much as they are able, what is so wonderful about that here concerning the Blessed One?', as the cause of wonder, 'by the Tathāgata, the Arhat, the perfectly and fully awakened Buddha' is mentioned. Because He has spoken of the reality of entities exactly as it is, He is the Tathāgata. Alternatively, just as previous ones have gone via the path of the two kinds of selflessness and attained the abandonment of the afflictive and cognitive obscurations, so here too He has gone, therefore He is the Tathāgata. ^3-10

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-11]]

Because He is worthy of being venerated by the entire world, or because He has destroyed the enemies that are the afflictions, He is the Arhat. Because He has properly and thoroughly understood all objects of knowledge, He is the perfectly and fully awakened Buddha. By those words, the complete perfection of all the Blessed One's intrinsic purposes, wisdom and abandonment, is completely shown. By that, it comes to show this: that kings and so forth benefiting subjects and so forth is not very wonderful here, because they benefit others out of desiring their own purposes such as gain and respect, whereas the Blessed One is the Tathāgata; having destroyed the enemies and perfectly and fully awakened, and having attained all complete perfections, whatever benefits He gives, those are shown to be wonderful here. Regarding this benefiting by the Blessed One, since there is no other cause for benefiting whatsoever besides great compassion, this aspect shows the Blessed One to be great compassion itself. 'Benefited' means that, having manifestly and perfectly awakened, at the time the Blessed One turned the wheel of dharma, He caused bodhisattvas whose roots of virtue were completely ripened to enter the bhūmis, in order to make the bodhisattvas thoroughly settle into the dharmatā. ^3-11

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-12]]

'Entrusted' means that beginners whose roots of virtue are not completely ripened are entrusted to those very ones who have been benefited, in order to make the bodhisattvas thoroughly settle into the dharmatā. Showing 'I pass into complete nirvāṇa' means saying, 'Whatever you do, act such that those who have not attained qualities will attain them, and that they will not completely decline from those they have attained.' Thus, by both 'benefited' and 'entrusted', making the lineage of the Buddhas uninterrupted is completely shown. Benefiting the bodhisattvas should be understood as supreme through five aspects: time, distinction, expansiveness, stability, and pervasiveness. Regarding that, time: because it is in other lifetimes. Distinction: because it is especially exalted above the benefiting done by non-Buddhists, śrāvakas, and pratyekabuddhas. Expansiveness: because those benefits are unsurpassed. Stability: because it is permanent. Pervasiveness: because that benefit benefits the mind-streams of oneself and others. 'Entrusted' should also be seen as supreme through three aspects: the basis, the thorough attainment of the dharmatā, and the giving of the command. ^3-12

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-13]]

If one asks, 'What is the basis?': Because they are entrusted to spiritual friends through whom they will not be wasted. If one asks, 'What is the thorough attainment of the dharmatā?': Because those benefited bodhisattvas are made to benefit others by means of the dharmatā. If one asks, 'What is the giving of the command?': Because they are entrusted with the command, 'You should benefit other bodhisattvas,' and not without respect. The meaning of the words and so forth of the raising of the topic and making the lineage of the Buddhas uninterrupted has been explained. ^3-13

### Explaining the Characteristics of Practice ^3-3-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-14]]

The characteristic of accomplishment will be explained. In relation to that, 'how' is stated. The noble Subhūti asked for six reasons: to sever doubts, to generate devotion, to cause engagement in the profound meaning, to make irreversible, to generate supreme joy, and to make the true dharma remain for a long time. Regarding that, it is to sever the doubts of those who suspect, thinking, 'How does this prajñāpāramitā make the lineage of the Buddhas uninterrupted?'; to generate devotion to the prajñāpāramitā for bodhisattvas whose mind-streams are not completely ripened, by proclaiming its abundant merit; to cause those whose mind-streams are completely ripened to engage in the profound meaning; to make irreversible those who, afflicted by incorrect accomplishment, undertake taking it up and holding it, since they desire abundant merit; to generate supreme joy for those of pure superior intention who are to be benefited, by seeing that that dharma is realized by themselves; and to make the dharma of the Mahāyāna remain for a long time in the future. In brief, it is to properly instruct those who have doubts, to cause unripened bodhisattvas who desire merit to properly grasp it, to properly encourage those who are afflicted by wrong accomplishment, and to cause those of pure intention to be exceedingly joyful. Regarding that, 'should abide' is by means of both aspiration and prayer. 'Should accomplish' is by means of resting in meditative equipoise in yoga. ^3-14

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-15]]

'The mind should be thoroughly controlled' is by having suppressed distraction. Regarding that, aspiration is manifest desire. Prayer is whichever intention has the characteristic of manifestly compounding the mind toward the manifestly desired object. Resting in meditative equipoise in yoga is whichever samādhi is non-conceptual. Suppressing distraction is retrieving the mind from being distracted from samādhi and causing it to engage in that very samādhi. By the first, the path of application is completely shown; by the second, the path of accomplishment; and by the third, the path of non-wastage. Because he asked about abiding, the Blessed One gave to the noble Subhūti the words 'well done'. 'Listen' means making one lend an ear and listen. 'Well' means making one grasp it unmistakably. 'Bear it thoroughly in mind' means making one grasp it well. Those, furthermore, were spoken in order to abandon the three types of listening individuals, who are like a vessel turned upside down, an impure one, and a leaky one, in corresponding order. 'I will explain' is a word meaning that if they are so, He will explain, but otherwise He will not; otherwise, like rain falling into vessels that are turned upside down, impure, or leaky, this rain of dharma would become meaningless. Alternatively, it is a word meaning, 'I am merely explaining, but you must strive to accomplish it.' ^3-15

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-16]]

The vehicle of the bodhisattvas is the bodhisattva vehicle, by which bodhisattvas definitely emerge. That, moreover, is comprised of the ten perfections and the ten bhūmis; 'properly entered into that' means having entered with the minds of aspiration and application. That specific detail is in order to exclude the awakening of śrāvakas and so forth, because if He had said 'properly entered into awakening', since there are three awakenings, it would be understood as other awakenings as well. 'Listening' is a word meaning 'has promised'. The meaning of the words and so forth of the characteristic of accomplishment has been explained. ^3-16

### Explaining the Bases of the Characteristics of Practice ^3-4-0

#### Showing Briefly ^3-4-1-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-17]]

Now, its basis will be explained. If that basis, moreover, is summarized, there are eighteen aspects, as follows: generating the mind; engaging in the perfections; the aspiration to attain the form body; the aspiration to attain the dharma body; the absence of manifest pride regarding the aspiration for distinctions in meditation; pleasing the arising of Buddhas; ripening pure realms; completely ripening sentient beings; freedom from attachment to treatises [concerning] 'for what reason?'; application to destroying the apprehension of the world of sentient beings and the receptacle world as a whole; making offerings and showing reverence to the Tathāgatas; freedom from turning away from joyous effort due to the body and mind being fatigued, from not striving, and from gain and honor, that is, when body and mind are thoroughly fatigued, one does not turn away from having initiated joyous effort; not striving is not initiating it; and desiring gain and honor is influenced by faith, thinking, 'would that others know me, thinking, "this one is endowed with joyous effort!"'—therefore, a bodhisattva should be free from all those and abandon them; being indifferent to suffering; freedom from tasting the flavor of concentration; freedom from the conceptualization thinking 'it is I' at the time of manifest realization; thoroughly seeking instructions; manifest realization; and thoroughly seeking the bhūmi of a Buddha. Those eighteen aspects of the basis are that upon which a bodhisattva should abide, what should be accomplished, and how the mind should be thoroughly controlled. By those bases, three bhūmis are comprised: as follows, by sixteen aspects of the basis, the bhūmi of engaging through devotion; by the single basis of manifest realization, the bhūmi of pure superior intention; and by thoroughly seeking the bhūmi of a Buddha, the bhūmi of a Buddha. Having generated the mind for the sake of the complete nirvāṇa of all sentient beings, thoroughly striving in the perfections generates the aspiration to attain the form body and dharma body of a Tathāgata. Then, one makes the mind free from the hindrances to manifest realization, beginning with manifest pride and so forth, down to finally the conceptualization thinking 'it is I'. Then, one thoroughly seeks instructions in order to manifestly realize. Then, one manifestly realizes. Then, one thoroughly seeks the higher bhūmi of a Buddha. Therefore, the sequence of those is like that. ^3-17

#### Explaining Extensively ^3-4-2-0

##### Explaining the Generation of the Mind ^3-4-2-1-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-18]]

Regarding that, with reference to generating the mind toward awakening, 'however many sentient beings' was spoken. 'However many' is the measure. To the thought, 'Is there a measure for them?', 'comprised as sentient beings' was spoken. Although the realm of sentient beings is immeasurable, nevertheless, what is called 'sentient being, sentient being' is whichever is renowned as the nature of similar lot, which is imputed from the meaning of the word that is the exclusion of whatever is thought to be its nature; that is what is comprised as sentient beings. Because, by having combined the seen object and the conceptualized object into one, one superimposes it as its nature, they are comprised by the specific aggregates of form and so forth; therefore, they appear to be endowed with life. Having taught it generally in that way, in order to teach it specifically, 'born from an egg' and so forth were spoken. That characteristic of specific detail, moreover, was spoken by means of three divisions: from the division of birthplaces, there are four types, 'born from an egg' and so forth; from the division of abodes, because the abodes of those with form and those without form are distinct, those with form are those engaging in the desire and form realms, and those without form are the formless ones; from the division of apprehending characteristics, there is 'with perception' and so forth; except for one part of Great Fruit and the sentient beings born in the Peak of Existence, all are with perception. ^3-18

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-19]]

One part of Great Fruit is without perception. This is also precisely made as the division of apprehending characteristics, because it is indicated by that, having been divided by its absence. Those born in the Peak of Existence are neither without perception nor not without perception. This division was made in order to benefit those with weak intellects; in order to abandon the derision of others, it was not extensively elaborated. In order to teach that 'all dharmas are without sentient beings' and this explanation are not contradictory, and in order to teach the realm of sentient beings as a conventional truth, 'however many' and so forth were spoken. 'By designation' means knowing them as having the nature of the specific five aggregates; it is a word meaning that they are not as imputed to be completely known by the tīrthikas. By this, the extreme of deprecation is abandoned, because if they exist imputedly, one does not cast deprecation. The expanse of non-abiding nirvāṇa in which there is no remainder of contaminated aggregates is called thus, but the complete nirvāṇa of the śrāvakas is not the expanse of nirvāṇa here, because that is inferior. It is not logical that those whose nature is compassion would seek an inferior fruit while an excellent fruit exists. ^3-19

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-20]]

If one says, 'Even though an excellent fruit exists, because one is unable to attain it, one seeks it in that way': according to your system, because there are three lineages and also those without lineage, even an inferior fruit cannot be attained by everyone. However, the two lineages of bodhisattvas are like this: by the power of which, those who are overwhelmed by the force of great compassion abandon such considerations as thinking, 'Is this possible?' and thinking, 'While other Buddhas and bodhisattvas who act for the benefit of others also reside here, what is the use of my making a meaningless prayer?'; and, having cast away reliance on others, those who are held by great compassion, seeing beings afflicted by the blazing fire of suffering, make the prayer thinking thus: 'I alone will protect them'; if one says it is as was explained: 'Having completely abandoned the conceptualization thinking "Is it difficult or not difficult?", you yourself promised: "These beings are distressed,"' even if that is so, why would one not make a prayer precisely for the excellent one that cannot be attained, like praying for a beloved son to become king? Why would one seek only the inferior? Therefore, it is logical that non-abiding nirvāṇa itself is the expanse of nirvāṇa without remainder of aggregates here, because in it there is no remainder of contaminated aggregates. ^3-20

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-21]]

If one says, 'Why was it not spoken thus: "should be passed into nirvāṇa"?' It is in order to clear away the first dhyāna and so forth. Because they are without the afflictions of the lower levels, the Bhagavan spoke of them as enumerations of nirvāṇa. If one says, 'Why was "into the expanse of nirvāṇa with remainder of aggregates" not spoken?' It is because the remainder of the aggregates of suffering has not been abandoned. The intention of bodhisattvas to bring all sentient beings to complete nirvāṇa should be seen by means of three causes: waiting for another time for those born in unfree states, and the state of being unfree being merely what one prays for in order to indicate it, but it is not limited to that, because complete nirvāṇa is impossible for those abiding there; completely ripening those who are not completely ripened; and liberating those who are completely ripened; therefore, they are not unable to be sought. By this intention to bring sentient beings without exception to complete nirvāṇa, the extensiveness of the mind of awakening is taught. Here, how one should abide by means of both aspiration and prayer is also taught. Now, in order to teach how one should accomplish it by how one enters meditative equipoise in yoga, 'thus' and so forth were spoken. 'Thus, even though conventionally all sentient beings are passed into complete nirvāṇa, how does the bodhisattva ultimately not observe any sentient being? It is because they do not exist'—thus one should individually investigate it by entering meditative equipoise in yoga. ^3-21

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-22]]

That which is the generation of the mind upon arising, thinking, 'Ultimately, not any sentient being has been passed into complete nirvāṇa by me'—by this the profound is taught, and the ultimate mind of awakening is also taught. By the former, the conventional is taught. Now, in order to teach how the mind should be completely held here by cutting off distraction, 'Why is that?' was stated. 'If' is to be connected with the meaning of 'what if'. ^3-22

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-23]]

If one says, 'Well then, is whoever engages in the notion of a person and so forth called a bodhisattva or what?', thinking, 'Why did he make mention of only "sentient being"?', one asked, 'Moreover, why?', and the other also stated, 'they are not to be.' The intention is this: those of bad intellect, having completely imagined that inside, other than the aggregates, there exists a person who performs actions and the four enjoyments, designate conventions with terms like 'sentient being' and 'living being' and so forth; and for those who assert an inexpressible person, too, because they have accepted it as having dissimilar characteristics from the dharmas included in the aggregates, by implication they have accepted it as precisely a different entity; because it would logically follow that everything is inexpressible, an inexpressible thing is never possible. Because there is no valid cognition that establishes it, and because there are also things that invalidate it, a bodhisattva who views it thus, due to manifestly adhering to a distortion, becomes precisely distorted. How could one who has become distorted become an ultimate bodhisattva? Others say, 'Is it not that there is indeed a valid cognition that establishes the self? Since being bound, being liberated, the path, connection with the fruit, remembering, recognizing, and so forth are renowned as having a single basis, according to your system which asserts no-self, if there is not any single thing that would be established as their basis, what would be the basis? Because the individual moments of the moments of consciousness are different.' ^3-23

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-24]]

There is no postulating that Devadatta is bound and later Yajñadatta is liberated. Nor is there the experiencing by one of the fruit of karma done by another. Because it would become an extreme absurdity, and it would logically follow that what is done is wasted and one meets with what is not done; thus, because that which is the moment of consciousness imputed as the agent has no connection with the fruit, there is the fault of what is done being wasted; and because precisely that which is not the agent is connected with the fruit, there also becomes the fault of meeting with what is not done. Remembering and so forth are also not renowned in the world as having different bases. If one asks, 'Well then, how is it?', they have precisely a single basis: what is experienced by the mind of Devadatta is not remembered or recognized by the mind of Yajñadatta, but it is renowned that whatever experiences it, that itself remembers and recognizes. Therefore, there exists a single person who follows along all occasions. The syllogism is: 'Whatever are bound and liberated, those have a single basis, like for example Devadatta being bound by iron fetters and being liberated; and both being bound and being liberated, which have become the basis of debate, are also being bound and being liberated'—this is a reason of nature. Similarly, for establishing the agent as having precisely a single basis, one should also investigate the syllogism,' they say. ^3-24

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-25]]

Regarding that, if here one establishes a person and a sovereign one that is ultimate, permanent, and single, then at that time, because a connection together between such a person and the reason is not established anywhere, it is inconclusive. Because all formations are momentary, Devadatta and so forth are not established in the nature of being permanent and single, so the example also lacks what is to be established. For that very reason, the reason is contradictory, because it is connected with the opposite of that. However, if for some reason one establishes merely that they have a single basis, at that time it is establishing what is already established, for by that, too, it is indeed asserted that being bound and so forth have a single basis due to existing in a single continuum. Thus, at whatever time, in whatever continuum, those which have become cause and fruit, ignorance and so forth, arise continuously without the continuum being interrupted, at that time one designates it as 'being bound'. Moreover, when the noble path arises in that very [continuum] by degrees through hearing and so forth, and through the cessation of ignorance and so forth a transformation of basis occurs, it is designated as 'being liberated', but there is no establishing that those two possess an object that is an ultimate, permanent, single entity, because worldly being bound and being liberated are also similar to that. In whatever continuum a virtuous or non-virtuous mind and experience have arisen, in that very [continuum] at another time, in dependence upon the continuum having completely ripened—like the complete ripening of the continuum of a mātuluṅga and so forth that has been smeared with the juice of lac and so forth—because fruits such as happiness, suffering, and remembering appear to arise, virtue and so forth are renowned as having a single basis; but there is not any single agent, enjoyer, or rememberer, because all these imputed conventions are thoroughly distinguished merely by cause and fruit. ^3-25

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-26]]

Nor is it an extreme absurdity, because the capabilities of causes are individually determined, just as the capabilities of seeds and so forth are determined. Regarding that, whatever single one follows along by which the entities of cause and fruit would truly become individually determined does not exist at all, because they appear in different occasions. If, regarding this continuum of a sentient being also, it becomes similar to that, what is the use of this completely imputed self? Even if one imputes a self, one must undoubtedly accept the entities of cause and fruit, saying: 'Whatever is the occasion of being an agent and so forth is the cause; whatever is the occasion of being an enjoyer and so forth is the fruit.' If it were not so, and there were no arising in those two occasions, or no benefiting of one by the other, then because it would become like space, how could being an enjoyer and so forth also be established? One must accept that capabilities are individually determined: thus, because it is connected with an individually determined nature, happiness and so forth will arise, but not for all. Therefore, if precisely those which appear completely as causes and fruits, virtue and so forth, are the agent and the enjoyer, what is the use of completely imputing a self whose nature does not appear to be seen? If one says, 'Even if one grants that that self is not established by inference, nevertheless, because it is an object of previously known direct perception, the self is established by direct perception,' that too is not logical, because of being mistaken regarding that, because of being accompanied by conceptualization, and because the cognition is not established as direct perception itself. ^3-26

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-27]]

When it appears to the I-cognition, by what would it become direct perception? A self that is single, permanent, and all-pervasive, such as what is imputed from one's own tenet system, is not observed. Undoubtedly it should be known in that way. If it were not so, and if the I-cognition were to become direct perception, then at that time proponents would not dispute regarding the self that has become the object, because, being accompanied by conceptualization, grasping at 'I' would have become of a determinate nature. This is because there is no superimposition regarding what has been made an object by determination. Therefore, because one is deceived by the arising of one similar thing after another, and because, by the force of habituation since beginningless time, one superimposes a singleness upon the individually determined aggregates of form and so forth and thus completely engages the other, one cognizes thinking 'I', because in that cognition those very [aggregates], form and so forth, appear. If one asks, 'If this grasping at 'I' possesses the body as its object, how would it grasp at a mere difference, saying 'my body'?' that is also similar for that which possesses the self as its focal object: if this grasping at 'I' possesses a self as its object, then at that time how would one think 'my self'? If one says, 'Even though there is no difference in the entity itself, because one wishes to cognize merely that by eliminating other differences, just like the expression 'the body of a pestle', there is no contradiction because it grasps by imputing a difference,' that is also similar for that which possesses the body as its focal object. ^3-27

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-28]]

Possessing the self as its object also contradicts arising by degrees, and will be explained below. Nor is grasping at 'I' itself the self, nor is it possible to state that its objects, form and so forth, are the self, because those have dissimilar characteristics from it. Because those are observed exactly in a sequence, they do not exist as a single nature or as a sovereign one. For that very reason, it is explained: 'The divine self itself is not the characteristic of the self; because the attendants are dissimilar, it is not.' If one designates it as a name, since that is asserted, there is no dispute whatsoever regarding this. Therefore, there is not even the slightest valid cognition that establishes the self. That there is no valid cognition other than direct perception and inference has been analyzed elsewhere. The invalidating valid cognition is also observed only as an eliminator: thus, if the self were not other than the aggregates, then at that time it would become momentary like the aggregates, or else the aggregates would become permanent like the self, because of being undifferentiated from it. Its absurdly becoming a multiplicity like the aggregates would also become difficult to reverse. Therefore, it is asserted that the name 'self' applies only to the aggregates, for there is no dispute regarding a name. However, if one asserts the aggregates as different from the self, then at that time, because there is no connection with happiness and so forth, it would not be established as an enjoyer and so forth. ^3-28

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-29]]

The syllogism is: whatever is unconnected with happiness, suffering, and so forth is not an agent or an enjoyer, nor is it logical to be one who liberates or one who cycles, like the son of a barren woman, for instance. Saying, 'Because the self, too, lacks any connection whatsoever with actions and so forth' is the non-observation of a pervader. The reason is also not unestablished. Thus, if there were a connection with happiness and so forth, it would be [one of] three alternatives: the characteristic of support and supported, or the characteristic of possession, or the characteristic of cause and effect. First, it is not the first alternative, because it does not do anything at all. Thus, if something is posited as a support by obstructing the downward movement of phenomena that move downward, such as water, then because happiness and so forth are bodiless and thus it is impossible for them to move downward, by doing what would the self become a support? Nor is it a support by causing abiding, because, since the abiding and the abider are undifferentiated, it would produce that very thing, and that also should be refuted. Even if it were different from the abiding, it would definitely not benefit that abider, because it would produce an abiding that has become another entity. It also lacks the power to generate an abiding in it, because it will be clarified that it lacks power regarding everything. ^3-29

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-30]]

For a nature that is changeable by itself, there is nothing whatsoever that causes it to abide, because it [already] abides. If it were not so, it would only be destroyed by it, and it would not be made to abide. For an unchangeable nature, too, what is the use of something that causes it to abide, because it abides as a stable nature by itself? Therefore, because it does not do anything, a connection of possession is also unsuitable, because it would be extremely absurd. If it were so, everything would become the cause of everything's possession. It is illogical for another entity to possess another entity, because everything abides in itself, like an iron peg. Furthermore, if by the force of possession the self were to become happy or suffering, then at that time that possession would be single; because there is no difference, it would not become connected with an individually determined nature of happiness and so forth. Therefore, by happiness and so forth, the single self, too, would not entirely become happiness itself or suffering itself. If one asserts determination by the force of the unseen, if it is determined by that alone, what is the use of thoroughly imputing possession? Because it lacks power anywhere. Regarding imputing a difference because the connected [things] are different, if ultimately the nature of entities is not different, by what would there become a difference of causal functioning that depends on a difference of nature? Therefore, this connection is thoroughly conceptualized only by bad intellects. ^3-30

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-31]]

Nor is it a connection through cause and effect, because happiness and so forth are seen to arise by degrees. It is illogical for an effect whose causes are not incomplete to sometimes arise or not arise, because there is no difference. For the self, which cannot be differentiated by others or possesses independent power, there is no dependence on a cooperative cause, by which it would arise by degrees in dependence on a cooperative cause. Therefore, if it is bound by being the cause that generates suffering, then at that time it would be bound at all times and would not become liberated, because it would not thoroughly abandon such a kind of nature. If it thoroughly abandons it, how would it become permanent? However, if it is liberated by being the cause that generates a state without suffering, then at that time it would exactly become liberated; if it is never bound, then at that time how could one thoroughly impute a bondage and liberation that have a basis? Likewise, if it is an agent by performing the characteristic of intention and so forth or by being capable of generating them, then at that time it would become an agent at all times, so how would it become an enjoyer? But if it is an enjoyer by being the cause that generates happiness and so forth, then at that time it would exactly become an enjoyer, so how would it become an agent? That which is thoroughly conceptualized by the Sāṃkhyas, unchanging like space due to happiness and so forth, is also not endowed with the logic to be an enjoyer, because it would be extremely absurd. ^3-31

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-32]]

If it changes, permanence itself degenerates. Merely by being a cause it does not become an agent and an enjoyer, because it would be extremely absurd. Likewise, one should apply that a rememberer and so forth are also unsuitable to possess that as a basis. Therefore, it is only in the position that advocates selflessness that the presentation of liberation, bondage, and so forth is endowed with logic, because that presentation is thoroughly differentiated merely by the difference of cause and effect. That entity of cause and effect also is possible only for the impermanent, but not for the permanent, because they lack the power to generate any effect by degrees, just as explained before. Nor simultaneously, because the nature of the power to generate such a kind of effect follows again, and thus it is illogical to be separated from generating the effect just like before. If it does not follow, the prior nature would degenerate. Other than successive and simultaneous, there is no other aspect by which it would perform a function, because those two have the characteristic of abiding by mutually excluding each other. For that very reason, whatever is non-momentary, whatever entities such as space are thoroughly conceptualized by others, all of them are objects of the convention of non-existence because they lack the capacity to perform a function—so the great Buddhist scholars state. ^3-32

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-33]]

Imputing the convention of non-existence to a rabbit's horn and so forth is also due to the cause of merely lacking the capacity to perform a function. By implication, the object to which the convention of existence is imputed is exactly what is capable of performing a function, because existence and non-existence have the characteristic of abiding by mutually excluding each other. If one imputes the name 'entity' even to that which is empty of all capacities of such a kind and is no different from a rabbit's horn and so forth, there is no dispute about names. However, those endowed with understanding of the meaningless assertion state that it is illogical to impute the convention of entity to such a kind of unobservable nature, which is no different from the son of a barren woman and so forth. Therefore, the reason is not unestablished. Because it would absurdly follow that space and so forth are also agents, it is also not inconclusive. Because it exists in what is harmonious with the position, it is also not contradictory. Because there is also no other characteristic of the self, therefore it is similar to the son of a barren woman. As for persons, too, if they perish, it would absurdly follow that they are included exactly within the aggregates, because the aggregates include all conditioned things. But if they do not perish, then at that time, by being dissimilar to the dharmas belonging to the aggregates, they would become exactly another entity from them, and the faults of that have already been stated. Therefore, there is no entity whatsoever that is called inexpressible. Therefore, the lion's roar of the Bhagavan, 'all dharmas are selfless,' terrifies all the herds of tīrthika elephants and is invincible, so there is no need for much. ^3-33

##### Regarding the Application to the Perfections ^3-4-2-2-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-34]]

By way of application to the perfections, he spoke the words 'Moreover' and so forth. That too should be seen as the nature of two phrases having the nature of a general rule and an exception: this is the meaning of saying that a bodhisattva should indeed give a gift, but not by abiding in entities. By this term 'gift,' through the medium of the three types of giving, all six perfections are taught, not just giving alone. Herein, the giving of material things teaches the perfection of giving. The giving of fearlessness teaches the perfections of moral discipline and patience. The giving of Dharma teaches the perfections of effort, concentration, and wisdom: if there is no effort, one becomes thoroughly weary of retaining and teaching the Dharma, and thus will not teach the Dharma; if there is no concentration, because one desires what is more than faith, one will become an afflicted teacher; if there is no wisdom, one will teach it erroneously. Therefore, if all three are absent, the giving of Dharma will not be accomplished. Others explain that effort goes everywhere. By saying 'should give a gift,' it teaches how to abide in the six perfections through aspiration and prayer. ^3-34

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-35]]

By "not abiding in entities" and so forth, it is taught how this is to be accomplished by entering into yogic equipoise. By "a bodhisattva should thus give a gift" and so forth, it is taught how, having annihilated distraction, the mind should be thoroughly apprehended. Herein, by "not abiding in entities," the reality of the application to the perfections is taught. Adhering as reality to entities such as the gift to be given, the giver, and the receiver is "abiding"; therefore, if a bodhisattva applies to the perfections after adhering to entities, it will become a wrong application, because they erroneously adhere while ultimately there is no entity whatsoever to which adherence would occur. This is because there is no valid cognition that establishes that, and because there is that which subverts it. Thus, that others ultimately assert the entity of an object as different from consciousness is, for a start, not established by direct perception, because it is illogical for an object that is other to be apprehended by consciousness. If the apprehension of an object by consciousness were to become one of three positions—with an aspect, without an aspect, or with another's aspect—for a start, it is not "with an aspect," because when seeing a multicolored mat and so forth, since they are not different from the single consciousness, it would absurdly follow that the aspects too have the nature of being single; or, since the consciousness too is not different from the aspects, it would absurdly follow that it has the nature of being multiple, just like the nature of the aspects. ^3-35

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-36]]

It is also illogical for it to be exactly multiple, because a single consciousness is not established, since there is no experience of a consciousness apprehending just a single atom. If that is not established, multiplicity is also not established, because multiplicity has the nature of a collection of singles. Because consciousnesses are bodiless, it is also impossible for them to arise apprehending by remaining covering the object, by which the conceptualization that multiple consciousnesses arise for the sake of forming the structure of the aspect of the painting would become sound. Furthermore, would they be similar in their entire nature, or in one part? For a start, it is not in their entire nature, because, just like the object, the consciousness would become the nature of inert matter. It is also not by a part, because a single thing has no parts. Even if one thoroughly conceptualizes it as a part by way of particular exclusions, since things such as being an entity exist in all similar dharmas, it would absurdly follow that everything would be known by everything. But if one posits that it is known by the two—arising from it and being similar to it—then it would absurdly follow that it also apprehends the immediately preceding condition that corresponds in object. Even granting that there is similarity, ultimately an external object is not established by direct perception, because the consciousness knows the aspect of blue and so forth that exists in itself. There is no second knowing of the aspect of blue and so forth, by which the object too would become an object of direct perception. ^3-36

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-37]]

If one says, "That consciousness of whatever it may be is exactly what apprehends the object," it is so by way of imputation, but ultimately there is no knowing of it, because it knows exactly the aspect that exists in itself. Because the object is utterly hidden, how could there be such a thing as saying that it is exclusively the aspect of that consciousness? A cause is certainly not established as being a producer merely by engendering its own nature, because an illusion appears. Atoms are not the object of a consciousness in which a gross form appears, because they are subtle. They are also not a collection, because it is not an entity other than them. Also, because the object of conceptualization is nonexistent. If the collection is another entity, it is illogical for it to be single, because it appears in different directions, and because it possesses the incompatible properties of being obstructed and unobstructed. Even if the collection were multiple, it would be exactly those atoms themselves, but it is not another entity. It is also not the position called "without an aspect," because it does not do anything whatsoever to blue and so forth. If it knew merely by existing, then all consciousnesses would know everything, because there is also no difference in all of them. Therefore, it could not be posited that "this is the knowing of blue, but not of yellow," because there is no particular cause for a positing whatsoever. ^3-37

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-38]]

If one accepts a single difference, that itself would be accepted as being with an aspect, because for the nature of consciousness, which has no difference in merely being the nature of conceptualization, there is no other difference apart from the aspect. It is also not the position called "with another's aspect," because it would absurdly follow too far. If that were so, then a consciousness of an aspect would also be conceptualized as possessing the object of form. Therefore, the object is established by direct perception. It is also not by inference, because it is not established that that object, which has not become an object of direct perception, is connected together with any mark such as consciousness. If one infers merely a cause in general that is different from the effect of consciousness, the assertion is not established, because there is also another cause, the other immediately preceding condition. There is also no other valid cognition. Therefore, for a start, an external object is not established. The ultimate nature of consciousness is also not established by direct perception, because there is no individual knowing of a non-dual nature. If it were not so in that regard, then everyone would not see reality. It is also not by inference, because it is not established that any mark is connected together with such an aspect. If one asks, "How would there be thorough affliction and complete purification?" It is not so, because, according to you, it is not established that those two and any of that are connected. ^3-38

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-39]]

Because it is not established that those two are connected with the ultimate entity in any way. It is not established as connected by the characteristic of arising from that, because that is entirely hidden. A connection with what is hidden cannot be apprehended by anyone who sees this side. When ignorance and so forth, which are agreeable when not examined, are not contradictory as the root of thorough affliction, and when ignorance and so forth, which are agreeable when examined, are reversed, complete purification is also viable as solely agreeable when not examined, possessing the characteristic of arising as a state incompatible with that; at that time, since there is ultimately no entity, how could it absurdly follow that those two also do not exist? This absurd consequence arises for someone who accepts those two as definitively single and causeless, but it does not for one who speaks of dependent arising conventionally. Therefore, there is not even the slightest valid cognition establishing the nature of an ultimate entity. There is indeed that which subverts it: thus, whatever is devoid of a nature of one and multiple, that ultimately has no nature, just like a lotus in the sky; and the entire aspect of entities of the nature of consciousness and objects of knowledge, as asserted by others, is also empty of a nature of one and multiple. This by implication is the non-apprehension of the pervader, because one and multiple pervade the nature. ^3-39

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-40]]

Those two also do not exist in it. Thus, for a start, that external entity of the sense-spheres of form and so forth is not single, because one apprehends contradictory properties, such as being obstructed and unobstructed. If, while possessing contradictory properties, it were still exactly single, then everything would become a single substance, and therefore they would arise and be destroyed together. It is also not multiple, because thus, if it is broken down into multiplicity, it would be differentiated into atoms, and the atoms, too, having no parts, do not abide on a far side and a near side, just like mind and mental factors. Therefore, they would not appear to abide pervading a direction; if they were to do so, therefore one must accept them as having different directional parts. If one accepts that, how could it be single? Therefore, thus, for a start, an external object is established as exactly devoid of a nature of one and multiple. A consciousness in which blue and so forth appears is also not of a single nature, because it appears to apprehend the aspect of a painting, just like multiple moments of consciousness existing in different continuums. It is also unsuitable to express that consciousnesses arise simultaneously in accordance with an enumeration of multiplicity, because for things without parts and without a body, it is illogical to abide on a near side and a far side. ^3-40

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-41]]

If they do not abide like that, they would not appear like that. If one says, "However, consciousness is exclusively single, and because false aspects appear to it, it does not have the fault of multiplicity," if that were so, then how would those false things appear to it? That which is called "appearing" refers to the nature of clarity. If it is a property belonging to the nature of consciousness, how could that which is false appear if it is utterly nonexistent, just like the horns of a rabbit and so forth? Therefore, if one accepts the aspects of the clear nature as exactly false, what other nature of consciousness is left over that would be true for you? There is no consciousness whatsoever called "the nature of clarity," because with reference to the exclusion of others, general designations are applied only to particulars. If the particulars are false, it would become utterly clear that everything is also false. As for aspects that do not exist, they have no connection with that true nature of consciousness by which they would definitively appear to it, either by the characteristic of being its nature or by the characteristic of arising from it. If there is no connection, it is illogical for them to appear, because it would absurdly follow too far. Therefore, the reason is also not unestablished. ^3-41

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-42]]

Because there is no other aspect of an entity, it is also not inconclusive. Because it exists in the homologous class, it is also not contradictory. Therefore, in whatever way entities are conceptualized, in that way they unravel. For that very reason, it is explained: "However entities are conceptually examined, their nature does not exist truly. Thus they do not exist in the nature of one and multiple." The Bhagavat also stated in the noble Laṅkāvatāra: "Just as forms appear in a mirror, although utterly devoid of being one and other, yet they do not exist there; likewise, if the nature of entities is taken apart by the intellect, the nature is unapprehendable. Therefore, they are inexpressible, and are explained as also having no nature." Similarly, it is also stated in the noble Samādhirājasūtra: "For example, just as a wet trunk of a banana tree, when taken apart layer by layer by someone desiring its core, has no core inside or outside; know all dharmas to be like that." ^3-42

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-43]]

Therefore, since all entities are like an illusion, pleasant when unexamined, one should thus give gifts without being attached. The meaning that "it is not by abiding in entities" is logical here. That being so, the application to giving and so forth is fully taught as it truly exists, but not otherwise. Lest one think, "If, because there are no entities whatsoever ultimately, one should not give gifts by abiding in entities, one should surely give gifts with attachment to non-entities," it is stated: "without abiding in anything whatsoever." A bodhisattva should also abandon the conceptualization of non-entities, because if there is no apprehension of entities, that does not exist. Conventionally, what is to be given, the giver, and so forth exist; therefore, the intention is that attachment to non-entities is also illogical. ^3-43

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-44]]

By "without abiding in form" and so forth, it extensively demonstrates exactly what a bodhisattva should not abide in when giving gifts. By the word "dharmas," the sense-sphere of dharmas is encompassed. Since they are illustrated by elaborating upon this sense-sphere, therefore the internal sense-spheres such as the eye are not mentioned. In order to teach that one should also not conceptualize things as non-arising and so forth, "Subhūti, however..." and so forth is stated; "sign" refers to an entity's own defining characteristic, that is, a mark. That, too, refers to being unarisen and so forth. Apprehending that is perception. Not conceptualizing that is not abiding in it. ^3-44

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-45]]

By that, the statement "without abiding in anything whatsoever" is taught. Alternatively, by all of this, profundity is also taught, and the refutation of the extremes of superimposition and deprecation is also taught. For the view of those who have apprehension, the suitability of completely perfecting the accumulations of merit and pristine wisdom is refuted; thus, whatever is given out of a desire to make offerings and to benefit generates extreme faith, but not by abiding in the non-apprehension of what is to be given, the giver, and the recipient. Thus, are what is to be given and so forth unapprehended because they do not exist, or because there is no object? In the first position, since no one gives anything whatsoever, it would absurdly follow that there is no merit. Because sentient beings would not exist, the great pains taken by bodhisattvas for the sake of sentient beings would also become meaningless; therefore, they are not entirely nonexistent. Because the Bhagavat sees the entities of what is to be given and so forth, it is not the case that there is no object. ^3-45

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-46]]

Keeping all those objections in mind—such as, "If that were not so, how could the Bhagavat have given away his son and so forth to those who desired them?"—it was asked, "Why is that?" and in reply, "Subhūti, whatever bodhisattva..." and so forth was stated. The intention is this: giving and so forth that arise from correct view, because they are completely pure, accomplish an agreeable and particularly noble result, but not that arising from error. It is like the branches of ethical discipline and so forth arising from wrong view. Because the entities of what is to be given and so forth do not exist whatsoever in the manner just mentioned, apprehending them is solely an error, like apprehending a mirage as water. Therefore, all the modes of the perfections arising from that are of little power because they arise from impure causes, like giving and so forth arising from the error of self and what is mine. The non-apprehension of what have become objects of apprehension, such as what is to be given, is unmistaken because its object is genuine reality, like apprehending a mirage as water. Therefore, because it arises from extensive causes, giving and so forth from that have a great result, like a sprout and so forth manifesting from a viable seed. ^3-46

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-47]]

Therefore, giving and so forth that abide in non-apprehension, because they arise from correct view, generate extreme faith for the wise, but not otherwise. It is also not asserted that what is to be given and so forth are completely nonexistent—whereby, because of not engaging in giving and so forth, there would be no merit—because they are accepted to exist conventionally. In the state of not being in meditative equipoise, because they are apprehended as illusion-like, it is also not the case that there is no apprehension in all respects. At that time as well, it is posited as non-apprehension arising from resolve. In the state of meditative equipoise, there is solely complete non-apprehension; at that time, engaging in giving and so forth is not accepted, but because actions are performed in the state of initial application or by those who have arisen from equipoise, objections are inappropriate. Because of its great result, this states the extensiveness of the application to the perfections. Because the entities of cause and result are presented conventionally, the extreme of deprecation is also thereby refuted. In three ways, merit is equal to space: because of being all-pervasive, being extensive, and being inexhaustible. Because it generates mundane and supramundane merit, it is all-pervasive; mundane merit is generated by the state of not being in meditative equipoise. Supramundane merit is generated by the state of meditative equipoise. Because it becomes distinguished with superior qualities, it is extensive. Because it will not be completely depleted as long as saṃsāra lasts, it is inexhaustible. ^3-47

##### Regarding the Desire to Obtain the Form Body ^3-4-2-3-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-48]]

Fearing the objection, "If, because it does not exist, giving is to be given without abiding in form, then how should a bodhisattva who desires to obtain the form body of the Tathāgata engage in giving and so forth?", the passage beginning, "Subhūti, what do you think?" teaches the basis for aspiring to obtain the form body; here, by implication, it clears away the discordant class characterized by the pride of manifesting as a Tathāgata with respect to the form body. ^3-48

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-49]]

By completely stating merely the marks as "the excellence of the marks," the entirety of the Tathāgata's form body adorned with the marks and excellent signs is completely taught. In that regard, the marks are the thirty-two, such as having a wheel on the hands and feet; fearing wordiness they are not written here, but you should understand them just as they appear in the sūtra. Those are also excellent because they abide in their domain, are clear, and are complete. The eighty excellent signs are the nails being like the color of copper and so forth; you should know them as they appear in the sūtra. Those marks and so forth are also false, like the marks of a magically emanated Buddha. Therefore, it is taught that one should abide by aspiration and prayer for such a form body. Thus, if one wonders whether those marks are the very nature of accumulated subtle particles, or have become a whole form composed by them: first, the first position is not the case, because the subtle particles have already been dismantled earlier. The second is also not the case. If there is no composer, then there is no whole form composed by it, and because contradictory phenomena explained earlier, such as obscuration, are apprehended. Therefore, based on what was said, the intention was stated; examining that, Subhūti clarified, "It is not." Thinking, "If the marks are solely false, then the Bhagavat spoke of the marks in various sūtras," and having asked, "Why is that?", he said, "Because that itself is without the excellence of the marks"; the meaning of these words is that the excellence of the marks was not spoken of ultimately, but like a magically emanated Buddha, he spoke of them solely as a false nature. In order to fulfill that very meaning spoken by the noble Subhūti, the Bhagavat stated the passage beginning, "As many as..." ^3-49

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-50]]

The wording "As many as are the excellence of the marks" means that ultimately, as much adherence to the excellence of the marks as there is, that much is false adherence. It is just as it was taught earlier. ^3-50

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-51]]

"As many as are without the excellence of the marks" should be understood as being explained by reversing that. By this, how one who enters meditative equipoise in yoga should accomplish this is taught. This shows how, having abandoned the two extremes, the mind should be firmly held here: "Thus" means that the Tathāgata should be viewed by the marks like a magically emanated Buddha. This clears away the extreme of deprecation, because the Bhagavat's form body is not deprecated conventionally. "Without marks" is to be viewed ultimately, because no marks whatsoever are established. This clears away the extreme of superimposition. By offering prayers in order to obtain the Tathāgata's form body, extensiveness is stated. Because of not apprehending that, it is profound. The basis for aspiring to obtain the form body has been explained. ^3-51

##### Regarding the Desire to Obtain the Truth Body ^3-4-2-4-0

###### The Truth Body of Explanation ^3-4-2-4-1-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-52]]

The dharma body is of two types: the dharma body of teaching and the dharma body of realization. The dharma body of realization is also of two types: that which has gnosis as its cause and that which has merit as its cause. ^3-52

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-53]]

In that regard, fearing that future beings would not believe in the dharma body of teaching because the five degenerations would be predominant and because a jewel of dharma such as this has an extremely profound and extensive result, he asked, "Are there...?" The words "Are there..." should be connected with the meaning of the words "will there appear any sentient beings whatsoever who will produce a true perception with respect to the teaching of the dharma of a sūtra such as this?" As for "the last five hundred," a collection of five hundreds is five hundred; since it is renowned that the Bhagavat's teaching will remain for five periods of five hundred years, he specified "the last," because at that time the five degenerations will be predominant. ^3-53

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-54]]

"The thorough destruction of the true dharma" is the decline of devotion, reading, reciting, receiving transmissions, explaining, listening, thinking, and so forth. "Such as this" means the profound and extensive meaning. "The words of the sūtra" are the statements that teach the sūtra, because by these the meaning is realized and made known. This complete statement of merely a sign completely teaches the entirety of the dharma body of teaching. Alternatively, "the words of the sūtra" refers to what is to be expressed. As for "the teaching" here, it is connected to "such as this," meaning profound and extensive. "Upon the teachings" means "upon the expressions" according to the first. For the second, it means "upon those which teach the meaning." Conceiving the meaning unmistakably is here "true perception," but it does not apply to perceiving "this is true" by adhering to it literally. By this, if the meaning is established, it clears away untrue perception. Because his knowledge is unimpeded in all respects, the Bhagavat sees those who will appear as if directly; in order to clear away Subhūti's doubt, he said, "they will appear." "Great beings" was said in order to truly teach their bodhisattva qualities. ^3-54

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-55]]

What kind of accomplishment they have, by what kind of causes they truly accomplish it, what kind of being embraced by spiritual friends they possess, and by what kind of true perception they will perceive, the Bhagavat completely teaches: by this passage "possessing discipline, possessing qualities, possessing wisdom," it is taught that they possess the accomplishment of the three trainings, in order, as higher discipline, higher mind, and higher wisdom. The reason discipline and wisdom are mentioned separately is that "qualities" here takes up those arising from concentration, in the manner of saying "cows and bulls". If one wonders how such possessors of qualities like discipline will appear in that afflicted later time, the passage beginning "Subhūti..." was spoken. "Having venerated" is to have venerated with robes, food, and so forth, and to have made offerings with parasols, victory banners, pennants, and so forth. ^3-55

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-56]]

"Planting roots of virtue" should be viewed in terms of earnestly practicing the excellence of qualities such as discipline, having received instructions and teachings on the crown of the head. Furthermore, in order to state that they accumulate virtue in much greater abundance than that, "and a hundred thousand Buddhas" is mentioned; by this, the true accomplishment of their causes is taught. ^3-56

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-57]]

"Those, the Tathāgata knows" refers to their intentions and names. ^3-57

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-58]]

"Those, the Tathāgata sees" refers to the body. By this, being embraced by spiritual friends is taught. That, too, was said in order to encourage those who have entered correctly and to generate shame in those who have entered wrongly. Were it otherwise, since the Bhagavat is omniscient, what difference would there be from teaching "he knows all"? Alternatively, this should be seen as principally teaching that they possess good fortune, meaning "he knows they possess good fortune." ^3-58

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-59]]

Therefore, in order to teach the complete accumulation of their heap of merit, he said, "all those sentient beings..." "Will produce" refers to the time when merit arises. "Will completely accumulate" refers to completely apprehending its latencies at the time of cessation. By all this, having cleared away untrue perception, he taught that one should abide by both the aspiration and the prayer to produce true perception, and he completely taught the extensiveness of this point. ^3-59

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-60]]

"Why is that?" completely requests the reason making it known how they will have venerated many hundreds of thousands of Buddhas, and here, he said, "those bodhisattva great beings..." and so forth. This completely teaches how one should accomplish meditative absorption in yoga. It also expresses profundity. Apprehending that a self exists by thinking "I am" is the perception of a self. ^3-60

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-61]]

Apprehending that "mine" exists is the perception of a sentient being. Completely apprehending that that very self remains as long as its lifespan is the perception of a living being. Apprehending it as going again and again into migrations is the perception of a person. ^3-61

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-62]]

If one wonders whether, in the absence of the perception of a self and so forth, there is the perception of dharmas such as form as there is for the śrāvakas and so forth, he said, "neither is it the perception of a dharma," intending that it is because ultimately no dharma whatsoever exists. If one wonders, in that case, is there a perception of non-existence like that of the nihilists, he said, "neither does a perception of no dharma engage." Because, conventionally, dharmas exist as pleasant when unanalyzed, for those abiding on the conventional path for the time being, the perception of no dharma is not endowed with reasoning. Neither is it for those abiding in ultimate gnosis, because at that time, no dharma whatsoever is observed which, by being negated, would cause the perception of no dharma to engage. That is because it does not exist if there is no apprehending as a dharma. It is impossible anywhere for there to be a negation without an object. Regarding that, for example, even though "mere representation" is true, if one conceptualizes "this is mere representation," one is abiding in mere representation itself; similarly, even though the non-existence of dharmas is true, if one conceptualizes the non-existence of dharmas, it is impossible to abide in that, so they are entirely equivalent. If one wonders whether, if a self and so forth do not exist, they do not engage in the perception of those, but they will have a perception adhering to names, thinking "this self is also only merely a name like form and so forth," he said "and perception," because names belong to the form aggregate, so like form and so forth they are entirely false, and even what is unassociated exists imputedly. Therefore, he intended that perceiving names is also just adhering mistakenly. ^3-62

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-63]]

If one wonders whether, if that were so, since in that state one is free from all perception, the yogi would become completely non-existent like a dead person or a person who has entered the meditative absorption of cessation, he said, "neither will no perception engage." At that time, there exists the very nature of correct conventional yogic gnosis, by which they become yogis. When rising from that, pure mundane gnosis arises from it. Were it otherwise, it would become entirely causeless. If one asks, "Who understands that very conventional nature of yogic gnosis?", [it is understood] by that very same reasoning: just as the realization of the selflessness of a person realizes the selflessness of the person present in oneself through self-awareness, similarly, it is entirely equivalent here as well. It is not that gnosis and awareness are not accepted even conventionally. The yogi does not observe the selflessness present in phenomena by having individually actualized their specific characteristics, because at that time, past things and so forth are not proximate and are infinite. If one wonders what it is like, then: through analysis that they have no inherent nature, all dharmas that become objects are generally subsumed as selfless, and whatever is thus posited is observed simultaneously. Although their gnosis has no object ultimately, it is posited as a valid cognition because it is non-deceptive, like the consciousness of a true dream. Since its own intrinsic nature is also subsumed within that generality, there is no observer of it separately in that. Therefore, from the realization of both as selfless, it is evident that they have venerated many hundreds of thousands of Buddhas. Those who have not venerated Buddhas, like the non-Buddhists, cannot possibly realize it. He intended, thinking, "This is not something one is familiar with in saṃsāra." By this, he completely taught how one should accomplish this through meditative absorption in yoga, and by what sort of true perception it becomes their perception, and he also completely taught profundity. ^3-63

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-64]]

Even if they do not have the perception of a self and so forth because a self and so forth do not exist, does not the perception of dharmas and non-dharmas, characterized as well-spoken and poorly explained, whose nature is collections of words and so forth, definitely arise for them? It is not so. Regarding the doubt: if bodhisattvas do not perceive them, having abandoned the dharmas of non-Buddhists generated by incorrect reasoning, how will they generate the aspiration to master the dharmas spoken by the Bhagavan? he requested, "Why is that?" and in reply, he said, "Subhūti, if..." The intention is this: just as dharmas characterized as form and so forth do not exist when individually analyzed as having a singular or plural nature, the explained dharmas are also like that. Whoever has the perception of a dharma has also definitely not abandoned the perception of a self and so forth, because its root is apprehending a dharma as a sign. Because mental engagement endowed with apprehending a dharma as a sign is mental engagement of just that, as love and so forth are not mental engagement of reality, it does not abandon the view of a self. Because of that very fact, there is only one vehicle; thus he said, "that is, the Great Vehicle." Furthermore, if a bodhisattva who has the perception of a dharma has definitely not generated the path of seeing, how will one who is like that abandon the perception of a self and so forth? Because that is to be abandoned by the path of seeing. Therefore, having analyzed all that, he said, "that very thing would become their apprehending of a self." He intended, thinking it is because that is the cause of it, and because its latent tendencies have not been abandoned. Were it otherwise, since those very things are of different types, how would the very perception of a dharma become the apprehending of a self? Dharmas here are asserted as characterized by scriptures generated by reasoning. The opposite of that are non-dharmas. If one wonders, if that is so, how is it that the Bhagavan taught bodhisattvas in various sūtras to completely apprehend the true dharma, having analyzed that, he requested "Why is that?" and in reply, he said "Subhūti..." and so forth. ^3-64

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-65]]

The statement "should not be wrongly apprehended" means that one should not apprehend it with the characteristic of perverse attachment through an erroneous path; the meaning of the words is that one should not become attached thinking it to be true. He intended, thinking, "It does not refute apprehending the characteristics taught as being like a raft for the purpose of accomplishing the intention." With "Therefore," and so forth, he connects that very meaning with other words. Because attachment to all dharmas and non-dharmas, thinking them to be true, is wrong attachment, if even those raft-like collections of scriptures generated by reasoning, which are spoken for the sake of crossing over the ocean of saṃsāra, are to be abandoned because they are not needed after reaching the other shore, what need is there to mention those non-dharmas generated by incorrect reasoning, like the non-Buddhists? The Tathāgata spoke thus, saying, "I have not permitted relying on them even out of necessity." It is not so. If dharmas and non-dharmas and so forth were true in nature, then at that time, because attachment to them has an object ultimately, it would become solely of a reasonable nature even for those who have crossed over to the other shore of saṃsāra, so why would the Tathāgata refute that? This is the summarized meaning. ^3-65

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-66]]

Thus, from the Noble Ratnakaraṇḍaka-sūtra as well, it is explained: 'The Bhagavan said, "Venerable Subhūti, if those who know dharma enumerations to be like a raft should abandon even dharmas themselves, what need is there to mention non-dharmas? Whatever dharma is abandoned is neither a dharma nor a non-dharma."' Regarding whatever dharma enumerations, such as sūtras, have the likeness of a raft, it is spoken in that way. The phrase 'even dharmas' is an illustration; therefore, dharmas characterized as form and so forth and the path are apprehended by it. The phrase 'non-dharmas' is also solely an illustration; by it, those entities of non-dharmas such as a self, which are completely imagined by others and taught in contradiction to what is renowned, are also apprehended. Alternatively, because it states dharmas and non-dharmas along with what is to be expressed, there is no fault in subsuming everything. By this, having eliminated distraction, it teaches how the mind should be completely apprehended here. ^3-66

###### The Truth Body of Realization ^3-4-2-4-2-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-67]]

If all dharmas and so forth were ultimately false in nature, then at that time the statement "Having become manifestly and completely awakened, the Bhagavan taught all dharmas" would also become mere falsehood. Therefore, having analyzed the thought that a bodhisattva should not aspire in order to obtain the realized dharma body whose cause is the wisdom of the Tathāgata, in order to generate the aspiration to obtain the dharma body whose cause is wisdom, he said, "Subhūti, what do you think?" By this, in terms of meaning, he completely teaches that ultimately, apprehending unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening as manifest and complete awakening is also a discordant class, while in the nature of the genuine relative, he also teaches aspiring and making prayers to obtain the dharma body of the Tathāgata's wisdom, whose cause is wisdom. ^3-67

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-68]]

By completely expressing the mere sign "unsurpassed, perfect and complete awakening", he also completely teaches the realized body whose cause is wisdom, which is differentiated by the particularities of realization of śrāvakas, pratyekabuddhas, and so forth; the qualities of śrāvakas and so forth are also precisely what is to be realized by a bodhisattva. Were it otherwise, how would the state of omniscience be obtained? In the nature of the relative, it is like this: complete awakening, what is to be completely awakened to, what is to be taught, and the teaching, and so forth—all exist as enumerations. ^3-68

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-69]]

Because the Bhagavan spoke intending the ultimate with a certain intention, saying, "Everything is solely false," therefore, having grasped the intention from what was asked, the elder, noble Subhūti stated: "As I understand the meaning of what the Bhagavan has spoken..." The statement "As I understand" is the explanation of the intention. ^3-69

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-70]]

Regarding the thought, "Why is there neither manifest and complete awakening, nor the teaching of dharma?" he stated, "Why is that?" "Cannot be apprehended" means that ultimately it is not even the domain of non-conceptual wisdom, just as explained before. "Cannot be expressed" means that it is also not the object of words, because the domain of all words is merely superimposed universals, and because universals are also non-entities. These two were spoken as the reasons for there being no manifest and complete awakening and no teaching of dharma, respectively. ^3-70

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-71]]

Regarding the thought, "Even if all entities cannot be apprehended and cannot be expressed, nevertheless, because they uphold their own characteristics, the entity of dharmas ultimately solely abides," he stated, "that is also not a dharma." Because it is impossible for all entities to uphold their own characteristics, being naturally passed beyond sorrow ultimately. Regarding the thought, "If that is so, then did the Bhagavan manifestly and completely awaken to non-existence?" he stated, "nor is it a non-dharma." That has the nature of refuting non-existence. By this, he teaches entering meditative equipoise in yoga, and he also refutes the extremes of superimposition and deprecation. With "Why is that?", he completely requests the justification here, and in reply here, he stated, "because it is distinguished by the unconditioned." The unconditioned is the ultimate nature of dharmas. Furthermore, when analyzed by reasoning, that abides solely in the characteristic of non-arising, just as analyzed before. As explained by the Bhagavan in the Buddhasaṃgīti: "Ānanda, whatever is the non-arising, non-disintegration, non-abiding, and non-alteration of dharmas is called a noble truth." ^3-71

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-72]]

As is stated: "Ānanda, intending this, the Tathāgata teaches that noble śrāvakas are distinguished by the unconditioned." This is unconditioned because, whether Tathāgatas appear or do not appear, it permanently exists in that way and has no alteration. Because of realizing it, noble persons are distinguished by it, because the nobles are distinguished by realizing the suchness of dharmas. Because it is illogical for another to be the nature of suchness. That unconditioned is not non-existent in the nature of a non-affirming negation, because it is the entity of the nature of entities. Were it otherwise, the nobles would not realize the nature of entities, because they would realize another entity that is not it. Because it would be absent if the apprehension of an entity were absent, it would absurdly follow that it is solely the apprehension of an entity. Therefore, in sūtras such as the noble Saṃdhinirmocana, it is taught, "Suchness is not a different object from entities," for the faults stated would absurdly follow. It does not ultimately exist as the entity of entities, because no entity of entities whatsoever is established. Like the entity of entities, it would absurdly follow that it is conditioned. Therefore, because it exists in dharmas rather than being non-existent, it would become a nature that is not suchness. It would absurdly follow that everyone would realize it like the entity of the nature of entities. It would also be difficult to reverse the absurd consequence that it would become the very cause of thorough affliction. For that very reason, in those very sūtras such as the noble Saṃdhinirmocana, it is stated, "Suchness is not a different object from entities." Because of that, he stated, "Ultimately it is not a dharma, nor is it a non-dharma." By this, he teaches the nature that refutes entities and non-entities, existence and non-existence, and so forth. By this, he also teaches cutting off distraction. By all this, he also teaches the very profundity of this abiding. Having analyzed the thought, "If there is no dharma whatsoever that the Bhagavan manifestly and completely awakened to or taught as dharma, how should a bodhisattva who desires to obtain the dharma body whose cause is merit engage in completely grasping the true dharma?", it is in order to generate the aspiration to obtain the dharma body whose cause is merit. ^3-72

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-73]]

He said, "Subhūti, what do you think?" Here, the seven precious substances are gold, silver, crystal, lapis lazuli, emerald, ruby, and red pearl. They are the ones appearing in the noble Saddharmapuṇḍarīka. A thousand of the four continents and so forth is a thousand-fold world system. A thousand of those is a second-order thousand. ^3-73

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-74]]

A thousand of those is a third-order great thousand-fold world system. "From that basis" means that this merit arises from that basis of giving precious substances; the meaning is that it arises from that cause. Merit is all virtuous intentions and the latent tendencies generated by them; its accumulation is the collection. Because its fruit is much, it is also much. "Much" is the particularity. By stating "much" twice, it describes it as extremely much. ^3-74

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-75]]

Regarding the thought, "If ultimately everything is unarisen, how does the accumulation of merit arise from that basis?", having stated "Why is that?", knowing his own intention, he stated in reply, "because it is not an accumulation." "Ultimately" is to be supplied. Because accumulations exist imputedly, the accumulation of merit does not exist as an entity. Merit is also imputed upon particular instances of the nature of latent tendencies. A bodhisattva also solely exists imputedly, because entities, being unmoving, do not engage in the manner of conditioning. Even when analyzed individually as the nature of one or many, they solely have a false nature. Because of that, ultimately there is no accumulation of merit. By this, he completely teaches entering meditative equipoise in yoga. He also refutes the extreme of superimposition. ^3-75

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-76]]

Conventionally, the accumulation of merit does abide. Because of that, the Tathāgata stated, "it is called the accumulation of merit." Were it otherwise, if that accumulation of merit existed ultimately, it would not be expressed, because the ultimate is not the object of words. Even if he did not state it, since it would be known as such, stating it would become entirely meaningless; therefore, because he stated it, it is known to be solely conventional. By this, he teaches the refutation of the extreme of deprecation. In that way, after noble Subhūti refuted the two extremes, subsequently, because the retinue became worthy of listening to the extensive dharma and because he also stated that the merit was much greater, the Bhagavan spoke the words "Subhūti, by whoever," and so forth. ^3-76

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-77]]

By this, because the dharma body whose cause is merit, realized by the Tathāgatas, definitely arises from the dharma body that is taught, it teaches in this way: "If, just for a while, teaching merely a four-line verse from that becomes an origin of merit like that, what need is there to mention the dharma body whose cause is merit, realized by the Tathāgatas?" Although this is not the literal meaning of the words, because it is realized by implication, there is no fault in this meaning. Therefore, it is the intention of thinking, "Although ultimately there is no dharma taught or manifestly and completely awakened to, because conventionally the teaching of the body of merit exists, a bodhisattva should engage in order to obtain merit." By this teaching that the merit is distinguished, he teaches its extensiveness. He also teaches the aspiration to generate it and the making of prayers. Because there are also verses with six lines, he makes the particularization "a four-line verse." This "verse" is not composed, but from prose even merely the words of a four-line verse are grasped. "Having grasped" means having recited it. "Explains" is the words. "Properly teaches" is the meaning. "Incalculable" is because it is extremely much. "Immeasurable" is because it is not an object of comparison. By this, in meaning, he completely dispels the discordant side of not generating merit. By expressing merely the name "merit", he teaches the entire collection of merit of the Tathāgata. ^3-77

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-78]]

Regarding the thought, "Is this merely something to be believed, or is it also something to be known through reasoning?", having asked "Why is that?", here he spoke the reasoning: "Subhūti, the unexcelled completely perfect awakening of the Tathāgatas, the arhats, the completely perfect Buddhas, has arisen from this." Thinking this, the expression "greater merit" also means it is great merit because its fruit is great, but because it has no body, it is not so by its own nature. That merit arisen from precious substances attains ordinary fruits of cyclic existence, such as great resources; if embraced by wisdom, giving and so forth attain the fruit of great awakening, but not merely by themselves alone. Since by the gift of dharma one attains the state of a Tathāgata, which is without comparison and inexhaustible, why would it not manifest as being great? Just as one is benefited by dharma, precious substances will not greatly benefit others in the same way; for in this way, through the gradual process of teaching it properly to others, from this dharma alone one will quickly attain the state of Buddhahood itself, but not from precious substances. Because of that, he intended, "Since it becomes the supreme benefit for others, the giving of dharma is exceptionally superior to the giving of precious substances." "Arises from this" means properly established; relying on the ten practices, one subsequently attains it by being passed down from one to another—that is the meaning. Those ten practices are writing, offering, giving, reading, listening, grasping, transmitting, reciting, contemplating, and meditating. ^3-78

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-79]]

"The Buddha Bhagavans are also born from this" means that conventionally Buddhas are imputed in dependence on that; this is the meaning. Ultimately, there is no difference between awakening and Buddhas. By this teaching that the merit is exceptionally superior, he teaches the aspiration to generate it and abiding by prayer. ^3-79

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-80]]

Regarding the thought, "If ultimately all dharmas are solely unarisen, then at that time all qualities of a Buddha would also be solely unarisen; how then does the unexcelled completely perfect awakening arise from this? Buddhas are not different from awakening," having asked "Why is that?", here he stated in reply, "qualities of a Buddha." The dharmas that have the characteristics of unexcelled completely perfect awakening and so forth, which are the cause of imputing a Buddha, are the qualities of a Buddha. Therefore, the intention is: "Although Buddhas ultimately are not different from awakening, because it becomes the cause of imputing them, it is stated that Buddhas are born from this." Because they too are ultimately unarisen, even though qualities of a Buddha do not exist, the Tathāgata stated "the unexcelled completely perfect awakening of the Tathāgatas has arisen from this" and so forth in reliance on the conventional; but not ultimately. ^3-80

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-81]]

For that very reason, he intended: "From various other standpoints, 'qualities of a Buddha' cannot be expressed ultimately; because they are to be individually and personally realized, they are inexpressible." By this he teaches entering into yogic meditative equipoise and subduing distraction, and he also teaches the profound. Here, some say: "Since the dharma body has no differentiation, how can there be these three different types of bodies? But if you say that because one will attain the dharma body, and because they are effects corresponding to the cause of the dharma body, the nature of merit and the nature of the teaching are called the 'dharma body' through imputation, then in that case the form body too would become the dharma body, because it is an effect corresponding to the cause of attaining it and because it is the cause of attaining it." That is not true. The dharma body of the Buddha Bhagavans is posited as the nature of completely pure exalted wisdom and whatever is its general dharmatā. This is neither a differentiation of its maturation, nor is it taught as "dharma" here by imputing upon the teaching and merit. What is it like then? It is solely the entity; because it is renowned as such by itself, as in "teaches the dharma", "accumulates the dharma" and so forth. ^3-81

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-82]]

Whatever is taught as "dharmas" because they hold their own and general characteristics, that is common to all entities, so the form body is not taught as the Tathāgata, because it is not that which makes known. Even though it is taught by imputation, the form body does not absurdly follow to be the dharma body, because it is an effect corresponding to the cause of merit. As it is said: "In short, the dharma body of the Victors arises from the collection of exalted wisdom, while the form body of the Buddhas arises from the collection of merit." Therefore, because only the collection of exalted wisdom is the cause of attaining it, it is impossible that it will be attained by the form body; because there is no cause for imputing it, the form body is not called the dharma body. The body of the teaching is the fruit of perfectly pure exalted wisdom, and the body of merit, having the characteristic of a collection, is also the cause of attaining it; thus it is indeed reasonable to teach it as the "collection of dharma" by imputation, so there is no need for a lot of words. Thinking that other bad objections might be distinguished as reasonable or unreasonable by those with wisdom, I have not written them, fearing verbosity. The topic of the aspiration to attain the dharma body has been explained. ^3-82

##### Showing the Twelve Bases in Order as Antidotes to the Twelve Discordant Factors ^3-4-2-5-0

###### Showing Manifest Pride ^3-4-2-5-1-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-83]]

From here onwards, the twelve topics should be known as the remedies for the twelve types of discordant factors, respectively. Those twelve types of discordant factors are: overweening pride; even if without overweening pride, having little learning; even if having much learning, cultivating mental engagement on a small object; even if cultivating mental engagement on an object that is not small, forsaking sentient beings; even if not forsaking them, engaging with attachment to outer treatises; even if not engaging in them, lacking skill in means to overcome the signs of entities; even if skilled in means for that, not having accumulated the collection of merit; even if having accumulated the collection of merit, experiencing indolence and partaking of alms through gain and respect; even if not experiencing that, being unable to bear suffering; even if unconcerned about suffering, not having accumulated the collection of exalted wisdom; even if having accumulated the collection of exalted wisdom, thoroughly grasping at a self; and even if lacking thorough grasping at a self, not possessing the instructions. Regarding that, although the Bhagavan has extensively taught the selflessness of persons and dharmas, by the force of having firmly accumulated manifest adherence to a self since beginningless time, thinking, "If whoever engages in the perception of a sentient being and so forth is not called a bodhisattva, if that is so, then how is it that the Bhagavan said: 'At that time, in that period, I became the king named Śūradatta'?" ^3-83

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-84]]

It would become "I shall teach the dharma to you" and so forth. Thinking, "One who does not have the perception of a sentient being and so forth would not intend in that way", in order to be free from that which becomes an obstruction of overweening pride, making it regarding the fifth topic, he said, "Subhūti, what do you think?" and so forth. Regarding this, the outer meaning is this: "If even my lowest śrāvakas, the stream-enterers and so forth, who have arrived but not attained nirvāṇa in order to realize the selflessness of dharmas, have no manifest adherence to 'I' and 'mine', what need is there to mention one who has realized both selflessnesses and abandoned obscurations without exception?" Whatever is "At that time I" and so forth, that is merely eliminating [other possibilities] regarding the continuums of others for those who desire to know for the purpose of designating worldly conventions; it is equivalent to conventions such as 'army' and 'forest', but is not due to manifest adherence to it being singular and permanent. Those abiding in the exalted wisdom of suchness cannot express conventions together with the world. Therefore, in order to be free from what becomes a basis for overweening pride, he caused them to grasp it perfectly, saying, "You too should not have manifest adherence to a self." Regarding that, the 'stream' has the characteristic of the path of seeing that goes to the great city of nirvāṇa. 'Entered' means attained; and one who has abandoned all the afflictions to be abandoned by seeing is called a 'stream-enterer'. Furthermore, because by having abandoned manifest adherence to the agent, the action, and the activity, one realizes this mere conditionality itself, this teaches realizing selflessness. 'My' refers to manifest adherence to the agent. 'Fruit' refers to manifest adherence to the action. 'Attained' refers to manifest adherence to the activity. By this, in meaning, thinking "I have attained" is thoroughly taught to be a discordant factor. ^3-84

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-85]]

By thoroughly expressing just a mere sign, saying, 'The stream-enterer does not think thus: "I have attained"', it thoroughly teaches abandoning all overweening pride. Regarding that, it also teaches aspiration and making prayers. ^3-85

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-86]]

The Noble Subhūti, too, by closely expressing the intention of the Bhagavan, in order to establish it ultimately and to lead into meditative absorption in yoga regarding that, said, "Bhagavan, he has not entered into anything." To the thought, "Why is that?", he said, "He has not entered into form," and so forth. By this, it teaches that ultimately both types of fruits are not to be attained. Thus, if the entity to be attained were to become a fruit, would it become conditioned or unconditioned? Regarding that, first, the unconditioned is just unarisen; because it is non-existent, being similar to a rabbit's horn, it is not suitable to be attained, because there is no difference from the state of not being attained. The conditioned is momentary, being destroyed immediately upon arising; because it does not abide, it will not be attained by anyone in any way. In whatever continuum, due to the active cause characterized by the selfless path having become present, a fruit characterized as a state completely discordant with the arising of afflictions has arisen, regarding that it is merely taught that "he attains the fruit," but ultimately it is not so. Furthermore, would the entities of that which is to be attained and that which attains be of different times or of the same time? First, the first position is not the case; because both must abide, if they are of different times, at the time when one is present the other is non-existent, so how could both of them abide? Neither is it from being of the same time; because, once both are established, there is no benefiting of one another. ^3-86

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-87]]

If there is no benefiting, it is not logical to ascertain the entities of what is to be attained and what attains it, because it would entail an absurd consequence. It is also not logical for the characteristics of what is to be attained and what attains it to be simultaneously related through the characteristic of generating mutual association. Thus, first, those entities which are bodiless do not abide in a locus, so they have no association. They do not become the entities of what is to be attained and what attains it merely by being at the same time, because it would entail an absurd consequence. It is also not merely from a single cause, because since that is common to both, there would be no certainty that 'this is what is to be attained, and this is what attains it.' For those that possess bodies, if they are associated with each other by their entire nature, it would entail the absurd consequence of them being one. If by one part, it would entail the absurd consequence of them possessing limbs. Neither is attainment from abiding without interval, because being common to both, without difference, there is no certainty. Therefore, this is merely superimposed by conceptualization. By this, all conventions such as agent and action should be clearly understood. 'Dharmas' refers to the dharmāyatana. By this, the unconditioned is also included, because the dharmāyatana has the nature of seven substances. The remainder, form and so forth, and a portion of the dharmāyatana, teach the conditioned. This is an illustration; therefore, the eye and so forth should also be grasped. ^3-87

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-88]]

Because ultimately he has not entered into anything, and he does not think 'I have attained', therefore, since he abides on the perfect path, he is called a 'stream-enterer'. ^3-88

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-89]]

To the thought, "If one becomes thus attached, thinking 'I have attained', why at that time is he not called a stream-enterer?", he said, "Bhagavan, if," and so forth. If someone becomes attached, thinking "I" as in "by me", they have not abandoned the view of self. He thought, "For whoever has not abandoned the view of self, the noble path has not arisen. Whoever has not had the noble path arise is an ordinary being, so how could they become a stream-enterer?" Thus, whoever becomes attached saying "by me", the view of self completely arises for them. By this, the thorough holding of the mind is taught. By all this, the profoundness of this point is expressed. ^3-89

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-90]]

In order to teach this very meaning by teaching it through the elaboration of an example, he said, "Once-returner," and so forth. Here too, the meaning of the words and the connections and so forth should all be applied as before. ^3-90

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-91]]

One who has abandoned the six aspects of afflictions operating in the desire realm that are to be abandoned by meditation is exactly the noble 'once-returner', because they will return once to the desire realm. One who has abandoned all afflictions operating in the desire realm that are to be abandoned by meditation is exactly called a 'non-returner', because they will not return again to the desire realm. At the time when one has abandoned all stains of the afflictions operating in the three realms, at that time, being worthy of being venerated by the entire world, they are called an 'arhat'. ^3-91

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-92]]

"There is not any dharma called an 'arhat'" means that because it is designated upon the continuum of pristine wisdom that has abandoned such aspects of afflictive obscuration, and because the continuum also exists imputedly, ultimately it does not exist at all; he thought, "It is because it is a superimposition." Now, the noble Subhūti, being a witness himself, in order to make his words worthy of being grasped, expresses himself by way of abiding without afflictions, which is an extraordinary quality, and being an arhat, which is a common quality, and in order to teach exactly the thorough holding of the mind, he said, "I", and so forth. "Without afflictions" is a particular meditative absorption. Furthermore, only arhats who are liberated in both ways enter into meditative absorption in yoga, thinking, "It is not suitable for others to have afflictions arise by focusing on me," and for those who have risen from that, others will not have afflictions arise by focusing on them; those who have the character of abiding by that absence of afflictions are spoken of in this way. This detailed explanation is pleasing. "He would not have prophesied of me" means he thought, "Because the view of self would not have been abandoned at that time, even the fruit of a stream-enterer would be difficult for me to find; so what need is there to even mention abiding without afflictions, which is to be attained by an arhat liberated in both ways?" To the thought, "How should one abide without afflictions?", he said, "He does not abide in anything." "Whoever does not abide by becoming attached in that way, thinking, 'I have attained some conditioned or unconditioned fruit', that very one is said in the tenets to abide without afflictions, and it is not anyone else"—this is the meaning of the words. ^3-92

###### Showing Little Learning Despite the Absence of Manifest Pride ^3-4-2-5-2-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-93]]

"Now, I have been prophesied by the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly completely awakened Buddha, as the foremost of those who abide without afflictions." ^3-93

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-94]]

"Bhagavan, although I am an arhat free from attachment" means it is not due to being attached to a self. Well then, how is it? It is due to being attached to mere convention, and I have already explained that. ^3-94

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-95]]

In order to free those who came to understand thus, thinking, "If ultimately there is nothing to be attained and no attainer, how did the Bhagavan, when he became a bodhisattva, obtain the dharma from the Tathāgata Dīpaṃkara? But if he did not obtain it, then why should those of us who desire to obtain the dharma please the Tathāgatas when they appear?", from the hindrance of having little hearing, he spoke by way of the sixth point: "Do you think that there is any dharma that the Tathāgata obtained from the Tathāgata, the arhat, the perfectly completely awakened Buddha Dīpaṃkara?" Subhūti also, having grasped the previous intention, said, "There is not." That there is no dharma whatsoever obtained as an entity of something to be attained and an attainer ultimately, is just as explained before. It is also because all dharmas are peaceful by nature. Although that is so, it is designated as "obtained" merely with respect to the arising of knowledge in which various collections of words and so forth appear by the power of the Tathāgata. Therefore, thoroughly seeking hearing is fruitful. By this, implicitly, it thoroughly shows that being attached, thinking, "When a bodhisattva thoroughly seeks hearing, they should please Buddhas who have appeared, and after that I will obtain the dharmas of a Buddha," is the discordant class. It also teaches abiding with aspiration and prayer to abandon that. By stating just a portion, "Did he obtain any dharma from Dīpaṃkara?", it turns away all signs of obtaining dharma. By this saying, "There is not any dharma that was obtained," it posits the ultimate of dharmas as ungraspable, and it also teaches entering into meditative absorption in yoga on that and eradicating distraction. It also expresses the profoundness of this point. ^3-95

###### Showing the Cultivation of Mental Engagement with a Lesser Object Despite Much Learning ^3-4-2-5-3-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-96]]

In order to free those who think, "If karma and agents and so forth are indeed ultimately non-existent, then how should bodhisattvas make aspirations in order to accomplish thoroughly pure buddha-fields?", from the hindrance of cultivating mental engagements with small objects, he spoke by way of the seventh point: "Whichever bodhisattva," and so forth. "Untrue" means false. Thinking, "If that is so, how did the Bhagavan speak in various places of the excellent thorough purity of the fields of bodhisattvas?", and asking, "Why is that?", in response, he said, "The arrays of fields," and so forth. Many fields are "the arrays of fields." "Without those arrays" means because ultimately there is no array whatsoever as an essence possessing parts or as an accumulation of subtle particles. ^3-96

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-97]]

Although those arrays are indeed entirely non-existent, nevertheless, like the arrays of fields apprehended in a dream, they are taught to exist conventionally as merely pleasant when unexamined; therefore, they are called "the arrays of fields". He thought, "Were it not so, since the ultimate nature cannot be expressed, it would not be expressed." Although non-abiding has been explained above, in order to benefit those of a forgetful disposition, to make them remember by means of summarizing at the end, he said, "Therefore," and so forth. "Thus" means: because no entity whatsoever is established in the ways stated, one should generate the mind without abiding in that—this is the meaning of the words. The remainder is as before. By this saying, "He speaks untruly of that," implicitly, it thoroughly shows that being attached, thinking, "I will accomplish the arrays of fields ultimately," is the discordant class. By stating just a sign, "The arrays of fields," it thoroughly shows freedom from all cultivation of mental engagements with small objects. It also teaches aspiration and making prayers regarding the cultivation of mental engagement with small objects. By this saying, "The Tathāgata spoke of them as without those arrays," since it posits the ultimate, it introduces entering into meditative absorption in yoga, and teaches profundity. ^3-97

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-98]]

By this saying, "Therefore, they are called 'arrays of fields'," having refuted the extreme of deprecation, it teaches eradicating distraction, and thoroughly shows the vastness of this point. ^3-98

###### Showing the Forsaking of Sentient Beings Despite Cultivating Mental Engagement with a Non-Lesser Object ^3-4-2-5-4-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-99]]

In order to free those who think, "Although sentient beings do not exist apart from the aggregates, is there not indeed something non-different? Otherwise, how would a bodhisattva engage in thoroughly ripening sentient beings?", from the hindrance characterized by forsaking sentient beings, he spoke by way of the eighth point: "Suppose, for example..." "A man" refers to Rāhu, the lord of the asuras; his body in the desire realm is the size of Mount Meru. Although it is indeed large, it is large pointlessly. Desiring to express the intention behind why the Bhagavan taught this, he asked, "Why is that?", and in order to express that very intention, he said, "There is no entity." Because it is ultimately devoid of the nature of one or many, it is entirely without an essence; like a dreamed body in a dream, the Tathāgata spoke of it conventionally, but not ultimately. Saying, "Therefore it is called a body," expresses the reasoning here. He thought, "Thus, this is merely conventional; therefore it is called that. Were it not so, because no dharma whatsoever is established ultimately, it would not be called that." In order to refute the Rgyaldpogpas and so forth who assert the non-existent as an entity, he said, "Because it has no entity." Since non-entity is the defining characteristic of abiding having abandoned entity, how could it not become an essence discordant with that? Therefore, it is called a body. Were it not so, since no body whatsoever is established ultimately, what would be called a body here? Therefore, this teaching of non-entity thoroughly shows this: even whatever body is the size of Mount Meru, when it is ultimately not a body of a self because of having no essence, then how could the bodies of others possess an essence or possess a self? By this, implicitly, it teaches that for a bodhisattva who strives in thoroughly ripening sentient beings, being attached to sentient beings ultimately is the discordant class. It also teaches aspiration and making prayers to abandon that. By stating just a sign, "a man", it is stated to include all various distinct bodies, such as those born from eggs and so forth. ^3-99

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-100]]

By this saying, "The Tathāgata spoke of it as a non-entity," it posits the ultimate, and thoroughly shows the point of entering into meditative absorption in yoga on that. It also expresses profundity. ^3-100

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-101]]

By this saying, "Because it is a non-entity," it teaches eradicating distraction. ^3-101

###### Showing Engagement with Attachment to Outer Treatises Despite Not Forsaking Sentient Beings ^3-4-2-5-5-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-102]]

Now, by expressing that this dharma discourse is extremely meaningful, in order to free those who are attached to outer treatises from the distraction of being attached to them, he spoke by way of the ninth point: 'Subhūti, what do you think?' By this, it thoroughly shows the preeminence of this dharma discourse through four aspects: completely gathering merit, being revered by gods and so forth, doing what is difficult to do, and making equivalent to the Tathāgata and so forth. ^3-102

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-103]]

Herein, completely gathering merit is what was spoken as 'begets much merit on that basis.' Being revered by gods and so forth is what was spoken as 'that place becomes a stūpa.' Doing what is difficult to do is what was spoken as 'they will be endowed with supreme wonder.' Making equivalent to the Tathāgata and so forth is what was spoken as 'in that place dwells the Teacher or whoever is like a guru.' Herein, expressing the merit of the gift of precious things thoroughly shows it to be preeminent through two distinguishing aspects: the distinction of the entity and the distinction of the field. The preeminence in the distinction of the entity also consists of vastness and excellence. Herein, vastness is being extremely abundant through the particularity of the basis, which is what was spoken as 'as many world systems as those.' ^3-103

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-104]]

Excellence is being renowned as extremely rare in the world, which is what was spoken as 'having completely filled with the seven precious things.' The field is preeminent because it is endowed with the perfection of one's own and others' benefit. Herein, the perfection of others' benefit is because it teaches others unerringly the objects to be abandoned and to be adopted, which was spoken as 'by the Tathāgata.' Because he spoke exactly in accordance with that suchness of entities, as an etymology, he is called 'one who speaks thus.' The perfection of one's own benefit has two aspects because the perfection of pristine wisdom and the perfection of abandonment are distinct. Herein, this saying 'arhat' teaches the perfection of abandonment; because he has conquered the enemies of the afflictions, as an etymology, he is called 'arhat'. ^3-104

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-105]]

By this saying 'perfectly completely awakened one,' it teaches the perfection of pristine wisdom. Therefore, stating the three words is the meaning of a general summary: 'it is endowed with the fruit.' Saying 'should trust' means aspiring; saying 'should generate trust, should realize' means one should understand, that is, generate wisdom—this is the meaning of the words. Herein, the former is the fruit of the latter. Alternatively, the latter is the explanation of the former. 'Explained' refers to the words. 'Perfectly shown' means aspiring, which is the meaning of generating trust. 'Innumerable' is because it cannot be completely determined by counting. 'Immeasurable' is because its power cannot be measured or because it is without peer. 'Recites' means one recites the syllables oneself. 'Teaches' means perfectly teaching the meaning of the words to others. Alternatively, 'reciting' is the words; 'teaching' is the meaning. 'Becomes a stūpa' means worthy of homage. Being a stūpa itself is 'becoming a stūpa'; for example, like saying 'becoming an authority.' ^3-105

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-106]]

He intended: "If it becomes a stūpa even by teaching a verse merely on the selflessness of persons, what need is there to mention teaching both kinds of selflessness?" 'Taking up' refers to the words. 'Retaining' means not forgetting. 'Reading' means making it into a volume. 'Mastering' refers to the meaning. By this, it teaches retaining through the wisdom arisen from hearing. Because one also retains through reflecting and the wisdom arisen from reflecting, he spoke of 'properly', which means understanding the meaning unerringly—this is the meaning of the words. In some texts, only the single word 'retaining' appears; herein, 'retaining' should be viewed as both the words and the meaning. 'Supreme wonder' is because of understanding a profound and vast dharma of such a kind. 'The Teacher dwells' refers to the dharma preacher, because he performs the deeds of the Teacher; this means it is like the Lord Buddha himself dwelling in actuality. 'Whoever is like a guru' means whoever it may be, such as noble Mañjughoṣa and so forth; because he accomplishes his deeds, one should view that dharma preacher as dwelling there. ^3-106

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-107]]

In some texts, the two words 'another' and 'others' appear; that is a synonym for all of 'whoever is like a guru', because it teaches 'dwells there'. By all this, it teaches the vastness of this topic. Meaningfully, it thoroughly teaches that abandoning the thorough upholding of the true dharma and being attached to outer treatises is the discordant class; it teaches that, having completely abandoned attachment to them, one gives rise to aspiration and prayer toward striving in the dharma. By stating merely the sign 'from this dharma discourse', it thoroughly shows: "Having abandoned attachment to outer treatises, one should strive in all true dharma." Because this dharma discourse is endowed with inconceivable power, in order to benefit others, he expresses that merit is produced for sentient beings merely by hearing its name; therefore, noble Subhūti requested: "What is its name?" ^3-107

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-108]]

This saying, "How should this be upheld?" thoroughly shows that even by upholding it, the merit will become immeasurable. ^3-108

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-109]]

"Prajñāpāramitā": that which is its name is called such. By this, it is wisdom, and because it realizes and thoroughly cuts through the beyond, it goes beyond. That is a compound: "It is wisdom and also going beyond." Alternatively, the beyond of wisdom is the beyond of wisdom; because by this one realizes and thoroughly cuts through it, it is called such. That, moreover, by implication, is the Tathāgata's wisdom, which is endowed with the supreme of all aspects; by other wisdoms, the beyond cannot be gauged. Alternatively, "prajñāpāramitā" is exactly this word meaning "reached." That is solely the wisdom spoken of; because others cannot possibly go to its beyond. By this, the perfection of generosity and so forth are also explained. Because it will express that, this dharma discourse is also called such; just like poetic compositions such as Rol rnyed ma phrogs pa. ^3-109

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-110]]

Examining from the thought, "Since it is to be known individually by the Lord himself, it is unshared, and since it is ultimately unarisen, it cannot possibly be expressed by words," he asked, "Why is that?" and in reply he stated, "Subhūti, that which was taught by the Tathāgata as prajñāpāramitā..." Because ultimately it is to be known individually by oneself and is therefore unshared, and because ultimately all dharmas are utterly unarisen, it is indeed inappropriate to express it by words; nevertheless, one imputes externally onto the inherent nature of a conceptual image, and since that conceptualization is inherently mistaken, the aspect imputed by it is also false itself. Therefore, whatever prajñāpāramitā that has become a conceptual image was taught by the Tathāgata and made understood by words is itself not a perfection, but the Tathāgata's wisdom is not so, because in the true relative it has reached the beyond of the knowable. If it were not so, how would the Lord become omniscient? Because the cognizer is mistaken, it does not understand it and what has become a conceptual image as distinct. Therefore, it is said that in the nature of knowing, that very ultimate wisdom is taught by the Tathāgata. Thus, he intended: "There is no contradiction in teaching it like that." Alternatively, whatever is the Tathāgata's wisdom, that very thing taught by the Tathāgata in the nature of causing realization is ultimately solely non-existent, because ultimately there is no going beyond. The word 'itself' should be viewed as placed after 'is not a perfection'. By this, it thoroughly shows how one should practice by positing it ultimately and entering into meditative equipoise in it. ^3-110

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-111]]

In order to teach that just as prajñāpāramitā is taught by the Lord by imputing externally solely onto the inherent nature of a conceptual image, and not otherwise, likewise all dharmas are also taught by the Tathāgata, he said, "Subhūti, what do you think?" because ultimately all dharmas are unestablished. Even if they were established, because specific characteristics are unshared, conventions cannot be designated for them. Because general characteristics are imputed. Apart from specific characteristics and general characteristics, there is no other expressible thing, because those two have the characteristic of abiding in mutual exclusion. ^3-111

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-112]]

Therefore, the elder Subhūti, intending that ultimately there is no dharma whatsoever taught by the Tathāgata, said, "Lord, there is no dharma whatsoever taught by the Tathāgata." By this, it teaches the subduing of distraction and profundity. ^3-112

###### Showing Unskillfulness in Means to Overcome the Marks of Entities ^3-4-2-5-6-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-113]]

Although it has thus been extensively taught that all dharmas are selfless, in order to free those beginners who think, "How should one meditate on all dharmas as selfless?" from a lack of skill in means for overcoming the signs of entities, concerning the tenth topic he said, "Subhūti, what do you think?" and so forth. Here it teaches that by realizing them to be free from the nature of one and many through two kinds of means, one should meditate on all dharmas as selfless. Among those, the two kinds of means are the means of attenuating and the means of making unapparent. Regarding that, by saying "however many particles of earth there are," it teaches that if one realizes it to be free from a singular nature, it is the means of attenuating. This world system is not logically singular, because it is endowed with distinct dharmas such as being obstructed and unobstructed. Therefore, if it is broken down into many, one should accept breaking it down into subtle particles. Therefore, it teaches that if one meditates on it as subtle particles, singularity itself will be abandoned. ^3-113

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-114]]

Because bodhisattvas for the most part pay attention to non-small focus, because they focus on a great trichiliocosm world system, he said "great trichiliocosm". ^3-114

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-115]]

By this, vastness is thoroughly taught. Examining from the thought of an objector who objects, "If those subtle particles are also solely unestablished because they are undifferentiated by distinctions of directional parts, how would those particles become many?", having asked, "Why is that?", here in reply he said, "Whatever particles of earth there are..." In order to clear away the apprehension of those many particles of earth as a singularity, whatever was taught in the nature of subtle particles is solely non-particles, because ultimately, like the nature of an emanation by illusion, subtle particles are solely non-existent and are of a nature thoroughly imputed by the mind. By this, it is taught merely as a means for those apprehending it as singular, but ultimately it is not so. ^3-115

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-116]]

Therefore, saying "they are called particles of earth" is because they exist as logical conventionalities. If it were not so, he intended with the sole thought that they would not be expressed, because they do not exist ultimately and because specific characteristics are inexpressible. It is also illogical to teach them as expressible conventionalities, because their inherent nature does not appear otherwise. By this, it teaches that the means of realizing them to be free from a multiple nature is making them unapparent. Thus, because subsequently, having individually analyzed even the subtle particles by logic, one makes it apparent, one will realize world systems to be free from a multiple nature, and after that, one realizes them to be natureless. ^3-116

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-117]]

Thus, having first expressed the receptacle world as solely natureless because it is free from a nature of one and many, in order to also express the world of sentient beings, he said, "Whatever world systems there are..." Here, "world systems" intends the world of sentient beings, because the other was already explained previously. He intended with the thought that saying "they are non-systems" is because they are free from a nature of one and many. Thus, first of all, whatever form aggregate there is from among them was explained by the receptacle world itself. How the remaining four aggregates are not endowed with a nature of one and many was already analyzed previously. Because it is not so, yogis see all dharmas as ultimately natureless, like an illusion. By this, profundity is also expressed. By implication, manifest adherence to the receptacle world and the world of sentient beings as entities ultimately is thoroughly taught as the discordant class. In order to abandon that, aspiration and making wishing prayers are also taught; by thoroughly expressing a mere sign, saying "great trichiliocosm world system", it thoroughly teaches one to meditate on all entities as natureless. By saying "they are non-particles" and "they are non-systems," by this ultimate postulation, it thoroughly teaches how that is to be accomplished by entering into meditative equipoise in yoga. ^3-117

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-118]]

Therefore, by saying "they are called world systems," having refuted the extreme of deprecation, it teaches the subduing of distraction. ^3-118

###### Showing the Non-Accumulation of the Collection of Merit ^3-4-2-5-7-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-119]]

Now, whatever bodhisattvas, when making offerings to the Bhagavan due to the great benefit of accomplishing the collection of merit, do not dismantle his body, they will not thoroughly complete the collection of merit, because they have not engaged in correct mental attention. ^3-119

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-120]]

Therefore, in order to free them from not having accumulated the collections, concerning the eleventh topic he said, "Subhūti, what do you think?" and so forth. Here, the general summarized meaning is this: when making offerings and doing service to the Tathāgata, one does not view the Tathāgata through the perfection of marks; if one asks, "then how?", it is as ultimate dharmatā. The remaining explanation here accords with the topic of aspiring to attain the form body. Regarding this, one should also not suspect it of being a repetition of words, because previously it was taught to dismantle it at the time of aspiring to attain the form body, whereas now it is taught at the time of making offerings and doing service to the Tathāgata, so they are distinct. Here, by implication, manifest adherence to the form body as the Tathāgata is thoroughly taught as the discordant class, and in order to abandon that, aspiration and making wishing prayers are also taught. By expressing a mere sign, saying "the perfection of marks," the entire form body is thoroughly taught. Saying "they are non-marks" is the ultimate postulation. By "therefore" and so forth, it teaches the subduing of distraction. ^3-120

###### Showing the Experience of Alms Through Laziness, Gain, and Respect ^3-4-2-5-8-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-121]]

Thus, although the means for realizing selflessness in all dharmas has already been taught, in order to free from attachment those who, attached to body and life and craving gain and honor, have become lazy and attached to gain, honor, and alms, concerning the twelfth topic he said, "Subhūti, whatever man or woman..." and so forth. By this, it thoroughly teaches: "Since you create an obstacle to whatever merit is not obtained even by thoroughly giving up this many bodies, out of attachment for the sake of a single body that is essenceless and the abode of many hundreds of thousands of sufferings, it is proper to exert yourselves in this with your whole being, with a mind that does not regard body and life." By this, by implication, laziness and so forth are thoroughly taught as the discordant class. In order to abandon that, it also teaches aspiration and making wishing prayers for the sake of thoroughly retaining the holy Dharma and so forth. By expressing a mere sign, saying "from this dharma discourse," it makes one listen and so forth to the entire Mahāyāna. By saying "much more merit," it expresses extensiveness. Saying "by the force of the Dharma" means "by the force of faith in the Dharma." That the noble Subhūti shed tears should also be viewed as being for the purpose of causing other lazy ones to generate immense faith by expressing the marvelous Dharma of the Mahāyāna, through the blessing of the Bhagavan. ^3-121

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-122]]

Therefore, having wiped away his tears with his hand, he spoke to the Bhagavan as follows. ^3-122

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-123]]

He said, "Bhagavan, it is wonderful that the Tathāgata has spoken this dharma discourse to whatever extent." Saying "to whatever extent" is a distinction of non-action; it means he spoke to the bodhisattvas by means of however many aspects are suitable to be explained. ^3-123

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-124]]

Thinking, "Why?", he said, "Ever since my exalted wisdom arose." Saying "ever since" means: "Starting from when the exalted wisdom whose domain is the noble truth arose, up until now, I have not previously heard a dharma discourse like this, which, as taught, has supreme merit as its result and which is not the domain of śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas." By this, it expresses that it is difficult to find. ^3-124

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-125]]

Thus, having heard of that distinguished merit, in order to thoroughly abandon the fault of those who, having aroused joyous effort and engaged, will perceive this Dharma literally, he said this, "most wonderful": "Because they have conviction in such a generator of extensive merit that is difficult for base beings to have faith in, and because they have conviction in the extremely profound selflessness of persons and dharmas, they will be endowed with the most wonderful qualities, more than those like myself who have realized the mere selflessness of persons." Saying "correct perception" refers to perceiving the unmistaken meaning, but it does not refer to perceiving by thinking, "this is correct," while manifestly adhering to it literally. This is because, here, all dharmas being without nature is taught with the ultimate in mind, whereas conventionally they are not without nature. Therefore, he intended that it does not contradict what is seen and so forth. If there were a correct perception, that which is to be perceived would also exist ultimately, because if there were nothing to be perceived, it would absurdly follow that there is also no perception. ^3-125

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-126]]

Therefore, having considered the objection thinking, "How are all dharmas established as being ultimately without nature?", he asked, "Why is that?", and in response he said, "Bhagavan, whatever is a correct perception..." Because what is to be perceived does not exist whatsoever, and because it is free from the nature of one or many, ultimately that itself is a non-perception; therefore, it is taught that one should not perceive that correct perception as a correct perception. ^3-126

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-127]]

Because it exists as merely pleasing when unexamined, therefore the Tathāgata calls it "correct perception." If it were not so, he would not have spoken of it, because it would be non-existent in every way. By this, positing it ultimately, both how it is to be established by engaging in meditative equipoise and how distraction is to be eliminated are taught. It also expresses profundity. The extremes of superimposition and deprecation are also abandoned. ^3-127

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-128]]

In order to generate wonder in those lazy ones themselves, he said, "For me to understand and have conviction in this dharma discourse being explained is not wonderful to me, but, Bhagavan, in the future time, in the final five hundred years, whatever sentient beings take up, hold, and fully comprehend this dharma discourse, they will be endowed with the most wonderful qualities." The summarized meaning for this is this: "If, in the future, at the time when the true Dharma will be thoroughly destroyed, there will arise such bodhisattvas who will hold this dharma discourse and who will not apprehend a person and so forth, since you do not have such practice even at the time endowed with the festival of the true Dharma, this is an occasion of great shame for you." Here, saying "me" refers to having realized the selflessness of persons. Saying "this" refers to what completely teaches the selflessness of persons and dharmas. Saying "being explained" refers to being made clear directly by the Bhagavan. Saying "understand" is the aspect of pure faith, so it teaches pure faith. Saying "conviction" is the aspect of belief. Saying "those sentient beings" refers to those who will arise in the future time, separated from the Bhagavan. Whatever words are mentioned here, such as "taking up" and so forth, have already been explained earlier. This will be taught as follows: he intended, "It is not wonderful that I, having realized the mere selflessness of persons, have merely come to have faith and belief in such a Dharma that teaches the method of both kinds of selflessness by the power of having heard it directly from the Bhagavan; but whoever, when separated from the Bhagavan, realizes such a dharma discourse through the stages of holding it and so forth, and fully comprehends it, they will be endowed with the most wonderful qualities, because when separated from the Bhagavan and there are many who abandon the Mahāyāna, it is extremely rare for faith in it to arise." By this, it is taught that, having abandoned laziness, one should abide with aspiration and prayers for holding the true Dharma and so forth. In order to teach that they will not only be endowed with mere scripture but also with the perfection of realization, he further said, "Moreover, Bhagavan..." ^3-128

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-129]]

Mentioning "self" and so forth is an illustration; therefore, they will not engage in the perception of a dharma, because they realize both selflessnesses simultaneously. Regarding the thought thinking, "If selflessness and so forth exist, then at that time that perception would certainly also occur," having asked, "Why is that?", he said in response, "the perception of a self." By this, it teaches thus: "Leave aside for a moment a self and so forth as was just stated; if even this perception of a self thinking, 'this is me,' which is well known to all beings, is nothing whatsoever because it cannot bear the burden of analysis, what need is there to mention that the unexperienced entities of a self and so forth will not exist?" By this, engaging in meditative equipoise is taught. ^3-129

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-130]]

Having considered the internally stated question thinking, "Even if a self and so forth do not exist, having become mistaken about them, what makes it evident that the perception of a self and so forth will not occur?", he asked, "Why is that?", and in response he said, "Because the Buddha Bhagavans are free from all perceptions." The meaning regarding this is this: because they have realized the reality of all entities without exception exactly as it is, they are called "Buddha Bhagavans." Because ultimately there is nothing whatsoever to be perceived, they do not engage in perception regarding anything whatsoever. If it were not so, they would become mistaken. Bodhisattvas engage because they train following the Buddhas. ^3-130

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-131]]

If it were not so, how would one attain their state? Therefore, just as the Buddha Bhagavans trained in being free from all perceptions, bodhisattvas should also train. Therefore, whatever bodhisattvas train following them will certainly not engage in the perception of a self and so forth. Saying "so it is" was spoken twice by the Bhagavan, acknowledging it, in order to bring certainty to those to be tamed. Up to this point, it was spoken with regard to turning away from joyous effort. Now, in order to separate those persons who, having heard this profound Dharma, become mentally terrified and do not initiate joyous effort from the very beginning, from that non-initiation of joyous effort, he said, "whatever sentient beings..." and so forth. If they are terrified, they will not initiate joyous effort. In the Śrāvaka vehicle, the Bhagavan taught, "This is merely dharmas; regarding this, a person is completely non-existent," and in the Mahāyāna, he taught, "Even those mere dharmas are false." Therefore, those with uncultivated bodies whose hearts are connected with the view of a self that is co-emergent from beginningless time, upon hearing the selflessness of persons, become terrified, thinking, "I do not exist." In order to refute that, he said "not terrified." Those who are strongly attached to mere dharmas become completely terrified at the time of hearing the teaching on the selflessness of dharmas. Because whatever mere dharmas there are also do not exist, they think, "Alas! I am undone!" and because there is no refuge even if they look in the ten directions, they become completely terrified; in order to refute that, he said "not completely frightened." At the time of thinking, because they are unable to apply themselves to the non-existence of both, they will become completely frightened. In order to refute becoming completely terrified from the very beginning, thinking, "How will we realize both selflessnesses?", he said "will not become completely frightened." Others say, "At that time, whatever terror there is from not having realized the two kinds of selflessness, the small, medium, and great degrees are taught respectively by the three phrases." Others say, "The three kinds of terror are at the times of hearing, thinking, and meditating." ^3-131

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-132]]

Now, in order to separate them from attachment to gain and honor, and in order to benefit those who desire to hear, "they will be endowed with supreme wonder," having asked a question beforehand by himself, he said, "Why is that?" ^3-132

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-133]]

Saying "this is the highest perfection," because this prajñāpāramitā is the proximate cause of awakening, and because without this, giving and so forth are not perfections, it is the supreme of all perfections. ^3-133

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-134]]

In order to teach that it is not spoken of as the highest by me alone, but is spoken of as the highest by all Buddhas as well, he said, "whatever highest perfection." By this, it teaches thus: "Since even what is spoken by one Buddha is to be taken as suchness because its meaning is unmistaken, what need is there to mention what is unanimously intended by all Buddhas?" Therefore, if one accomplishes the highest with one's whole being, how will one not become endowed with supreme wonder? This teaching of it as the highest teaches the second occasion for shame: "If this Dharma is thus supreme, for you to remain careless, attached to gain and honor in this way, is very shameful." By this, thoroughly holding the mind is taught. ^3-134

###### Showing with Reference to the Inability to Bear Suffering ^3-4-2-5-9-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-135]]

Even though the occasion for shame has thus been explained, in order to separate those who are not inclined to continuously initiate joyous effort because of not tolerating suffering, from that non-tolerance of suffering, he spoke again, saying "Subhūti..." and so forth, with regard to the thirteenth point. Regarding this, this point teaches how one tolerates, by what sign that tolerance is known, what its aspect is, what kind of aspect it is, and what the antidote to the basis of non-tolerance is. ^3-135

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-136]]

If one asks, "How does one tolerate?": it is the non-apprehension of the nature of all dharmas; it is what is said as "Whatever is the Tathāgata's perfection of tolerance, that itself is not a perfection"; because ultimately the one who tolerates, that by which one tolerates, that towards which one tolerates, what is to be tolerated, and whatever tolerance is, are all not apprehended, that is precisely not a perfection. He intended: "Ultimately, because it is unborn in this way, it does not go beyond." By this, having taught that that tolerance is unborn by its intrinsic nature, it also states its profundity. ^3-136

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-137]]

Thinking, "What makes it evident that bodhisattvas tolerate bringing to mind all dharmas as unapprehended in this way?", he asked for a reason that makes it known, saying "Why is that?", and in response he said, "Subhūti, at whatever time..." and so forth. The limbs are the hands and feet and so forth. The minor limbs are the fingers and so forth. ^3-137

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-138]]

Saying "I did not have any perception whatsoever" refers to the perception of another's body, the perception of a weapon, the perception of cutting, the perception of an other, the perception of tolerating, and so forth; by this, it is stated that all dharmas are unapprehended. In response to the thought, "If there is no perception whatsoever, one would become mindless like a state of fainting," he said, "nor did I become without perception." He intended that at that time, the yogi's cognition that realizes all dharmas as selfless exists with the intrinsic nature of the genuine relative. ^3-138

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-139]]

Examining the question posed by the thought, "What makes that evident?", he himself said "Why is that?", and in response he said, "Subhūti, if..." The intention of saying "even the perception of malice" is this: for whomever there is a perception of self, by just that much, attachment to self will definitely arise. Thus, if one is endowed with attachment to self, the conceptualization of "mine" and "other" undoubtedly arises; in this way, one conceptualizes as "mine" whatever is included as benefiting oneself, and conceptualizes as "other" what is otherwise. Therefore, one engages in accordance with "mine". Therefore, one has attachment to what accords with self and "mine", and hatred for what contradicts them, and the entire collection of faults connected with them will also arise. Because of that very thing, it is explained: "If self exists, there is the perception of other; by dividing self and other, there is grasping and hatred; through connection with these, all faults thoroughly increase." He intended: "For whomever the collection of faults such as malice arises, how could I be renowned as speaking of tolerance? Because by reason of which, by the power of tolerance, the king of Kaliṅga would have gone to the sentient hells at exactly that time." By this, because there is no change when harm is done, the definitely arising things like malice and so forth do not exist; therefore, it is taught that such tolerance is known by the sign of being free from the perception of self and so forth. By this very thing, the three kinds of tolerance are also taught: here, by saying "by the king of Kaliṅga," the tolerance of not being intolerant toward those who do harm is thoroughly taught; by saying "limbs and minor limbs," it is the tolerance of accepting suffering; by saying "at that time to me" and so forth, it is the tolerance of certain realization regarding dharmas. By this, implicitly, the conceptualization of dharma and person is thoroughly taught as the discordant class, and it causes those to be tamed to abandon that and perfectly take up generating the tolerance just mentioned; in this way, by this, beings to be tamed think: "Since the Bhagavan himself cultivated such supreme tolerance for the sake of awakening, why should we not cultivate this? If one does not do difficult deeds, one will not attain the difficult-to-attain state." Therefore, it generates joy for that in those to be tamed. By stating just the sign, "limbs and minor limbs," all tolerance of accepting suffering is thoroughly taught. ^3-139

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-140]]

Now, in order to teach accordingly what kind of tolerance that is, he said, "Subhūti, I clearly know..." That too is the context of "when the king of Kaliṅga cut off my limbs and minor limbs"; because it is evident, it was not stated. That tolerance is also thoroughly taught in two aspects: having as its object the continual suffering of others doing harm, and having as its object the unbearable suffering of others doing harm. Regarding that, the object of continual suffering and so forth is what is called "for five hundred lifetimes"; by this, its vastness is stated. The object of unbearable suffering and so forth is what is stated as "limbs and minor limbs". The bases of intolerance are three kinds of suffering, as follows: the suffering of saṃsāra, the suffering of sentient beings engaging wrongly, and the suffering of being without resources. Regarding that, as the antidote to the first basis of intolerance, he said, "Therefore..." By all this, having abandoned intolerance, it is taught that one should abide by the aspiration and prayer for awakening; he intended: "If one generates the mind toward awakening, no one will be touched by the suffering of saṃsāra." ^3-140

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-141]]

Because even if one has generated the mind toward awakening a few times by chance, one is terrified by the perception of the three kinds of suffering through adherence to self and does not generate it again, he said, "having thoroughly abandoned all perceptions." The perception of self and so forth, and the perception of the three kinds of suffering are "all perceptions" here. As for adherence to form and so forth, having thoroughly examined saṃsāra like the śrāvakas and so forth, the mind is tormented by that suffering and one does not generate the mind toward awakening even on rare occasions; therefore, he said "without abiding in form" and so forth. Completely adhering to form and so forth is abiding in them; here too it is explained as before. Here it does not become a repetition: by this, the antidote to the basis of intolerance is stated; by the former, it is engagement in the perfections. ^3-141

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-142]]

Here, for those to be tamed, "nor abiding in non-existent dharmas" is to be supplied; by this, adherence to non-existence is negated. By saying "not abiding in anything whatsoever," adherence to form and so forth as existing merely nominally is negated; by this, meditative absorption in yoga is taught. ^3-142

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-143]]

Examining the question posed by the thought, "Is it because, while form and so forth exist, there is hatred for abiding in them? Or is it negated for the purpose of thoroughly abandoning wrong adherence to only non-existence?", he asked, "Why is that?", and in response he said, "because that very abiding is not abiding." The meaning regarding this is this: from beginningless time, your mind abides in form and so forth by the power of adhering to form and so forth as entities; whatever is adherence to truth does not abide ultimately because whatever entities of form and so forth there are do not exist. By this conclusion, "Therefore...", abiding in the second position is taught here; by the word "giving", as before, here too all six perfections are intended. As the antidote to the second basis of intolerance, he also said, "Subhūti..." and so forth. By all this, it is thoroughly taught thus: "Even if the bodhisattva joyfully undertakes engaging in the extremely difficult perfections, for whose sake does he do so? If those sentient beings also do not exist ultimately and the perception of sentient beings also does not exist ultimately, how could they become weary due to such sentient beings engaging wrongly?" By negating sentient beings and the perception of them, both kinds of selflessness are stated. ^3-143

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-144]]

By this, "for the sake of all sentient beings", its vastness is taught here. By all the rest, its profundity is taught. Having taught that for the time being it is proper to tolerate through reasoning, now, in order to teach that it is also proper through trust in the Tathāgata, he said, "speaking what is authentic" and so forth. The first is the teaching. The rest is the explanation. Alternatively, the four truths are taught. They should be correlated in order. Alternatively, "speaking what is authentic" teaches what is to be proven. "Tathāgata" teaches the reason: just as the blessed Buddhas have abandoned the obscurations of afflictions and to knowledge and are thus gone, since he too has gone and come in that way, he is the Tathāgata. Therefore, he speaks what is authentic, what is not mistaken; because he does not possess the obscuration that is the cause of teaching mistakenly, he proves it by the non-observation of a cause. Thinking, "How is it proven that he is exclusively a Tathāgata?", he said, "speaking the truth." That is whoever has the nature of speaking the truth, the characteristics of the four noble truths; therefore, he is determined to be called the Tathāgata. It is impossible to teach an extremely obscure meaning authentically in all aspects, as it was said: "What arises from method and its cause, being obscure, is extremely difficult to explain." Thinking, "How is it known to be true that he speaks the truth?", he said, "speaking suchness." Because he speaks the reality of entities, without mistake, just as they are, he speaks suchness. ^3-144

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-145]]

Because he speaks the reality of what is to be abandoned and what is to be adopted, along with the method, just as it abides, how could he not be speaking the truth? Thinking, "Even if that is so, what makes it evident that the four truths taught by him abide in that way?", he said, "not speaking falsely." The meaning is: because he speaks what is not false, possessing valid cognition, and does not speak what is invalidated by valid cognition, therefore it is the truth. By all this, the Blessed One is proven to be a valid authority by means of the reason of the result. For that very reason, it was said: "The entire authentic teaching of the Savior / proves his distinguished knowledge." Since ultimately not even one nature of an entity is established, if it is difficult to prove even one truth, how could there be four truths, such that by expressing four truths he would become one who speaks authentically? Thus, if the Blessed One taught that the five aggregates are like an illusion, how could their nature be the truth of suffering? Because suffering too is ultimately false, its origin and cessation also simply do not exist; for what is utterly non-existent like a rabbit's horn and so forth, those two are impossible. Since cessation does not exist, the path leading to it also ultimately does not exist; if there is nothing to be traversed to, there is no traversing. As it is taught: "Leaving aside awakening, if one sees not even one truth as established, how could there be four?" Having examined that, in order to abandon the extreme of exaggeration of those who adhere to "speaking authentically" according to the literal words, he also said, "Subhūti..." and so forth. "Neither true" is because ultimately no nature whatsoever is established. Regarding "nor false": because falsehood abides as the exclusion of truth, since that which is to be excluded does not exist, by what would it be false? Alternatively, "not true" is because the essence of how it is known is false like an illusion. "Nor false" is because it abides as merely pleasant when unexamined. By this negation of the two extremes, the middle path itself is taught. In order for the śrāvakas to abandon adherence to self, the four truths are taught by way of the aspect of mere selflessness of persons, but they are not for the sake of all, so there is no contradiction. ^3-145

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-146]]

Furthermore, whatever thought wonders, 'What is the difference between one who gives gifts by observing the body and so forth and pursuing them, and one who does not pursue them because of not observing anything?'—the remedy for this third basis of intolerance is as follows: he said, 'For example...' and so forth. One who gives gifts having fallen into pursuing the entities that have the nature of body, existence, and resources, adhering to them exclusively as happiness, is like a person who has entered into darkness. Even though they have eyes, because they do not observe the reality of entities, they do not know the meaning of what is to be abandoned and what is to be adopted; thus, they fall into mistaken entities, the state of all suffering. Therefore, the bodhisattva will completely fall away from the happiness of great resources. Because of not possessing that, being afflicted by suffering like the śrāvakas and so forth, they turn away from awakening. One gives gifts without falling into entities. One who gives gifts without observing entities of the body and so forth and without pursuing them is, for example, like a person possessing eyes who sees various forms because they exist, and having seen them, completely abandons what is to be abandoned and takes up what is to be adopted. ^3-146

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-147]]

To the thought, "Moreover, if the sun has risen, perhaps they might not see," he said, "when the sun has risen." To the thought, "Even if the sun has risen, if they are in a house or the like that is completely dark, they might not see," he said, "when day has dawned." The expression "when day has dawned" indicates [the dispelling of] darkness; therefore, the meaning is "in a place where there is no darkness." Having suspected the thought, "Even when those are assembled, a blind person would perhaps still not see," he said, "possessing eyes." That is how one should view a bodhisattva who has not fallen into entities. For them, the darkness of ignorance having dawned [i.e., cleared] and the sun of exalted wisdom having risen, they see various objects of knowledge exactly as they authentically are, and because the mistaken entities—the state of all suffering, the suffering which is to be abandoned—are false, they completely abandon them; and desiring the authentic state with the characteristics of unexcelled, completely perfect awakening—the state of all happiness, which is to be adopted—they begin to engage in it. Therefore, it is intended that bodhisattvas, having found the excellence of great resources, their bodies made steadfast by unexcelled happiness, will not turn back from awakening. For that very reason, they will not turn back. Previously, it was taught as "abiding" in that by mere observation. Here, it is "falling" into the characteristic of attachment to that, preceded by observation. Therefore, here "falling" is stated. In the former, "abiding" was stated. By all this, the thorough apprehension of the mind is taught. ^3-147

###### Showing with Reference to the Non-Accumulation of the Collection of Wisdom ^3-4-2-5-10-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-148]]

Even though they have been established in patience in this way, some taste the flavor of concentration, and becoming attached to it, they do not exert themselves in the Dharma of the Mahāyāna, which is the cause for completely fulfilling the accumulation of exalted wisdom. In order to separate them from being devoid of the accumulation of exalted wisdom, he spoke the words "Subhūti..." and so forth, in the context of the fourteenth basis. By this basis, in order to separate them from tasting the flavor of concentration, the excellence of the qualities of exerting oneself in the true Dharma is completely demonstrated through five aspects: being considered by the Tathāgata, completely gathering exceptionally noble merit, showing the Dharma and its accomplishment, being honored by gods and so forth, and complete purification of sins. Among these, being considered by the Tathāgata is what was stated as "The Tathāgata knows them..." and so forth. The explanation of the words "take up..." and so forth is like before. ^3-148

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-149]]

Because the Bhagavan is omniscient, he knows all and sees all. Having examined the thought, "What is so wonderful about them in that?", in order to teach the excellence of completely gathering merit, he said, "all those sentient beings..." The meaning is that by generating an immeasurable aggregate of merit, he knows that they are fortunate. ^3-149

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-150]]

Having examined the thought, "What excellence does that aggregate of merit have, by which it becomes immeasurable?", in order to teach the complete gathering of exceptionally noble merit, he again said, "Subhūti, whichever man or woman..." and so forth. Morning is a particular state of the sun, light, earth, water, and so forth; in order to teach that "time" is conceptually imputed only to such a particular state, and is not completely imputed by the Vaiśeṣikas and so forth, it is particularized by being in common locus with the word "time". Were it not so, since it is a single essence, there would be no differentiation such as "this is morning". If one accepts that it is differentiated by the particulars of others, then if those very particulars are the cause for imputing conventions such as morning to particular states of the sun and so forth, he intended, "What is the point of conceptually imputing time which does not do anything at all and whose power is not seen?" It is also not logical for it to be characterized by slow or fast intellects and so forth, because those slow intellects and so forth, even if arising from its cause, contradict arising gradually. That was indeed taught before. Since the valid cognition of refutation was taught previously in the context of refuting the self, there is no need for much more. Regarding that, in order to teach that the convention "time" is imputed to such particular states of living beings and so forth by the power of conventions generated by autonomous whim, and not by the power of conceptually imputing a connection between sound and meaning, it is particularized by the word "lifetime". If any one of them were stated, it would not teach the intention as it was taught, so all three words were stated. Because it is the object of convention, it is imputed to the word of convention and made into a common locus. Here this is stated in order to separate them from tasting the flavor of concentration, and since in the former passage giving and such an aspect of time were not taught, it does not become a repetition. ^3-150

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-151]]

By the words, "Generates a much greater, incalculable, and immeasurable merit than that very basis," its vastness is completely demonstrated. This is because it outshines the merit arising from giving that is exceptionally noble in terms of the particulars of number, substance, and time. Thus, "as many as the grains of sand in the river Gaṅgā" teaches the particular of number. "Body" means difficult to fathom, which is the particular of substance. "Hundreds of thousands of millions of billions of eons" is the particular of time. Those millions of eons are also billions. That is also hundreds of thousands - the words are combined thus. Regarding the thought, "How is that to be known?", in order to teach the excellence of praising the Dharma, he also spoke the words, "Subhūti..." and so forth. ^3-151

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-152]]

Because "this discourse on the Dharma is inconceivable" teaches here the exceedingly profound characteristic that all dharmas lack an intrinsic nature, which is to be individually known by the Tathāgatas and cannot be conceived by others such as the śrāvakas and so forth. "Incomparable" is because such an aspect is not taught elsewhere, so there is nothing comparable to this or exceptionally nobler than it. ^3-152

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-153]]

By this, because it has no equal and no superior, it teaches thus: "From an inconceivable cause, an inconceivable result itself arises." Regarding the thought, "How is it incomparable?", he spoke the words, "those who have perfectly entered the supreme vehicle." "Gone extremely beyond" is what is called "supreme"; thus he spoke the words, "this discourse on the Dharma of the śrāvakas and so forth..." "The highest vehicle" is grammatically analyzed as either the vehicle of the highest ones, or that it is both a vehicle and highest; those words are spoken to those who have perfectly entered into it. Similarly, "those who have perfectly entered the supreme vehicle" is also consistent, as those two belong to synonyms. The intention behind teaching synonyms is to benefit individuals to be trained, because some are transformed by one or another. Others say that it is "highest" in relation to vehicles other than this one. It is "supreme" because it completely purifies all afflictive obscurations and cognitive obscurations. Alternatively, "highest" means excellent. ^3-153

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-154]]

Regarding the thought that "even in the vehicle of the pratyekabuddhas there is excellence in relation to the vehicle of the śrāvakas," he spoke the words, "those who have perfectly entered the supreme vehicle." "Gone extremely to the utmost" is what is called "supreme"; since among the many vehicles of the śrāvakas and so forth this one has gone extremely to the utmost, the word "supreme" is stated here, and that is the Mahāyāna itself. Since the śrāvakas and so forth are not vessels for such a teaching of the Dharma, it was therefore spoken for the sake of the bodhisattvas. By that, it is established that "there is no comparison for this discourse on the Dharma." "Spoken by the Tathāgata" is because it is not the domain of others. ^3-154

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-155]]

By the words, "this discourse on the Dharma..." and so forth, having thoroughly established the meanings as they were explained, he concludes and teaches the excellence of praising those who accomplish it. Because this discourse on the Dharma is inconceivable and immeasurable, its ripening result is also inconceivable; therefore, the meaning is that "whoever holds this discourse on the Dharma..." and so forth is established. "Immeasurable" is what is taught. The rest is the explanation. ^3-155

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-156]]

Here, "inconceivable" is taught because it transcends the object of thought, and because it cannot be inferred. "Incomparable" means without equal. "Immeasurable" is because it cannot be measured even if all sentient beings were assigned to do so. "Boundless" is because it is not suitable to be measured even by great regions and so forth, thus it is greater than even space. Alternatively, this and all of them belong to synonyms. ^3-156

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-157]]

The words, "All those sentient beings will bear my awakening upon their shoulders," mean that by carrying the burden of awakening, it is like bearing it on the shoulder. By all of this, it is taught that having completely let go of tasting the joy of meditative concentration, one should abide through aspiration and prayer in the very retention of the Dharma and so forth, which is the cause for fulfilling the accumulation of pristine wisdom. "Why was it spoken by the Bhagavan for the sake of those who have perfectly entered the highest vehicle and not for others?" ^3-157

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-158]]

Thinking, "Does the Bhagavan also have hatred and desire?", he asked, "For what reason is that?" and in reply, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, those who are inclined toward the inferior..." and so forth. By this, it teaches thus: "Since the Bhagavan loves all sentient beings like an only child, he certainly does not possess hatred and desire; nevertheless, he teaches those who have become vessels at the time they become so, and not otherwise. If it were not so, since he is omniscient, the Bhagavan would become precisely non-omniscient." "By those inclined toward the inferior..." and so forth means by the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas. "By those who hold the view of a self..." and so forth means by the non-Buddhists. "There is no basis for that" means there is no occasion for that. The meaning is: "How could ordinary beings be able to hear what even the śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas are unable to hear? How could they be able to take up and master what those ones are unable to hear? It is impossible." By this, they are introduced to meditative absorption in yoga. If one asks, "If so, how was the noble Subhūti able to hear this discourse on the Dharma?", it is by the blessing of the Bhagavan, so there is no fault. In order to teach the excellence of the veneration paid by gods and so forth, demonstrating that "out of all places, the single place where this sūtra is explained will be praised," he also spoke the words, "Subhūti..." and so forth. Previously it was spoken in order to separate from attachment to outer treatises, whereas now it is spoken in order to separate from tasting the joy of meditative concentration, so there is no repetition; it should be known that there is no repetition because the meaning that summarizes everything is spoken precisely in different contexts. "Will be worthy of offerings" means with parasols, victory banners, pennants, and so forth. ^3-158

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-159]]

"Worthy of homage" means to be bowed down to. "Worthy of circumambulation" means to be circumambulated. Therefore, because it is endowed with qualities such as being worthy of offerings and so forth, it is a caitya. ^3-159

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-160]]

Regarding the thought, "If, among those who strongly endeavor in such a discourse on the Dharma as this, some appear to be afflicted, how could this be?", in order to rely on the distinction of thoroughly purifying misdeeds, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, whichever sons of noble family or daughters of noble family..." and so forth. "Will be afflicted" means harming by scolding, exposing faults, and so forth. "Will be thoroughly afflicted" means harming by killing, binding, and so forth. Regarding the thought, "How could such great beings be afflicted?", he asked, "For what reason is that?" and in reply, he spoke the words, "Thus, of those sentient beings..." and so forth. "Of previous lifetimes" means what was done in previous lifetimes. "In this very lifetime" means in the present lifetime. ^3-160

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-161]]

The words, "will also attain the awakening of a Buddha," mean that having thoroughly purified misdeeds, those who endeavor in meditation at another time will swiftly perfectly awaken to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening. ^3-161

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-162]]

Regarding what was spoken, "From that very basis, one generates a much greater merit, countless and immeasurable," thinking, "In what way should one understand it to be countless and immeasurable?", he spoke the words, "Subhūti, I directly know..." and so forth. Here, it is taught to be countless and immeasurable by way of power and magnitude; in that regard, the words, "Subhūti, I directly know that in the past, in times even more countless than countless eons..." and so forth thoroughly teach its power. This is the power of that accumulation of merit, because it outshines even all these accumulations of merit that have become much more distinguished and supreme. It is from Dīpaṃkara onwards up to countless eons; "countless eons" is because the counting is exhausted. The words, "Prior to, even much prior to Dīpaṃkara, I pleased however many eighty-four hundred thousand millions of trillions of Buddhas appeared," mean offering with the offering of practice. "Did not displease their minds" is because, even in the interim, that practice was not forsaken. "Whatever is..." refers to the specific action. ^3-162

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-163]]

Therefore, the meaning is this: of these two accumulations—the accumulation of merit of whatever is pleasing and so forth, and [the accumulation of merit of] whatever is taking up and so forth, which are [respectively] faith and so forth and the accumulation in the nature of latencies generated by it—the former accumulation of merit "does not even approach a hundredth part" of this latter accumulation of merit. In order to teach that it is even less than this amount, he spoke the words, "a thousandth part." "Number" is appropriate only for those that are similar; for example, one says, "The Himalayas, and the second is the Vindhyas," but not a white mustard seed. "Part" means a fraction. That also has a similar nature; for example, a part of Mount Meru is a mountain peak, but it is not the tip of a blade of grass. "Counting" is also only for those that are similar; for example, one says, "Mount Sumeru is a mountain, and the Himalayas are also a mountain," but one does not say a white mustard seed is. "Example": an example is also only for what is similar; for example, one says a gayal is similar to a cow, but one does not say so of a bee. Regarding the thought, "Although it is small, by the power of having thoroughly refined it, it will perform its function; for example, if a sickle is made sharp, it performs the function of a sword," he spoke the word "match": one finds a method of flattening and widening it. The meaning is: "Even if it is made sharp, because it is extremely small, it does not bear [the match]." For that very reason, he mentioned "as a cause", [meaning] it does not bear even [being] the entity of its cause. These should be known only from the word of the Omniscient One, because regarding such things that are beyond the objects of the sense faculties, only the word of the Omniscient One is authoritative. Furthermore, in a thoroughly pure time, at that time since all sentient beings are also thoroughly pure, it is not a great marvel; [but] in a thoroughly impure time, that sentient beings become thoroughly pure in their thoughts for even a mere instant is a marvel. Thus, for now, it has been taught to be immeasurable by way of power. ^3-163

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-164]]

Regarding the thought, "How is it countless by way of number?", he spoke the words, "Subhūti, if at that time..." and so forth. By that, it is thoroughly taught to be extremely numerous. Even by counting that merit, having become weary, one will become mad, a specific mental illness of the head becoming bewildered. Subsequent to that, arising from madness, one's mind will become disturbed. By this, the profound and the vast are taught. Regarding the thought, "How is it known that from this discourse on the Dharma, there will be such a result endowed with number and power?", he further spoke the words, "Subhūti..." and so forth. In all cases, the result will only be in accordance with the cause. It is also not apprehended that a nyagrodha tree and so forth, although small, because it is extremely endowed with an essence, becomes a great result called branches, leaves, and wood. ^3-164

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-165]]

Although this discourse on the Dharma also consists of few chapters, it is extremely profound, so because it teaches inconceivable dharmas, it is inconceivable. ^3-165

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-166]]

Therefore, from this inconceivable one, a result in accordance with the cause and condition will become inconceivable, because the power of the causes and conditions, being omniscience itself, is inconceivable. Here, the word 'maturation' refers to mere result; therefore, results in accordance with the cause and so forth will also be included. By all of this, thoroughly holding the mind is taught. ^3-166

###### Showing Strong Grasping to a Self ^3-4-2-5-11-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-167]]

Thus, although the tasting of the flavor of dhyāna has been cleared away, when approaching the time of clear realization, in order to separate from the apprehension of a self those bodhisattvas who, having seen their own distinction, think, "Since I have rightly entered the path of perfectly complete awakening, I will cause sentient beings to thoroughly pass beyond sorrow," and to teach how one should abide, how one should practice, and how one should thoroughly hold the mind at that time of clear realization, with reference to the fifteenth topic, the noble Subhūti asked the Bhagavan how one who has rightly entered the bodhisattva vehicle should abide and so forth. The teaching of the three—abiding and so forth—is just what the Bhagavan spoke earlier. ^3-167

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-168]]

By saying here, "Subhūti, because there does not exist at all any dharma called 'one who has rightly entered the bodhisattva vehicle'," by this addition he separates them from the apprehension of a self. The explanation of the rest is like before. Regarding what was said: "If this selflessness of persons and dharmas was earlier rightly and thoroughly explained by the Bhagavan, even though it has been so explained, how can the bodhisattva's apprehension of a self arise later at the time of clear realization? Why is it that, in order to clarify that, what has already been taught is taught again and again? However, if even after being taught thus they become completely bewildered, in that case, what was explained above would become endless"—although it has been taught thus, from beginningless time, by the power of having extremely firmly and manifestly adhered to a self, the complete bewilderment of some is not immediately reversed, so the meaning that has already been taught is taught again. By the very teaching of it again, because the complete bewilderment of the retinues at that time is reversed, it does not become endless. The result of the Bhagavan's teaching is reversing the complete bewilderment of the people to be tamed, so it is not without result. Therefore, when complete bewilderment has been reversed, since he does not engage [in teaching] again because it would be without result, this is weak. ^3-168

###### Showing Being Devoid of Instructions ^3-4-2-5-12-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-169]]

For the sake of those who think, "If it is so that there is no dharma whatsoever called 'one who has rightly entered the bodhisattva vehicle', how did the Bhagavan, having received instructions from the Teacher Dīpaṃkara, manifestly and completely awaken to unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening? Or, if there is no manifest and complete awakening, then one who desires clear realization does not need to thoroughly seek instructions," in order to separate them from the state of being unendowed with instructions, with reference to the sixteenth topic, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, what do you think of this..." and so forth. Earlier, he negated apprehending the explained dharma at the time of aspiring to attain the dharmakāya. Now, he negates manifest and complete awakening at the time of manifest and complete awakening, so it is not a repetition. Ultimately, there does not exist any dharma that is the nature of knower and object of knowledge, just as it was examined earlier. By the power of that, through the gradual generation of wisdom arising from hearing and so forth, which is merely difficult when examined, the Bhagavan's self-arisen omniscient pristine wisdom, subsumed by right conventional reality, merely arises by itself. Regarding that, if one asks, "Is it merely the power of the Bhagavan Dīpaṃkara?", although it may well be so, nevertheless, regarding that, the power of all buddhas is common; it is not of the Bhagavan Dīpaṃkara alone. ^3-169

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-170]]

Regarding the thought, "If there is no manifest and complete awakening, how did he prophesy the Bhagavan for manifest and complete awakening?", he spoke the words, "Subhūti, if..." and so forth. The intended meaning is this: If, ultimately, just as the dharma is individually and personally known by the Bhagavan, at that time I had manifestly and completely awakened, or if I had fallen into the manifest pride of thinking, "I have manifestly and completely awakened to the dharma," at that time, because of having already manifestly and completely awakened and because of erroneously and manifestly adhering to it, I would not have been prophesied by the Tathāgata. Why? Precisely because at that time I did not manifestly and completely awaken, and had no manifest adherence to dharmas and so forth, because I did not fall into error, I was prophesied to abide on the path of right pristine wisdom. By this saying, "there does not exist any dharma that has manifestly and completely awakened," by implication, the conceptualization of having received instructions from Dīpaṃkara is thoroughly taught to be the discordant class, and in order to abandon that conceptualization, aspiration and making wishing prayers are also taught. By expressing merely the sign "Dīpaṃkara", all conceptualizations of taking up a dharma are reversed. By this saying, "it does not exist", its profundity is also expressed. ^3-170

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-171]]

By teaching that instructions are thoroughly sought for the sake of perfectly complete awakening, its vastness is expressed. This is because it is extremely superior to the instructions of śrāvakas and pratyekabuddhas. ^3-171

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-172]]

"If that dharma which the Tathāgata manifestly and completely awakened to as unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening does not exist, then how at that time could he be a Tathāgata? In this way, if one says 'Tathāgata' because, just as previous buddhas have gone to manifest and complete awakening, likewise he has gone here and manifestly and completely awakened, since there does not exist any dharma whatsoever to manifestly and completely awaken to, how could that be?" Regarding the thought of someone who thinks thus, the question "Why is that?" was asked, and in reply, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, 'Tathāgata'..." and so forth. "Suchness" refers to the unerring nature of dharmas; its essence is peaceful from the very beginning and so forth. Furthermore, since that is common to all dharmas, it is particularized by stating "right"; "right", with the meaning of unerring, refers to the pristine wisdom of the Tathāgata. Its suchness, the true nature which is ultimately unarisen, is called "Tathāgata", just as it was stated: "The always unarisen dharma is the Tathāgata." Moreover, since the true nature is ultimately not other than the phenomena, the essence of realizing the true nature of all dharmas being ultimately unarisen—which is pleasing only when unexamined, and is ultimately unarisen, but exists as thoroughly imputed in right conventional reality—this extremely superior pristine wisdom itself is called "Tathāgata"; this is the meaning. Furthermore, because it is ultimately unarisen, there is nothing whatsoever to be apprehended or to apprehend, so ultimately there does not exist any dharma whatsoever that he manifestly and completely awakened to, but in reliance on right conventional reality, how that extraordinary pristine wisdom manifestly and completely awakened is merely called "Tathāgata"—this is the intended meaning. ^3-172

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-173]]

Regarding the thought, "Why is it not said that he manifestly and completely awakened in reliance on the ultimate?", he spoke the words, "Subhūti, if anyone..." and so forth. "Why is that?" is connected to "does not speak truly". The rest is easy to understand, so it is not explained. "Well then, regarding the presentation that the Tathāgata manifestly and completely awakened conventionally, what is its true nature presented as?" Regarding this thought, he spoke the words, "Whatever dharma...". "Whatever" refers to the dharmas characterized as form and so forth. "In that there is no truth" means that its appearing aspect, like an illusion, does not arise with any strength when examined. Because it is utterly non-existent like a rabbit's horn. "There is also no falsehood", because in an unexamined essence it appears like an illusion. Alternatively, because ultimately whatever dharma it is is not established, "there is no truth". Because whatever truth to be analyzed is not established and all dharmas are unarisen, "there is also no falsehood". By this, having negated the extremes of superimposition and denigration, it establishes the middle way and induces entry into meditative equipoise upon that. "Therefore" means because thus ultimately all dharmas are unarisen, so there is neither truth nor falsehood. The Tathāgata is also differentiated by the completely pure true nature of being unarisen; therefore, because the unarisen true nature is common, the Tathāgata said, "All dharmas are the Buddha's dharmas," as in, "The always unarisen dharma is the Tathāgata. All dharmas are also like the Sugata. Those with childish minds who apprehend signs practice dharmas that do not exist in the world," and so forth. ^3-173

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-174]]

Regarding the thought, "If these dharmas are exclusively unarisen, well then, how can they be called 'all dharmas'?", he spoke the words, "Subhūti, what is called 'all dharmas'..." and so forth. Here the meaning is this: this is not a convention designated because they exist ultimately, but rather, although form and so forth do not ultimately exist as dharmas, because they bear their own and general characteristics in reliance on the unexamined non-ultimate, they are therefore called "all dharmas." Ultimately they are not, because for one abiding in that, even though everything is seen, all conventions are non-existent. By this, the suppression of distraction is taught. ^3-174

##### Showing Clear Realization ^3-4-2-6-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-175]]

Regarding the thought, "If that dharma which the Bhagavān manifestly and completely awakened to does not exist at all, how could bodhisattvas, having actualized the taking up of the instructions, realize that selflessness of dharmas and persons?", having analyzed that, in order to teach clear realization he spoke the words, "Suppose, for example..." and so forth. This teaches clear realization through the attainment of pristine wisdom and the absence of pride of superiority. If one asks, "What is it like through the attainment of pristine wisdom?", it is in reliance on two types of pristine wisdom: the pristine wisdom that thoroughly apprehends the lineage and the pristine wisdom of equality. Therein, the pristine wisdom through the attainment of which one is born into the lineage of the Tathāgata, and is certain in the lineage of the Buddha, is the pristine wisdom that thoroughly apprehends the lineage. Because that has arisen, one is "possessed of a body". "Possessed" means making it one's own; "body" is the body of the Tathāgata, so whoever has attained that is "possessed of a body". Regarding the thought, "How does one know that that pristine wisdom has been attained?", he spoke the words, "a great body". This teaches clear realization after having attained the pristine wisdom of equality through the equality of the selflessness of persons and dharmas; therefore, it teaches that it is "the pristine wisdom of the lineage". Through that pristine wisdom of equality, having realized all sentient beings as the selflessness of persons and dharmas, one subsumes them exclusively within oneself. Therefore, because one subsumes the bodies of all sentient beings into oneself, it is "a great body". It is not that this is not the literal meaning: thus, because all sentient beings are subsumed into oneself, it is called "body". Furthermore, since those are extremely numerous, it is a "great body", so it is not the literal meaning. This expresses the vastness of this topic. ^3-175

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-176]]

The intended meaning is this: this clear realization of a bodhisattva is also merely differentiated by the arising of extremely superior pristine wisdom in reliance on right conventional reality, but in order to teach that ultimately there is nothing whatsoever that clearly realizes or is to be clearly realized, the noble Subhūti stated to the Bhagavān the words, "Bhagavān, a person possessed of a body [spoken of] by the Tathāgata..." and so forth. By this, in meaning, it thoroughly teaches [that this] is the discordant class of adhering to "possessed of a body" and "a great body". In order to abandon that, it teaches abiding by aspiration and prayer. ^3-176

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-177]]

By thoroughly expressing merely the sign "possessed of a body and a great body", it turns bodhisattvas away from adhering to all dharmas. It also establishes [them] in the ultimate, and also induces entry into meditative equipoise upon that. It also expresses profundity. It also abandons the extreme of superimposition. The previous one negated the extreme of denigration. "If the Tathāgata stated that that very body is non-existent, then at that time how could bodhisattvas who have attained clear realization think thus, 'I have attained the clear realization of a bodhisattva, so now I will liberate sentient beings'?" Regarding this concept of others, having analyzed the supreme, in order to teach clear realization through the absence of pride of superiority, he spoke the words, "Whatever bodhisattva...". ^3-177

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-178]]

By this, it teaches that whoever thinks thus, "I will thoroughly lead sentient beings beyond sorrow," or thinks, "I am a bodhisattva," possesses the pride of superiority and in the genuine sense is not a bodhisattva. The words "Why is that?" are a thorough inquiry here into the logical reason that makes this known. What is called "dharma" is the nature of form and so forth, or the nature of persons and so forth. The noble Subhūti, because that dharma is not seen when analyzed by reasoning, stated the words, "that does not exist". Because that reasoning is common to all, the Bhagavān, in order to induce entry into both selflessnesses, spoke the words, "Therefore...". The words "No sentient being..." and so forth are illustrative; they should also be viewed as being without nature. By this, the suppression of distraction is taught. Clear realization has been explained. ^3-178

##### Showing the Thorough Seeking of the Buddha Ground ^3-4-2-7-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-179]]

From here on is the thorough seeking of the Buddha. Furthermore, that of the Buddha is thoroughly subsumed by seven excellences. The seven excellences are the excellence of the thoroughly pure field, the excellence of thoroughly pure unsurpassable seeing, the excellence of thoroughly pure unsurpassable knowing, the excellence of gaining mastery over the accumulation of merit, the excellence of body, the excellence of speech, and the excellence of mind. ^3-179

###### The Excellence of the Complete Purity of the Field ^3-4-2-7-1-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-180]]

Here, in order to clear away the doubt thinking, "If there is no dharma whatsoever called a bodhisattva, who will make effort in order to thoroughly accomplish a buddha-field?", having made it the context of the excellence of a thoroughly pure buddha-field, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, whatever bodhisattva..." and so forth. Furthermore, the words "should also be expressed in that way" mean that because of possessing the pride of superiority, one should not say "he is a bodhisattva." This is because, without having realized the selflessness of persons and dharmas, they act with the pride of superiority, thinking, "I have realized [it]." Thus, by acting with the pride of superiority thinking "that is me", they have not realized the selflessness of persons. Because they act with the pride of superiority regarding that [even though] the arrays of fields do not exist, they have not realized the selflessness of dharmas. By this, how a bodhisattva should abide by resolving upon the arrays of fields as being like an illusion is taught. For those who think, "If ultimately those arrays of fields are non-existent, how did the Bhagavān state [them]?", he asked the words, "Why is that?" The explanation of this is like the previous one. This also does not become a repetition. The previous one is in order to clear away the conceptualization of a bodhisattva on the occasion of a bodhisattva abiding on the stage of action by resolve praying for a thoroughly pure bodhisattva field. Now, it was spoken in order to clear away the conceptualization of those who conceptualize thus, thinking "when bodhisattvas engage in order to accomplish a thoroughly pure buddha-field, they engage by adhering thus." At that time, because such other conceptualizations of some do not arise, it also does not become endless. By this, how a bodhisattva should practice upon this is taught. ^3-180

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-181]]

Regarding the thought, "What sort of [person] is called a bodhisattva?", he spoke the words, "Subhūti, whatever bodhisattva..." and so forth. Saying twice the words "dharmas are selfless" teaches that dharmas are selfless through the two kinds of selflessness. Saying twice the words "bodhisattva, being" teaches the realization of those very two kinds of selflessness. Alternatively, one [of the phrases] "he is a bodhisattva" is repeated. The latter, the words "is called a bodhisattva", is the method. If one asks, "Then what is being taught?", it teaches the words, "He is called a bodhisattva because of comprehending both of those non-existences: the absence of apprehending persons and so forth, thinking 'Who will accomplish [it]?', and also the absence of apprehending as dharmas the 'arrays of fields'." By this, thoroughly restraining the mind is taught. By this entire point, profundity is taught. By teaching that bodhisattvas engage with it by resolving in order to accomplish that illusion-like buddha-field, extensiveness is expressed. ^3-181

###### The Excellence of the Complete Purity of Unsurpassed Vision ^3-4-2-7-2-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-182]]

If there is no dharma whatsoever called a bodhisattva, at that time the Tathāgata would be exclusively devoid of possessing thoroughly pure seeing and thoroughly pure knowing; because, since that arises from a bodhisattva, if that does not exist, how would that be fit to occur? That which is without a cause would become the meaning of existing eternally and so forth. ^3-182

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-183]]

Therefore, for the sake of whoever in the gathered assembly conceptualizes thus, having made it the context of the two, unsurpassable thoroughly pure seeing and knowing, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, what do you think of this?" and so forth. The noble Subhūti also, having first grasped the intention of the Bhagavān, stated the words, "He possesses [it]." By this, [it is taught that] even though ultimately there is no dharma whatsoever called a bodhisattva, conventionally there is the very existence of a bodhisattva whose nature is mere aggregates pleasant only when unexamined, who, having thoroughly fulfilled the accumulations of merit and pristine awareness, becomes a Tathāgata of thoroughly pure knowing and seeing. Were it not so, if that did not exist even conventionally, then who would become a Tathāgata? Thus it teaches. Here, not saying that the Tathāgata possesses only the eye of prajñā teaches that he possesses the eyes in order to teach that thoroughly pure seeing is distinguished. Briefly, there are four kinds of eyes: apprehending form, apprehending ultimate truth, apprehending conventional truth, and apprehending the objects of knowledge in all aspects. Apprehending form is also of two kinds: the result of karma and the result of meditation. They are in accordance with the sequence of the five eyes. Here, because the object is coarse, apprehending form is taught first. By the power of the ultimate, conventional knowledge becomes thoroughly pure; therefore, apprehending the ultimate truth is taught first, and apprehending the conventional truth is explained later—this is the connection teaching the sequence. ^3-183

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-184]]

He possesses the eye of prajñā regarding the object that is the selflessness of persons and dharmas. The person is empty because conventionally it is merely appearing as dharmas but is not a person; regarding the object of mere dharmas is the eye of dharma. Knowing all objects of knowledge in all aspects is called the eye of the Buddha. By this, by teaching the five eyes in genuine conventionality because he dwells directly actualizing [them] now, thoroughly pure seeing is expressed. ^3-184

###### The Excellence of the Complete Purity of Unsurpassed Knowledge ^3-4-2-7-3-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-185]]

In order to express thoroughly pure knowing, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, what do you think of this?" and so forth. The minds and mental factors of the three times are the stream of mind. Therefore, because the three times are the object, knowing that object is called 'knowing', not 'seeing'. Furthermore, it should be known as the various natures of affliction and purification, such as 'a mind with attachment' and 'a mind free of attachment'. ^3-185

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-186]]

The words 'that many world systems' are an illustration, because he thoroughly knows the streams of sentient beings belonging to infinite world systems. Thus, conventionally the Tathāgata possesses the fleshly eye and so forth; therefore, a bodhisattva should dwell in aspiration and prayer in order to accomplish that. By this passage, both excellences are taught. ^3-186

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-187]]

The fleshly eye and so forth are themselves unapprehendable when analyzed by the nature of one and many, and it is also unreasonable to apprehend them by consciousness as a different entity. Investigating the intention of one who thinks, 'If it is like what was analyzed before, how can the Tathāgata possess the fleshly eye and so forth and thoroughly know the stream of mind?', the question, 'Why is that?' was asked. And in order to teach how a bodhisattva should accomplish that by engaging in meditative equipoise in yoga on these two purities, he spoke the words, 'the stream of mind, the stream of mind' and so forth. The words 'there is no stream' are from the ultimate perspective. ^3-187

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-188]]

Even though they are without nature, it is spoken based on conventionality; for that very reason, he spoke the words, 'it is called the stream of mind'. The intention is the thought that, were it otherwise, ultimately it would not be expressed because it is beyond all spheres of expression. Mentioning 'stream of mind' is an illustration; this is the occasion to express the words 'the fleshly eye and so forth are also taught as lacking the nature of an eye', which is stated directly. In order to teach how the mind should be thoroughly restrained regarding this, the question 'Why is that?' was asked, connecting to the words, 'there is no stream'. Because the past mind and so forth are ultimately unarisen, they are unapprehendable, but it is not that they are unapprehendable because it is invalid to apprehend them even though they exist. If that were so, it would not be taught that there is no stream of mind, because merely being unapprehendable does not make entities lack a nature. How the minds of the three times do not exist ultimately has been explained before and will also be taught. Mentioning 'mind' is an illustration; therefore, the past and so forth of the fleshly eye and so forth are also unapprehendable. Here, by the words 'there is no stream' and so forth, profundity is expressed. By the remainder, extensiveness. ^3-188

###### The Excellence of Possessing the Heap of Merit ^3-4-2-7-4-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-189]]

If the minds of the three times do not exist, at that time the aggregate of merit would also not exist, because that depends on the mind. ^3-189

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-190]]

Therefore, having investigated the thought, "How should a bodhisattva strive to accomplish the excellence of possessing the aggregate of merit of the Tathāgata?", addressing the excellence of possessing the aggregate of merit, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, what do you think of this?" and so forth. ^3-190

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-191]]

By this, he teaches thus: "If a son of noble family or daughter of noble family who gives away even outer entities such as jewels comes to possess a great aggregate of merit, why would the Tathāgata not possess the aggregate of merit by having habituated to many hundreds of hardships for immeasurable, innumerable eons?" Therefore, it is taught that although the aggregate of merit does not exist ultimately, because it exists conventionally, a bodhisattva should dwell in aspiration and prayer in order to accomplish mastery over it. This also expresses extensiveness. ^3-191

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-192]]

Having investigated the thought, "Why is the aggregate of merit not posited as existing ultimately?", in order to teach both how a bodhisattva should accomplish this and how the mind should be thoroughly restrained, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, if" and so forth. The words "if" (gal te) are synonymous with "if" (ji ste). ^3-192

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-193]]

The words "the Tathāgata would not speak" are because ultimately expression does not apply; the intention is the thought, "Even without being taught, it would be renowned like that." How the aggregate of merit does not exist ultimately has already been taught previously. By this, both how a bodhisattva who has engaged in order to accomplish the excellence of possessing the Tathāgata's aggregate of merit should accomplish it by engaging in meditative equipoise in yoga through not apprehending it ultimately, and how the mind should be thoroughly restrained by abandoning adherence to apprehending it ultimately, are taught; profundity is also expressed. ^3-193

###### The Excellence of the Body ^3-4-2-7-5-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-194]]

If the aggregate of merit were indeed non-existent, then at that time the two aspects of the excellence of the body of the Tathāgatas—the excellence of the body of minor marks and the excellence of the body of marks—which are asserted to be born from unsurpassed merit, would also not exist because they would be without a cause. ^3-194

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-195]]

Therefore, having investigated the thought, "How should a bodhisattva strive in order to thoroughly accomplish the two aspects of the excellence of the body of the Tathāgata?", for that sake, addressing the excellence of the form body of the Tathāgatas, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, what do you think of this?" and so forth. The words "form body" should be viewed as being the body of minor marks. ^3-195

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-196]]

The body of marks will be explained below, in the manner of "cow and ox". From this, the thought of the noble Subhūti is this: "Just as the aggregate of merit is completely non-existent as an aggregate ultimately, likewise the form body is also completely unarisen; therefore, one should not view the Tathāgata by the thorough establishment of a form body ultimately." Conventionally, just like a Buddha magically emanated by an illusion, the form body does indeed exist; therefore, it is indeed suitable to arise from the aggregate of merit. Therefore, it is taught that a bodhisattva, having belief in the form of a Buddha magically emanated by an illusion like that, should dwell in aspiration and prayer in order to accomplish it. Here, it will also not be repetitive; because previously, at the time of aspiration to obtain the form body on the level of action through belief, and at the time of making offerings and serving the Tathāgata, adherence to it in that way is completely abandoned, whereas now, it is spoken in order to clear away the bad conceptualization of those in the retinue who say, "A bodhisattva who has engaged in order to accomplish the excellence of the form body of the Tathāgata on the level of pure superior intention possesses adherence to form". ^3-196

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-197]]

Thinking, "Then how is the form body of the Tathāgatas taught?", he asked, "Why is that?", connecting to the words "one should not view the Tathāgata by the thorough establishment of a form body." This is unestablished; the form body is not established as having the nature of possessing limbs, or as having the nature of accumulated subtle atoms, just as it was analyzed previously. By this, how a bodhisattva should accomplish it is taught. The word "therefore" means it exists thus conventionally; because it is not that it does not exist in all aspects like the horn of a rabbit. By this, having refuted the extreme of deprecation, how the mind should be thoroughly restrained is taught. By all these points, profundity is taught. By teaching that bodhisattvas should strive in order to accomplish the excellence of the form body of the Tathāgata conventionally, extensiveness is also taught. Addressing the excellence of the body of marks, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, what do you think of this?" and so forth; all the explanation here is identical to the previous one. ^3-197

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-198]]

If one should not view the Tathāgata by the thorough establishment of a form body, at that time the teaching of the Bhagavān would also not exist, because that is subsumed by the form body. ^3-198

###### The Excellence of Speech ^3-4-2-7-6-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-199]]

Therefore, having investigated the thought, "How should a bodhisattva strive in order to accomplish the excellence of the speech of the Tathāgata?", for that sake, addressing the excellence of speech, he spoke the words, "Subhūti, what do you think of this?" and so forth. ^3-199

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-200]]

Ultimately, because there is no teacher, no thing to be taught, and no teaching, the Tathāgata does not consider the thought, "I have taught the Dharma." If it were not so, because it would become a mistake, he would not become omniscient. How a teacher, such as a self and so forth, a thing to be taught, such as outer and inner entities, and a teaching, meaning an action of form or the nature of sound, do not exist, has already been extensively taught in that way previously, because it was previously established that emptiness pervades all entities. Conventionally, the teacher, the thing to be taught, and the teaching are merely imputations of convention that are pleasing when unexamined, but ultimately they are not. Thinking, "Even though the reality of entities has been taught like that, if some become bewildered and think that the three exist ultimately, then at that time what would happen?", he spoke the words, "Subhūti, whoever..." and so forth. "Deprecation" is because of speaking meaninglessly. Thinking, "How can that which is non-existent speak?", he spoke the words, "by wrong grasping", meaning that because it is wrongly grasped through mistaken adherence, that does not exist. By this, how a bodhisattva should dwell is taught: "A bodhisattva who engages in the excellence of the speech of the Tathāgata should engage by knowing it to be like an echo and so forth". ^3-200

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-201]]

Thinking, "If those three, the teaching and so forth, do not exist, how are these various natures of words and speech, the teaching of the Dharma by the Bhagavān, which teach the selflessness of persons and phenomena and so forth, known? If that exists, then what is taught by it, the thing to be taught, also indeed exists; and what teaches it, the teacher, also indeed exists, so by implication, all three exist," he asked, "Why is that?", and here in reply, he spoke the words "the teaching of Dharma". Having intended this thought: "If the teaching of Dharma ultimately exists, it would presumably be either the nature of a specific characteristic or the nature of a general characteristic. Firstly, it is not the first alternative, because all ultimate specific characteristics were previously extensively cleared away. It is not the second either, because if specific characteristics do not exist, since there is no basis, there is nothing at all. If a general characteristic is accepted as an entity, because it would be subsumed merely as a specific characteristic, by that being cleared away, that too is indeed cleared away. If one conceptualizes that a general characteristic is an imputed nature through the exclusion of the other, because the teaching of Dharma would lack reality, it would be establishing what is already established. Even if one were to allow that its specific characteristic ultimately exists, it is illogical for that to be the teaching of Dharma, because what is thoroughly imputed as words by others upon the conceptualized nature of manifested sounds and so forth contradicts performing a function sequentially or non-sequentially; because it is devoid of all potency, it has no connection to anything at all, such as consciousness and so forth. Without doing anything, how could it become words? And if a specific characteristic is impermanent, because what is seen at the time of imputing the designation does not follow along at the time of imputing the convention, it is illogical to be words. It is illogical for what is imputed with the designation to be other and what is imputed with the convention to be other, because it would result in absurd consequences." ^3-201

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-202]]

Therefore, because there is no following along, it is also illogical for a specific characteristic to be expressed. If a general entity is also accepted as that which is to be expressed, because it becomes the nature of that which is to be expressed, because it is the nature of a specific characteristic, it indeed does not go to other objects. It is also illogical for a single thing to pervade many manifestations that are individually distinct regarding object, time, and so forth. Like this: if its entire identity is complete in one manifestation, at that time, through whatever thoroughly conceptualizes its nature in that one alone, there does not exist in it another nature related to other manifestations that would permeate others, because its singleness would be impaired. If it pervades by a part, it would result in the faults of possessing parts and of not being apprehended. Like that, if it is apprehended in accordance with positing that it pervades however many distinct manifestations by a part of it, although it might be suitable to apprehend it because apprehending in one manifestation is apprehending a part, in those individually distinct manifestations of age, object, time, and so forth, because apprehending from one manifestation is apprehending a part, since there is no simultaneous apprehension, it would indeed become devoid of conception. Therefore, that which is to be expressed and the expression are logical only as the nature of an imputed aspect, but ultimately they are not. Because of that very reason, the Bhagavān stated elsewhere: 'By whatever names, whatever phenomena are expressed, they do not exist in that; that is the reality of phenomena.' Because the teaching is also false, whatever taught the existence of the thing to be taught and the teacher from that approach is indeed cleared away because that too comes from that approach, so the Bhagavān did not state separately that both of them do not exist. By this, how a bodhisattva should accomplish it and how the mind should be thoroughly controlled is taught. By all this, the profound is taught. ^3-202

###### The Excellence of Mind ^3-4-2-7-7-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-203]]

The excellence of mind is seven distinct aspects: the close placement of mindfulness, manifest perfect awakening, designating the Dharma as having great meaning, designating the instruction as having great meaning, thoroughly apprehending the dharmakāya, not abiding in nirvāṇa and saṃsāra, and perfectly pure abiding. ^3-203

###### Close Placement of Mindfulness ^3-4-2-7-7-1-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-204]]

Here, now, thinking, "In the future time, for those enveloped by the net of bad views, will such a profound Dharma be fruitful?", the retinue lacked confidence; in order to clear away the lack of confidence of whoever thinks like that, the noble Subhūti, with regard to the close placement of mindfulness out of the excellence of mind, asked, "Bhagavān," and so forth. ^3-204

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-205]]

Did not the Bhagavān too previously state, "in the future time, those possessing moral discipline and possessing good qualities will arise," and so forth? Now, in order to teach not to be manifestly attached to them as exclusively existent and non-existent, he did not state that they exist, but in order to refute the two extremes, he stated "Subhūti, they are..." and so forth. Ultimately, because the existence of the nature of aggregates, persons, and so forth is not observed, "they are not sentient beings either"; by this, the extreme of imputation is refuted. Because they thoroughly abide as pleasant when unexamined, "nor are they non-sentient beings"; by this, the extreme of deprecation is refuted. By this, by teaching "they will arise", what the Bhagavān's unmistaken close placement of mindfulness towards sentient beings is like is taught, and it is taught that a bodhisattva should abide by aspiration and prayer in that excellent close placement of mindfulness of the Bhagavān. By this "they are not sentient beings either", meditative absorption in yoga is taught. Having considered one who thinks, "If they are not sentient beings, then how did the Bhagavān state 'in the future time, those possessing moral discipline and possessing good qualities will arise' and so forth?", he asked, "Why is that?" ^3-205

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-206]]

As for "because they were stated as not being sentient beings", ultimately sentient beings indeed do not exist. ^3-206

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-207]]

In reliance upon the existence of the nature of the aggregates abiding conventionally, they are stated; therefore, because they conventionally exist, they are called "sentient beings". If that were not so, if they did not exist even conventionally, they would not be spoken of. By this, thoroughly controlling the mind is taught. By all this, the profound is expressed. Because the Bhagavān taught the close placement of mindfulness as unobstructed towards all sentient beings, the extensive is taught. ^3-207

###### Complete and Perfect Awakening ^3-4-2-7-7-2-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-208]]

If there were no Dharma taught by the Bhagavān whatsoever, then at that time there would also be no manifest perfect awakening; therefore, the effort of bodhisattvas to accomplish the Tathāgata's manifest perfect awakening would become meaningless. ^3-208

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-209]]

For the sake of those who think, "If one wishes to manifestly perfectly awaken to however many dharmas there are, then at that time there is also a teaching," with regard to manifest perfect awakening, he stated, "Subhūti, what do you think of this?" and so forth. The noble Subhūti, grasping the previous intention, replied, "There is not." He thinks like this: "Intending the ultimate, the Bhagavān refuted the teaching." Just as there is no Dharma taught whatsoever ultimately, there is also no manifest perfect awakening; that was taught previously. Conventionally, there is indeed the Dharma taught by the Bhagavān, perfect awakening, and the path which is its cause. Therefore, a bodhisattva should make effort in order to accomplish the correct conventional nature of unexcelled, perfectly complete awakening; by this, how a bodhisattva should abide is taught. Previously, at the time of thoroughly seeking instruction, the false imagination of a bodhisattva who thinks "manifest perfect awakening is ultimate" was removed, whereas now, at the time of thoroughly seeking the level of a Buddha, such false imaginations of others are removed, so it does not incur the fault of repetition. For one who thinks, "What is the unexcelled, perfectly complete awakening posited as the correct conventional like?", he stated, "Even the slightest..." and so forth. ^3-209

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-210]]

The nature of realizing the selflessness of dharmas without remainder, while the Bhagavān, when abiding in whichever exalted wisdom, does not observe even the subtle nature of dharmas whose characteristic is retaining their own characteristics, is unexcelled, perfectly complete awakening. Because it is supreme over whatever knowledges of outsiders, learners, and non-learners, it is "unexcelled". Because it is the nature of comprehending the suchness of all entities just as they are, it is called "perfectly complete awakening"; such is the meaning. Here, as the reason for not observing dharmas, "do not exist" was stated. Because they do not exist, therefore they are not observed; it is not that they are not observed despite existing, because if all dharmas existed, they would become the very things that make known the observation of the Tathāgata's exalted wisdom. By this, the nature of realizing the selflessness of dharmas is taught. Now, in order to teach the nature of realizing the selflessness of persons, he stated, "Furthermore, Subhūti," and so forth. ^3-210

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-211]]

As for "that Dharma is equal", it is because in all dharmas realized by it, the selflessness of persons is equal. ^3-211

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-212]]

As for "there is no inequality whatsoever", it is because it is unmistaken in all respects, because manifest attachment to the entities of the continuum of apprehending a self is abandoned. The Bhagavān has abandoned apprehending a self along with its latencies; therefore, the perfect awakening that is the nature of his very realization of the selflessness of persons is called "unexcelled, perfectly complete awakening", and it does not belong to others. Regarding the thought, "In what way is that Dharma equal?", he stated, "without a sentient being" and so forth. By all this, what is to be accomplished by meditative absorption in yoga is taught. ^3-212

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-213]]

Regarding the thought, "How does he manifestly perfectly awaken?", he stated, "by all wholesome dharmas" and so forth, which means that he manifestly perfectly awakens by wholesome dharmas whose nature is the accumulations of merit and exalted wisdom. ^3-213

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-214]]

Regarding the thought, 'If, because they do not exist, not even the slightest dharma is observed, how would wholesome dharmas exist, since one manifestly perfectly awakens by them?', he stated, 'wholesome dharmas' and so forth; ultimately, dharmas are indeed non-existent. ^3-214

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-215]]

Because they exist conventionally, he stated 'wholesome dharmas'; therefore, there is no fault in saying that one manifestly perfectly awakens by wholesome dharmas. By this, thoroughly holding the mind is taught, because it removes the distraction of apprehending the extremes of superimposition and deprecation. By all these points, the profound is taught. By thoroughly expressing the own nature of perfectly complete awakening, which is the nature of realizing the selflessness of persons and dharmas without remainder, the extensive is also taught. ^3-215

###### Designating the Dharma as a Great Purpose ^3-4-2-7-7-3-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-216]]

Regarding the thought, "How is that perfectly complete awakening of the Bhagavān to be known?", having analyzed, in order to teach its existence by designating the Dharma as having great meaning, he stated, "Furthermore, Subhūti," and so forth. ^3-216

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-217]]

As for "Sumeru, king of mountains," it is an illustration; whatever merit there is from giving having completely filled infinite world systems with precious things, that too simply does not bear comparison. As for "from this prajñāpāramitā," because it does not thoroughly express merely a mark, it illustrates all dharmas of the Great Vehicle. The word "up to" includes "a thousandth part," "a hundred thousandth part," and "even as an example and cause." Thus, by the reason of designating the Dharma as having great meaning, the Bhagavān's consummate mind is known as the unexcelled nature of perfectly complete awakening, because without such a perfectly complete awakening, such a Dharma could not be explained. Therefore, in order to accomplish such a consummate mind through aspiration and prayer, one should abide; by all this, how a bodhisattva should abide is taught, and by this very thing the extensive is also expressed. That perfect awakening is also to be viewed as the nature of the authentic conventional, but not ultimately, because it was explained previously. Although not stated directly, by implication, meditative absorption in yoga, thoroughly holding the mind, and the profound are also taught. ^3-217

###### Designating the Instructions as a Great Purpose ^3-4-2-7-7-4-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-218]]

When the Bhagavān abides in supramundane exalted wisdom, because he is extremely in meditative equipoise, the notion of sentient beings and so forth does not arise at all; that is not a great marvel. When bestowing the transmission of excellent liberation to sentient beings, during the time of not being in meditative equipoise, even when abiding in pure mundane exalted wisdom, the notion of sentient beings and so forth does not arise at all. Teaching that, by the reason of designating the instruction as having great meaning, in order to teach that the unexcelled consummate mind of the Bhagavān is known, having acted in the context of instructing the instruction having great meaning, he stated, "Subhūti, what do you think of this?" and so forth. ^3-218

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-219]]

Having asked, without waiting for an answer, as if having urgency, he himself stated, "Subhūti, do not view it like that," and so forth. Therefore, it is taught that a bodhisattva should abide by aspiration and prayer regarding such a cause of the instruction having great meaning, the consummate mind of the Bhagavān. ^3-219

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-220]]

Regarding the thought, "If sentient beings indeed exist, because they would become the cause of thorough affliction, does the Tathāgata not think that? Or is it because sentient beings are non-existent?" Having analyzed the thought of thinking this, it was asked, "Why is that?", and in order to teach that because any sentient being of the nature of the aggregates and a person is ultimately non-existent, it is not observed like that, he stated, "Because any sentient being released by the Tathāgata does not exist at all." By this, how a bodhisattva should accomplish this by meditative absorption in yoga is taught. Regarding the thought, "How does one know they are non-existent?", he stated, "Subhūti, if..." and so forth. The intention here is this: the Tathāgata's exalted wisdom engages just as the object of knowledge is. If it were not so, because obscurations were not abandoned, how would one become a Tathāgata? Therefore, if sentient beings existed, at that time they would be completely seen by the Tathāgata, because he observes the self directly and therefore has no doubt. That itself would become a tight apprehension of a self for him. Therefore, the binding of the entire net of afflictions arisen from the cause of apprehending a self for him would become slight or extreme; because the obscurations of the extremely tight bindings of attachment and so forth were not abandoned at that time, the Bhagavān would not have attained the state of a Tathāgata. Since the Bhagavān has attained that state, therefore, he has abandoned all obscurations. By abandoning obscurations, all the mountains of apprehending a self are destroyed. Therefore, he does not know sentient beings to be non-existent. ^3-220

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-221]]

Regarding the thought, "Even though it is selfless, is there not the apprehension of a self, that is, the characteristic of the view of a self? How does the Bhagavān not perceive a self and so forth?", he stated, "Subhūti, as for what is called the apprehension of a self..." and so forth. If he were to apprehend any entity of a self and so forth, at that time the Bhagavān, who has abandoned obscurations, would have the apprehension of a self arise in him, but because there is nothing whatsoever to be apprehended as a self, the Tathāgata stated it to be exactly non-apprehension. Regarding the thought, "Then what is it that is called the apprehension of a self?", he stated, "That too..." and so forth. They are "childish" because they do not have hearing and so forth, and do not clearly know what is to be abandoned and what is to be adopted. They are "ordinary beings" because, being without realization, they have become distinct from the noble ones. In order to exclude those ordinary beings who, having listened to authentic treatises and abiding in proper mental engagement, when resolving upon the selflessness of dharmas do not apprehend a self by adhering to it as true, he said "childish". If he had said only "childish", even such ordinary beings who are skilled in the aggregates and so forth are of a childish nature because the path of the noble ones has not arisen in them, so it is in order to teach a distinction of the childish. Even though that apprehension of a self is mistaken, because they are childish, it appears to them like an apprehension of a self; therefore, by the power of their intention, he teaches it as "the apprehension of a self." ^3-221

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-222]]

It does not become true merely by that, but it would absurdly follow: why would one not apprehend a self and so forth as true even through the application of words like "selflessness"? Regarding the thought, "If there is no self, who is called 'ordinary beings'?", he stated, "Childish ordinary beings," and so forth. Ultimately, beings are completely non-existent; because it was stated relying on the conventional, there is no fault. By all this, because the extremes of exaggeration and deprecation are eliminated, thoroughly holding the mind is taught. By all this, the profound itself is expressed directly; teaching [this] as the cause of designating the instruction as having great meaning with respect to the entire realm of sentient beings also teaches the extensive consummate mind. ^3-222

###### Completely Upholding the Dharmakāya ^3-4-2-7-7-5-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-223]]

If childish ordinary beings do not exist, how could there be Tathāgatas, who have become consummate bodies adorned with marks and excellent signs? Regarding the thought, 'Having generated the mind of awakening arisen from the root of compassion, ordinary beings themselves apply themselves to the perfections and become consummate Tathāgata bodies adorned with marks and so forth, but if they do not exist, because there is no root, how could they become such consummate Tathāgata bodies?' — for the sake of those seated in the retinue who had accumulated no small realization but who, by adhering to form and so forth as entities, were thinking in this way, he stated, 'Subhūti, what do you think of this?' and so forth, doing so in terms of thoroughly grasping the dharmakāya. Earlier, a presentation as the dharmakāya was not made; because this was stated in order to present the dharmakāya, it does not become the fault of repetition. Knowing that if investigated ultimately, the consummate marks are unjustified as the nature of having parts or being an accumulation of subtle particles, grasping the very same earlier intention, the noble Subhūti requested, 'That is not so.' The Bhagavān stated another justification for this very point, 'Subhūti, if...' and so forth. ^3-223

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-224]]

If the Tathāgata were differentiated merely by consummate marks, since the reason for expression is the same, at that time why would a wheel-turning king not also become a Tathāgata? If one says, 'But due to abiding in their places, being clear, and being complete, the consummate marks of the Bhagavān are particular, whereas those of a wheel-turning king are not, so therefore he is not called a Tathāgata,' that is also incorrect: even the particulars of abiding in their places and so forth have no difference whatsoever in being of the nature of insentient matter. Therefore, if the Tathāgata were differentiated by dharmas such as marks of the nature of insentient matter, at that time a wheel-turning king would also become a Tathāgata, but that is not so. Therefore, the intention is that the Tathāgata is differentiated merely by having realized the nature of all entities just as they are. By this, it is taught that a bodhisattva should abide by aspiration and prayer regarding such an aspect of the body which has the exalted wisdom of the Tathāgata as its cause. Now, in order to teach that ultimately one should not adhere even to that exalted wisdom of the Tathāgata, he presents the dharmakāya, and teaches how a bodhisattva should accomplish this by meditative absorption in yoga through two verses. ^3-224

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-225]]

Regarding that, by the former verse, how the Tathāgata should not be viewed and by whom he is not seen are taught. ^3-225

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-226]]

If one asks, 'How should he not be viewed?' In conventional designations by seeing and so forth. If one asks, 'By whom is he not seen?' By those who have engaged in abandoning through what is wrong. By this saying, 'see in form,' it is taught that a person who adheres to form conventionally designates seeing what is adorned with marks and so forth as possessing the form of dharmas and persons ultimately. 'Know through sound' means 'I know from sound that the Tathāgata is like this, like that' — this is the meaning of the words. By this, conventional designation as hearing is taught. These two conventional designations are illustrations; by them, differentiating and cognizing are also subsumed. Those beings who have engaged in abandoning through what is wrong do not see me perfectly because they have engaged in a wrong path. Because one abandons by this, it is what makes one abandon; this refers to the path. Because it has become inverted, it is wrong; that also is the cause of not seeing. Regarding the thought, 'Well then, how should a Buddha be viewed?' he stated, 'see the dharmatā,' and so forth. Whatever is the genuine nature of dharmas is called dharmatā; because it is established by reasoning and scripture, it is of the nature of being primordially peaceful and so forth. That itself is expressed by synonyms such as tathatā, dharmadhātu, the authentic limit, the signless, and the ultimate. ^3-226

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-227]]

Because it exists thus at all times, it is tathatā. Because, focusing on it, all the qualities of a Buddha arise, and because it has become their cause, it is the dharmadhātu. Because it is un-inverted, it is authentic; because it has become the limit of that, it is the authentic limit. Because it lacks all signs of entities, such as blue and so forth, it is the signless. Because it is the domain of supreme exalted wisdom, it is the ultimate. Regarding the thought, 'Why should he be viewed as the dharmatā?', he stated, 'dharmakāya.' The word 'kāya' (body) refers to nature. Therefore, the meaning becomes this: those presented as Tathāgatas having authentic exalted wisdom as their cause are ultimately of the nature of dharmas; whatever are the nature of something, those should be viewed as the essence of that; one should not view something as the essence of something else. Regarding the thought, 'If so, the dharmatā would become of the nature of appearance. If not, having investigated, how could one be able to see those things illustrated by it?', he stated, 'dharmatā' and so forth. Regarding the thought, 'Is it not cognized because one is unable, or because if it were thoroughly known, it would be accompanied by a fault?', he stated, 'unable' and so forth. The intention is: 'If the nature of the dharma-possessor were established, one would be able to cognize its dharmatā; but when ultimately there is no dharma-possessor whatsoever established in its own essence, at that time how could one be able to cognize only that dharmatā in its own continuum? It does not exist merely alone.' If one asks, 'If so, since the dharma-possessor Buddhas of the essence of authentic exalted wisdom are also not established ultimately and therefore there is no seeing them, how should they be viewed as the dharmatā?', there is no fault in that: the very fact that one ultimately does not see all dharma-possessors is itself seeing the dharmatā. Since the dharmatā is of the essence of non-arising, how could it be grasped by grasping at arising, which is discordant with it? By grasping at a vase, one will not grasp its non-arising. By this, it is taught: 'The Tathāgata should not be viewed as the form body; he should be viewed as the dharmakāya.' ^3-227

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-228]]

To abandon the doubt of whoever thinks, 'Even though the Tathāgata is not of the nature of perfect marks, it is logical that the defining characteristic of the Tathāgata's unexcelled, perfectly complete awakening arises from the cause of perfect marks, because thus, when he had become a bodhisattva, sign-readers, having seen him as possessing perfect marks, prophesied that the Bhagavān would attain unexcelled, perfectly complete awakening,' he stated, 'Subhūti, what do you think about this?' and so forth. The intention is: 'If perfect marks were to become the cause of unexcelled, perfectly complete awakening, then at that time a wheel-turning monarch would also become so.' Sign-readers, having seen such a kind of mark, prophesied it, but it is not the cause, so there is no fault. By this, thoroughly apprehending the mind is taught. By all these points, profoundness itself is taught. By teaching the defining characteristic of the Tathāgata's exalted wisdom conventionally realizing the nature of all entities without exception, extensiveness is also taught. ^3-228

###### Non-Abiding in Nirvāṇa and Saṃsāra ^3-4-2-7-7-6-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-229]]

If, because all dharmas do not exist ultimately, one is unable to cognize that dharmatā, in that case you would become a propounder of annihilation. ^3-229

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-230]]

Therefore, for the sake of whoever doubts, thinking, 'Because the Tathāgata also does not exist, non-abiding nirvāṇa is also not established,' with respect to not abiding in saṃsāra and nirvāṇa, he stated, 'Subhūti, do you think thus, that those who have authentically entered the bodhisattva vehicle' and so forth. Also, there is no conceptualization that what has arisen is destroyed, because one realizes the dharmatā just as it abides. Therefore, it is explained: 'Here there is nothing whatsoever to be removed, and nothing whatsoever to be posited. One looks authentically at the authentic. Seeing authentically, one is completely liberated.' Also, there is no conceptualization of annihilation by obstructing the arising of future dharmas, because the dharmatā is taught just as it abides. Because all dharmas are accepted as arising in accordance with conditions, being pleasant when unanalyzed, and because the Buddha Bhagavān, the essence of the authentic conventional, who has attained mastery over emanations and so forth by the power of great compassion, is accepted as not cutting off the continuum of engaging in saṃsāra by teaching methods in accordance with those to be tamed, by this, the Bhagavān's not abiding in nirvāṇa is taught; and that one should abide with aspiration and prayer for such a body of the Bhagavān is also taught. Regarding the thought, 'If thus the Bhagavān is accepted as engaging in saṃsāra, he would become of the very nature of saṃsāra,' having investigated it, in order to teach his very non-abiding in saṃsāra, he stated, 'Moreover, Subhūti' and so forth. ^3-230

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-231]]

'Selfless' is because they are under the power of others, like an illusion. They are not of the nature of a self by themselves, because a permanent thing contradicts being a cause since it is neither sequential nor non-sequential, and because what is not a cause does not do anything, making it impossible to be an empowerer. There is also no self empowered by another. Therefore, in both ways, all those dharmas are selfless; just as it is stated, 'Monks, childish ones who lack learning impute and engage with "I" and "mine", but here there is no "I" and "mine".' By this, the patience regarding the selflessness of persons in all dharmas is taught. By 'non-arising', the patience regarding the selflessness of dharmas is taught. Thus, if entities were to arise, would they arise causelessly or from a cause? Furthermore, if from a cause, one might conceptualize whether they arise from a permanent cause or an impermanent cause. In that regard, since entities appear occasionally, the first conceptualization is unsuitable: because that which has no reliance has no distinctions anywhere, it would become either permanently existent or non-existent, just like the times of existence and non-existence. Therefore, precisely because they arise occasionally, the second alternative is also unsuitable: since the capacity of permanent things cannot be differentiated by other assisting factors, and since they do not rely on anything, how could they produce effects sequentially and not simultaneously? It is illogical for an effect whose causes are not incomplete to occasionally not appear; by what reason would it arise sequentially? Those who propound an intrinsic nature as the cause of entities also effectively accept non-existence: thus, what is established by intrinsic nature would become a cause, but what is unestablished would not, because it has the defining characteristic of being devoid of all capacities. ^3-231

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-232]]

If that intrinsic nature of entities is established, it is accepted as being established precisely without a cause; if another cause, distinct from it, is not asserted, how could it not be causeless? Because entities are not other than their intrinsic nature, when that is established, they are established, so they would be causeless. If that were not the case, since they would not be established when that [intrinsic nature] is established, how could they become of a nature that is not distinct from it? Therefore, whatever destroys the position that propounds causelessness applies equally here. Since being simultaneous or non-simultaneous contradicts being a cause, the third alternative is also unsuitable. Thus, if it were to become a cause, it would either be simultaneous with the effect or at a different time. It is not from being simultaneous, because that which has not attained its own identity is empty of all capacities and thus has no engagement with any effect. Its own identity, just like its nature, is precisely the essence of the entity that attains it simultaneously with the effect, so it does not engage with that effect. Furthermore, if they are at different times, it would occur having been separated by another time, for example, just as the Vaibhāṣikas assert the cause of ripening as a cause; or it would occur through meeting, for example, just as the Sautrāntikas say that the destruction and arising of the cause and effect are simultaneous like the rising and falling of the ends of a balance scale. In that regard, if the effect arises having been separated by another time, it would be accepted as having arisen precisely from what is destroyed, but since that is devoid of all capacities, what is destroyed is illogical as a causal entity. ^3-232

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-233]]

If that were not so, how could a destroyed entity that is not an agent become destroyed? As for the position of meeting, if they meet with their entire nature, then at that time they would become simultaneous; if two partless entities meet with their entire nature, there is no other outcome except that they become a single time. Therefore, just as when subtle atoms meet with their entire nature, a mass would become just the size of a subtle atom, similarly it would be difficult to avoid that an eon would become just a single instant. If they meet by one part, they would become possessed of parts. Therefore, all entities are ultimately unarisen; they merely appear as if arising due to being false and pleasant when unanalyzed, similar to the nature of what appears to a mistaken consciousness. Since mere renown is not a valid cognition, how could it be logical for someone to say, 'Because the cause and effect of entities are renowned even up to cowherds, it is illogical to destroy them'? This is because renowned forms and so forth are destroyed by valid cognition. Therefore, the Bhagavān negated the valid cognitions of the eyes and so forth in the Samādhirāja Sūtra: 'The eyes, ears, and nose are also not valid cognitions. The tongue, body, and mind are also not valid cognitions. If these faculties were valid cognitions, what would the noble path do for anyone?' ^3-233

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-234]]

'Thus, these faculties are not valid cognitions; by their intrinsic nature, they are inert and unspecified. Therefore, whoever desires the path of nirvāṇa should perform the activities of the noble path,' so it is said. Whatever characteristics of direct perception the masters have spoken of, they too are direct perception conventionally; they should be seen as engaging with the ultimate. Conventionally, they are not analyzed and debated accordingly; therefore, because they are taught conventionally, they cannot withstand analysis. Therefore, the Bhagavān negated the arising of entities from self and from other in the Śālistamba and so forth. Accordingly, it is also said in the noble Laṅkāvatāra: 'Mahāmati, because they do not arise from existence or non-existence, all phenomena are unarisen.' It is said in the Great Prajñāpāramitā: 'Subhūti, that no phenomenon whatsoever is arisen is called non-arising.' Regarding that, whatever is wisdom is called patience with unarisen phenomena. Accordingly, it is also said in the Ratnākara Sūtra: 'That which has no nature, because it has no nature, how could there be other conditions? Because it has no nature, how could it generate others? That cause has been thoroughly taught by the Sugata.' Furthermore: 'The arising of entities is conventional; ^3-234

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-235]]

ultimately, they have no nature,' so it is also said. If one says, 'All these are spoken with regard to the imagined intrinsic nature,' it is not so, because all intrinsic natures have already been eliminated by valid cognition. By this, it is taught: if even others who possess small roots of virtue do not experience the suffering of saṃsāra, how could a bodhisattva in whom the great light of pristine wisdom has arisen because of having obtained patience with unarisen phenomena, and who has obtained the generation of an unsurpassable heap of merit, experience the suffering of saṃsāra? Thus, one experiences suffering in saṃsāra in two ways: they have the characteristics of physical and mental discomfort. Among those, the former is the effect of non-virtuous karma. The latter is born from ignorance; it is born from wrong understanding. Regarding that, because they have obtained patience with unarisen phenomena, bodhisattvas have neither non-virtue nor ignorance, the causes of suffering. Because the causes are absent, it is impossible for them to be touched by the slightest trace of suffering. By this, how bodhisattvas should practice is taught. Even if they have generated an extremely large heap of merit, would they not simply remain in saṃsāra like sentient beings of the form realm and so forth? To address the doubt of thinking, 'How is it taught that merely by having generated a large heap of merit they do not abide in saṃsāra?', it also says, 'Subhūti,' and so forth. ^3-235

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-236]]

By this, whoever, even though having generated a heap of merit, apprehends the heap of merit through adhering to it in an inverted way, since they have not abandoned the inversions which are the root of all those afflictions, even if they attain a special migration, they are posited as a saṃsāric being. A bodhisattva, having obtained patience with unarisen phenomena, has no adherence to the heap of merit as being true; therefore, because they have abandoned deep ignorance which is the root of all those afflictions, they do not abide in saṃsāra,' thus it is taught. ^3-236

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-237]]

To dispel the conceptualizations of others, the noble Subhūti, as if not having grasped the intention, expresses a contradiction, saying, 'Should it not be grasped?' His thought is this: 'The Bhagavān has said in various sūtras that a bodhisattva should grasp the heap of merit. If here he says it is not to be grasped, how would it not be a contradiction?' To teach the intention by which both were spoken, the Bhagavān also said, 'It is indeed to be grasped.' Whatever was spoken of as 'grasped' is the way of grasping characterized by accomplishment. Here, the characteristic of wrong apprehension is eliminated, so there is no contradiction. Wrong apprehension is apprehending the wrong path. By this, abandoning that conceptualization teaches the thorough holding of the mind. Furthermore, beginning with 'Subhūti, whatever son of noble family or daughter of noble family...', the extensive is taught. By the rest of the preceding, the profound was taught. By all these points, having negated the extremes of permanence and annihilation, the middle path is expressed. Not abiding in saṃsāra and nirvāṇa has been explained. ^3-237

###### Completely Pure Abiding ^3-4-2-7-7-7-0

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-238]]

Completely pure abiding will be explained. That, too, is divided into three aspects: abiding through deportment, abiding by having mastery over the world of the vessel and sentient beings, and abiding by not having afflictions. ^3-238

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-239]]

Regarding that, if the Buddha is to be seen as the dharmakāya and not as the rūpakāya, then how would this not contradict what is well-known from scripture regarding the Bhagavān's four deportments, taking birth, going forth, and so forth? For the sake of those who think thus, having made reference to abiding through deportment, which is the excellence of abiding, it is said, 'Furthermore, Subhūti, if someone...', and so forth. By the two terms 'goes' or 'comes', walking is taught. The remaining ones teach the deportments of standing and so forth in their respective order. The teaching of deportments is merely an illustration; therefore, taking birth and so forth are also to be seen as taught here. Whoever adheres entirely literally to what is fully expressed in the scriptures regarding the Bhagavān's deportments and so forth, because of adhering in an inverted way, does not know the meaning of what the Tathāgata has spoken. The Bhagavān is not differentiated by the form body; he is completely pure pristine wisdom. Because that pristine wisdom is bodiless, going and so forth are impossible for it even conventionally. Because going and so forth are differentiated by the specific states of the aggregate of form, those are spoken of in dependence on the emanation body of the Bhagavān; they are not in dependence on the body of pristine wisdom, because going and so forth are impossible for it conventionally and ultimately. Therefore, it is taught that a bodhisattva should abide with aspiration and prayer toward such an excellence of abiding through deportment. Having considered the thought of those who think, 'If going and so forth are not present in the Tathāgata, how is the term "Tathāgata" taught as if differentiated by going?', it says, 'Why is that?', which should be connected with the statement, 'They do not know the meaning of what I have explained.' ^3-239

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-240]]

Here, in response, it is said, 'Tathāgata,' and so forth. Thinking thus, this expression is not differentiated by going, but is differentiated by realization, because the word 'going' has the meaning of realization. Because he has realized and understood the suchness of entities just as they abide, he is the Tathāgata. Alternatively, because he too has known and understood the suchness of entities just as the previous Buddhas understood it un-invertedly, he is the Tathāgata. Moreover, in correct relative truth it is suitable as the very body of pristine wisdom, because there is no conceptualization regarding anything else. Because that, too, is bodiless, going anywhere and so forth is impossible even conventionally. Ultimately, because it is unarisen, it is utterly impossible. Therefore, because he has understood the nature of entities, he becomes the foremost of the entire world, and being worthy of offerings, he is called an arhat. ^3-240

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-241]]

Because he has perfectly and completely understood, he is called a perfectly and completely awakened Buddha. If it were not so, because the form body is of the nature of insentient matter, he would not be called that; such is the intention. By this, entering evenly into yoga and the thorough holding of the mind are taught. By all these points, the profound is taught. By teaching that, conventionally, in dependence on the emanation body of the Tathāgata, going and so forth are unobstructed in infinite world realms, the extensive is also taught. To teach that whatever is the emanation form body of the Tathāgata and whatever is the mental-nature body are also devoid of the nature of one and many, and thus are false just like a magically emanated Buddha, having made reference to abiding by dismantling the world of the vessel and sentient beings, it is said, 'Furthermore, Subhūti...', and so forth. Previously, it was taught that a bodhisattva on the level of action through zealous application dismantles that, whereas now, by teaching the abiding of the Bhagavān on the level of a Buddha, it does not become repetitious. Here, too, by realizing through two methods that it is devoid of the nature of one and many, selflessness is taught. ^3-241

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-242]]

By the statement, 'Suppose, for example, it were reduced to powder like a collection of infinitesimal particles', having realized that it is devoid of a singular nature, the method of making it subtle is taught; here, too, the explanation is just as before. ^3-242

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-243]]

To someone who thinks, 'Since infinitesimal particles have different directional parts, are they not unestablished? How could they become a collection?', it is asked, 'For what reason?', and in response, 'Bhagavān, if...' is stated. Thinking thus: 'That collection which is analytically divided into infinitesimal particles merely as a method for reversing the apprehension of it as singular, was spoken of by the Bhagavān, but it is not that it ultimately abides. If it were not so, since a collection of infinitesimal particles would be manifestly well-known to everyone, the Bhagavān would therefore not have declared "a collection of infinitesimal particles"; because even without his speaking, it would be well-known as such.' By this, it is taught that one should abide with aspiration and prayer toward such an abiding by dismantling the form of the Tathāgata. ^3-243

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-244]]

Having considered the thought of someone who thinks, 'If the collection does not exist, why did the Bhagavān ask, "Would that collection of infinitesimal particles be large?"', he stated, 'Why is that?'. The words 'that is not a collection' mean that since those infinitesimal particles which abide through the analytical division of directions such as east and north are not established by nature, in reality they are not suitable to be a collection. ^3-244

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-245]]

It is merely spoken in order to dispel the apprehension of it as singular; therefore, it is called a collection of infinitesimal particles. If they do not have different directional parts, infinitesimal particles would not be surrounded by other infinitesimal particles from different directions, such as beyond and near, and above and below. Therefore, they would not become extensive, just like mind and mental factors and so forth. It is not suitable to state that the parts themselves become infinitesimal particles, because the analysis applies equally to them. If you say, 'Even if they do, there is no end,' it is not suitable: if infinitesimal particles become of an endless nature, what have you established? An endless nature is not established as an entity; therefore, our own assertion is established. Since we do not arbitrarily assert the nature of multiple aspects of particles, we state, 'If infinitesimal particles have no end, by what are they established?' Since you accept that whatever you posit as the partless nature of infinitesimal particles abides in different directions such as east and north, you have accepted that they have different directional parts, and therefore, it absurdly follows that they have parts. Therefore, in order to prove the absurd consequence, the basis of the reason is unestablished. It is not that it is inconclusive due to mind and mental factors either, because since they are bodiless they do not abide in directions; they do not abide by directions such as east and north. Therefore, it is taught, 'It is not the eye, it is not in form, nor does it abide between those two; wherever it abides, it is neither existent nor non-existent,' and so enough with absurd consequences. By this, because it teaches the method of making it unapparent having understood that it is devoid of a multiple nature, entering evenly into yoga and the thorough holding of the mind are taught. By the statement, 'That collection of infinitesimal particles would be large', the extensive is taught. By the rest, the profound is taught. ^3-245

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-246]]

Now, making reference to abiding by dismantling the world of sentient beings, he stated, 'that which was spoken of as the trichiliocosm, the great chiliocosm,' and so forth. Since the world of the vessel has already been explained previously, here 'world realm' is asserted to be the world of sentient beings. The mention of 'trichiliocosm, the great chiliocosm' for both indicates infinite world realms. Therefore, the statement, 'the Tathāgata spoke of that as not a realm', is explained just as before, because that world of sentient beings, the nature of the five aggregates, is also devoid of the nature of one and many. By this, it is taught that a bodhisattva should abide with aspiration and prayer toward such an abiding by dismantling the Tathāgata's world of sentient beings. By making it unapparent, how this entering evenly into yoga is to be accomplished is also taught. Now, in order to teach how the mind is to be thoroughly held, it is asked, 'Why is that?', which connects with 'the Tathāgata spoke of that as not a realm'. ^3-246

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-247]]

In response, he stated, 'Bhagavān, if...' and so forth. If the world of sentient beings, just as it is well-known, were ultimately to become the nature of a single coarse entity, then, since the Tathāgata realizes the reality of entities just as they abide, he would apprehend that very thing as a mass. Therefore, since the cause of apprehending a self is merely the apprehension of the nature of the aggregates as a mass, that very thing would become a firm apprehension as a mass for the Tathāgata, implying that he has not abandoned it. But it is not so. Therefore, that apprehension as a mass which the Bhagavān spoke of elsewhere to childish ordinary beings as the cause of apprehending a self ultimately does not exist as an apprehension, because the apprehended mass has no nature whatsoever of either one or many. ^3-247

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-248]]

It is merely spoken conventionally in the nature of what is well-known to childish ordinary beings; therefore, it is called 'apprehension as a mass'. In order to establish that point, the Bhagavān stated, 'the meaning of apprehension as a mass'. ^3-248

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-249]]

What does it teach? It teaches that since what is conventionally called 'apprehension as a mass' is ultimately non-existent, that phenomenon is inexpressible. Having considered the thought, 'If an apprehended mass does not exist in that way, then at that time the apprehension of it does not exist either, so how can one conventionally speak of 'apprehension as a mass'?', he stated, 'that too is grasped by childish ordinary beings'. His thought is that since childish beings, through the power of delusion, apprehend even what does not exist as existent, the convention is designated by the power of their thinking, and so there is no contradiction. Therefore, they are called 'childish ones'; if it were not so, then since the convention would be designated in accordance with the actual entities, how could they become childish ones? Here, how the mind is to be thoroughly held has already been taught. The extensive and the profound are also to be applied here just as before. By this, it is taught that when all entities are ultimately empty of nature, at that time how could the Tathāgata's form body and wisdom body ultimately become a nature and a thought? It is because the root, sentient beings, do not exist. Now, by 'Subhūti, whoever...' and so forth, because one comes to enter evenly into yoga in all situations, the method by which one is to be placed in equipoise in all things is taught. The connection is applied like this: if apprehension as a mass does not exist, then at that time the view of the transitory collection which arises from it as a root is itself non-existent; therefore, considering the thought, 'How did the Bhagavān speak of the view of the transitory collection out of the five views?', for the sake of that he stated, 'Subhūti, whoever...' and so forth. Here, by teaching how it is to be made non-conceptual, who makes it non-conceptual, upon what it is to be made non-conceptual, by what it is made non-conceptual, and what the non-conceptual is, the method of entering evenly into yoga is perfectly taught. Here, starting from 'Subhūti, whoever...' up to 'therefore it is called 'the view of a self'', by this, how it is to be made non-conceptual is taught. How so? The others, the tīrthikas, explain it as a self, but since the Bhagavān posits selflessness in persons, in order to negate a self, he spoke of 'the view of a self'. ^3-249

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-250]]

By dismantling that view of a self, selflessness in phenomena is posited; he stated, 'the Tathāgata spoke of that as not a view'. If one does not apprehend that view of a self as reality itself, when applying oneself in that way, a bodhisattva will enter evenly into yoga; because it is non-conceptual, therefore that is the method of entering into it. ^3-250

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-251]]

By this saying, 'Subhūti, those who have perfectly entered the bodhisattva vehicle in this way', it completely teaches who makes it non-conceptual. By this saying, 'all phenomena', it teaches upon what it is to be made non-conceptual. By this saying, 'should be known, should be seen, should be resolved upon', it states by what it is to be made non-conceptual. If one asks, 'By what is it to be made non-conceptual?', it is by knowing, by seeing, and by resolving. Here, knowing is the prajñā of hearing. Seeing is the prajñā of reflection, because it causes realization. Resolving is the prajñā of meditation; intense desire is intended to be expressed here by the word 'resolving'. That is accomplished by the power of meditation; therefore, the prajñā arising from meditation, at the stage of the aids to penetration, is spoken of by the word 'resolving'. ^3-251

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-252]]

'One should resolve in such a way that one does not abide even in the perception of a phenomenon' teaches what the non-conceptual is. ^3-252

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-253]]

'The Tathāgata spoke of that as not a perception' is stated here as the reason. Because even the very non-existence of anything whatsoever to be perceived is empty of the nature of one and many, ultimately it is not a perception. The Tathāgata, relying on the conventional, spoke in another way. His thought is, 'Furthermore, if one abides knowing it as a false meaning, how will one not conceptualize?' The method of entering evenly into yoga in all situations has already been explained. Not having affliction is also of two aspects: not having the affliction of explaining, and not having the affliction of saṃsāra. ^3-253

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-254]]

Here, having considered the thought, 'If there is no perception of a phenomenon, then at that time, when the Tathāgata teaches the Dharma to sentient beings, if that perception arises, how would he not become afflicted? If, out of compassion, he remains for the sake of sentient beings as long as saṃsāra lasts, how would he not become afflicted by the perception of a phenomenon?', in order to teach that by that very perfection of mind there is no affliction of explaining, and by the verse there is no affliction of saṃsāra, he stated, 'Furthermore, Subhūti...' and so forth. Previously, from another context, by thoroughly teaching the praises of the world systems, the benefits were praised; but now, in the context of not having affliction, by teaching infinite world systems, the benefits are not stated, so it does not become a repetition of words. Here too, the logic is to be stated just as before. 'Having taken up' means reading aloud. 'Or holding' means the words. 'Or reading' means reading a volume and so forth. 'Comprehending' is a synonym for apprehending the meaning. By this, it completely teaches that because this Dharma has great significance, therefore a bodhisattva should unfailingly abide by aspiration and prayer in such a teaching of the Dharma of great significance. ^3-254

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-255]]

Although he indeed explains, in order to teach explaining in such a way that one does not become afflicted, he stated, 'If one asks, how does one perfectly teach?' and so forth. The thought is this: Although he explained the teaching that all phenomena are ultimately unproduced and inexpressible, he did not explain those phenomena by the nature of being inexpressible. That is to say this: It was taught that he teaches in such a way that the person to be trained thinks, 'Because all phenomena are unproduced from the very beginning, they are inexpressible; therefore, they are not to be taught by anyone.' In that way, one teaches without being afflicted, because the meaning is not mistaken. If it were not so, by teaching mistakenly, one would become afflicted. Therefore, at the time when the Tathāgata teaches the Dharma, while he has entered pure mundane wisdom, even though the perception of appearing as a phenomenon arises, because there is no adherence to that as reality itself, it does not become the fault of being afflicted. By this, entering evenly into yoga, thoroughly holding the mind, and profundity are taught. By the former, vastness is taught. ^3-255

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-256]]

Now, the entire body of the prajñāpāramitā is posited, and by the verse, not having the affliction of saṃsāra is taught. By this verse, the four aspects of the characteristics of the conditioned are completely taught: the characteristic of the nature, the characteristic of the object of enjoyment, the characteristic of realizing the drawbacks, and the characteristic of realizing the escape. Here, the nature of the five aggregates, dependent origination which is how entities appear, is called 'the conditioned' because it is produced by the assemblage of causes and conditions. The nature of all those should be seen like a star: just as stars appear at night but do not appear in the daytime, similarly, conditioned things appear if there is the darkness of the fault of ignorance, but since they do not appear when the light of the sun of wisdom has arisen, they should be seen as having a star-like nature. The conditioned, which is of the nature of adherence to the view of person and phenomena, should be seen like a visual aberration because non-existent objects appear. The characteristic of consciousness should be seen like a butter-lamp because it burns depending on the wick of karma and the oil of craving. Thus, by those examples, the characteristic of the nature of the conditioned is completely taught. ^3-256

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-257]]

By the example of an illusion, the characteristic of the object of enjoyment is completely taught, because it appears in a mistaken manner. Here, the two aspects of the characteristic of realizing the drawbacks regarding the nature of the conditioned are: realizing it as impermanent, and realizing it as suffering. By the example of dew, realizing it as impermanent is taught, because it does not remain for a long time. By the example of a water bubble, realizing it as suffering is taught: feeling is like a water bubble, because it was stated, 'Whatever feeling there may be, that is suffering here.' By the three aspects of suffering, all feeling should be known as suffering. Here, the feeling of suffering is suffering by the suffering of suffering. Those conditioning factors that accord with it are also suffering by their very accordance with it. The feeling of happiness is suffering by the suffering of change. Those phenomena that accord with it are also suffering by their very accordance with it. The feeling of neither suffering nor happiness, and those entities that accord with it, are suffering by the suffering of conditioning, by the characteristic of production and destruction. Thus, all conditioned things should be seen as possessing suffering. ^3-257

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-258]]

Here, the characteristic of realizing the escape is realizing person and phenomena as empty. Because one escapes by that object of observation, the Bhagavān stated the two aspects of the object of observation of the escape. As it is also stated in the noble Samādhirāja, 'If one individually analyzes selflessness regarding phenomena, and having analyzed them, if one meditates, that is the cause of obtaining the result of nirvāṇa. Whatever other cause there is, it will not become peaceful.' Thus it is taught. All conditioned things possess the two aspects of selflessness: thus, by the distinction of past, present, and future, the conditioned are of three aspects. Here, those that are past remain merely as a memory; because they lack an inherent nature, they are empty of phenomena and person themselves, and there is no blessing by a self. Therefore, by the example of a dream, realizing their two aspects of selflessness is taught. By the example of lightning, because the present does not possess the nature of a single substance or multiple, and its nature cannot be apprehended, realizing it is taught. The future is of the nature of not having arrived; by the example of a cloud, because its seeds, which are bad states, are generated in the sky-like mind, the two aspects of selflessness are taught. ^3-258

![[bo-kamalasila-tika#^3-259]]

Therefore, by all this, it is completely taught that the bodhisattva should always abide by aspiration and prayer to examine the nature of all conditioned things in that way. By teaching the realization of the escape, entering evenly into yoga and thoroughly holding the mind are taught. By all these points, profundity is taught. In this verse, this is completely taught: since the root of affliction is mistake, if even others whose mental engagement possesses repeated examination of the nature of the conditioned, by thoroughly realizing conditioning factors in that way, do not become afflicted because they are without mistake, then how could the Tathāgata, who has meditated on the nature of all entities as they are, and who is free from the entire net of mistake, become afflicted? Therefore, it should be known that the Tathāgata, having understood all the faults of the conditioned, possessing a mind that is unsullied by the water of the faults of saṃsāra like a lotus, having pledged to guide all wandering beings, making it fruitful, possessing a mind endowed with great compassion, abides as long as the wheel of saṃsāra revolves, with the thought of accomplishing benefit and happiness for wandering beings without a time limit. As it is said, 'In order to make the world happy, he abides in neither saṃsāra nor nirvāṇa.' Also, it is stated, 'He abides on the ground of looking upon all sentient beings.' 'By that sky-like vast merit obtained from my explaining as best as I could in this way that which generates the Munis who abide in the three times, may I become Buddhahood itself that clears away the darkness of the world.'
