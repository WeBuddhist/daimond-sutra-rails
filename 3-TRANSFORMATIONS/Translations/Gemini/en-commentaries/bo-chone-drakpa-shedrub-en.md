# A Commentary on the Diamond Sutra: The Excellent Path to Liberation, the Sun Illuminating the Profound Meaning by Chone Drakpa Shedrub ^0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^0-1]]

The Commentary on the Vajracchedikā, the Sun that Illuminates the Profound Meaning of the Excellent Path for Travelling to Freedom. ^0-1

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^0-2]]

Herein is contained the Commentary on the Vajracchedikā, called the Sun that Illuminates the Profound Meaning of the Excellent Path for Travelling to Freedom. Namo Mañjughoṣāya. I bow to the lord of sages, the lord of clouds, who has risen in the exceedingly vast, undefiled sky of the dharmakāya, is beautiful with the glory of the thunderous roar of profound emptiness, and makes the continuous rain of the two benefits descend upon the fields of the disciples. I respectfully bow at the feet of the noble Subhūti, prophesied as the supreme of those free from afflictions, who, as an arhat manifestation of the stable circle, questions the profound meaning and is skilled in giving all answers. I bow to the spiritual friends who came in succession, such as Nāgārjuna, Āryadeva, and Candrakīrti, who were prophesied by the victorious ones to comment on the profound meaning, as well as the second victorious one, Lobsang, along with his spiritual sons, and so forth. Here, with faith, I shall explain as suitably as possible a commentary on the Prajñāpāramitā in Three Hundred Stanzas, which is renowned as the Vajracchedikā. Since this text primarily explains the meaning of selflessness and contains many repeated statements, and since apart from one Indian commentary there do not appear to be any Tibetan commentaries on it, although it seems difficult to explain exactly as it is, I shall explain it according to the capability of my own mind. This has three parts: the preliminaries, the main part, and the concluding stages. ^0-2

### Translating the Title and Explaining the Meaning ^1-1-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^1-2]]

The first: In the language of India, it is Āryavajra and so forth; when translated into the Tibetan language, ārya is 'noble', vajra is 'vajra', cchedaka is 'cutter', prajñā is 'wisdom', pāra is 'to the other side', ita is 'gone', nāma is 'called', mahā is 'great', yāna is 'vehicle', and sūtra translates as 'sūtra'. Here, regarding the occurrence of pāramitā: by adding the second case ending am between pāra and ita, deleting the i, and applying the ma to the i, through phonetic conjunction it becomes mita. The explanation of the meaning of this title is: just as the vajra in the hand of Śakra cannot be destroyed by other outer entities, and is capable of reducing to dust whatever entity it strikes, such as a rock, so too, the actual prajñāpāramitā, the expressed subject matter of this text, the pristine wisdom that realizes emptiness, cannot be harmed by discordant factors, and it cuts all afflictions and suffering from the root; such is the meaning. ^1-2

### Prologue ^1-3-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^1-4]]

The third, the prologue: "Thus did I, the compiler, hear at one time: the Bhagavān was residing in Śrāvastī, in Prince Jeta's Grove, Anāthapiṇḍada's Park, together as one with a great saṅgha of monks, one thousand two hundred and fifty śrāvakas, and exceedingly many bodhisattvas." Furthermore, among the six great cities in India such as Śrāvastī, this Śrāvastī belonged under the dominion of King Prasenajit, and there was an excellent pleasure grove with good ground called Prince Jeta's. At a time when a few years had passed since the Bhagavān became enlightened, the householder called Anāthapiṇḍada wished to establish a good, large vihāra as a place for the Bhagavān and his retinue to reside continuously; so he gave Prince Jeta many thousands of ounces of gold to cover the grove and bought that Jeta's Grove. Prince Jeta also offered a plot of land near the gateway to the Bhagavān, and in that grove, Anāthapiṇḍada, relying on Śāriputra, had divine and human builders construct an exceptionally exalted park, and when it was finished, the Bhagavān, knowing Prince Jeta's intention, bestowed the name of the vihāra correspondingly. ^1-4

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^1-5]]

This Anāthapiṇḍada was a holy great being who had intentionally taken birth for the sake of being the patron of the Teacher, and was capable of seeing the treasures of jewels in the water and underground, taking them at will, and enjoying them. Then, for the sake of the disciples, in the morning the Bhagavān put on his three dharma robes, took up his alms bowl, and went to the great city of Śrāvastī for the purpose of alms. Having received alms, he later returned and performed the act of partaking of the meal. ^1-5

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^1-6]]

Having abandoned eating the later meal, the afternoon alms, he set down his alms bowl and so forth, washed and cooled his feet, and crossing his legs in the lotus posture upon the seat that had been arranged, he straightened his body and sat, establishing mindfulness with the knowledge that he would teach this text. As for the Bhagavān going for alms, it was not because he himself needed to eat the alms food, but for the sake of the disciples accumulating merit, teaching the dharma, and so forth. As explained in the Suvarṇaprabhāsa, it is impossible for hunger and thirst to occur for a Buddha, and even if he needed to eat, since he himself masters the pristine wisdom of the sky treasury, he could not possibly be destitute of requisites; he is capable of transforming earth, stones, and so forth into gold, silver, and jewels, and he can transform those and inferior foods into food possessing a hundred tastes, and whatever kind of bad food it might be, when partaken of in the Buddha's mouth, it turns into food of perfect taste such as others would not experience; and this is the meaning of what is said in the Abhisamayālaṃkāra: "For him, unpalatable tastes appear as supreme tastes." Previously, when the Teacher demonstrated the manner of needing to eat horse-fodder barley for three months, Ānanda thought, "Although the Teacher was born in a royal caste, now the time to eat horse fodder has come," and while he was sorrowful, the Teacher brought forth a single grain of barley from his mouth, said "Eat this," and gave it to him; when Ānanda ate it, he was satisfied, and since for seven days and nights the notion of needing to eat food did not arise in him, he marvelled. In the Suvarṇaprabhāsa it is explained that although it appears that the Teacher takes and eats alms, in reality he does not eat, and he also has no feces or urine; and in the Acintyaguhyasūtra it is explained that the Tathāgata's body is like a solid block of gold, and his body has no hollows, stomach, large intestine, small intestine, and so forth; thus it accords with reality. ^1-6

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^1-7]]

Then, many monks also approached, that is, went into the presence of the Bhagavān, circumambulated him three times, and sat down to one side, that is, together. ^1-7

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^1-8]]

Not only that, at that time the Elder Subhūti also assembled in that very retinue and was seated. ^1-8

### The Answer ^2-2-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-5]]

Being pleased with what Subhūti had thus asked the Bhagavān, he granted his affirmation, saying "Excellent!" and that it is so, and acknowledged that the Tathāgata benefits and perfectly entrusts the bodhisattvas. ^2-5

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-6]]

For that reason, he said, "Listen well and hold it well in mind without forgetting," and "I will explain to you the three requested above, such as how one should abide." In reply, Subhūti said, "Bhagavān, it is so," and listened as the Bhagavān had instructed, and the Bhagavān spoke these words, explaining to Subhūti: Although this Subhūti merely adopted the guise of a śrāvaka, in reality he appears as an emanation of Mañjuśrī; and when the Teacher taught the prajñāpāramitā, Subhūti himself was appointed as the initial explainer of the prajñāpāramitā sūtra, which has a great underlying intention. Regarding the summary of the text, Ācārya Kamalaśīla explained eighteen topics, from generating bodhicitta, engaging in the pāramitās, and aspiring to attain the form body, up to the Buddha's perfect seeking; and he explains them by correlating the first sixteen of those with the stage of zealous application, the next one with the stage of pure noble resolve, and the eighteenth with the stage of a Buddha. ^2-6

#### Generating the Mind of Awakening ^2-2-1-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-7]]

Here, wishing to explain it somewhat condensed, the first, generating the mind toward awakening, is: 'Subhūti, here one who has entered the bodhisattva vehicle should think thus at the time of initially generating the mind: 'As to however many immeasurable and countless realms of sentient beings there are, when they are subsumed by the categorization of the classes of sentient beings by way of their mode of birth—whether born from eggs, or likewise the four of being born from a womb, from heat and moisture, and miraculously born; sentient beings with form who exist in the desire realm and form realm; those without form who exist in the formless realm; sentient beings with perception other than the sentient beings of Great Fruit and the Peak of Existence; the sentient beings of a part of Great Fruit who are without perception; and the sentient beings born in the Peak of Existence who are without gross perception but not without subtle perception; in short, to whatever extent the realm of sentient beings is imputed by the designation of the name "sentient being"—I shall lead all of them entirely to perfect nirvāṇa in the expanse of non-abiding nirvāṇa that is without any remainder of the two obscurations and the aggregates of suffering.' Thinking thus, one should generate the mind. To summarize the meaning, it is generating the mind for the sake of all those sentient beings attaining the non-abiding nirvāṇa, the dharmakāya of a Buddha, the svabhāvikakāya. Having first brought forth great compassion that wishes to save all sentient beings from whatever applicable forms of the three sufferings, one newly generates the mind that wishes to establish them in ultimate nirvāṇa, and if it is one that has already been generated previously, one makes that generated mind vivid and causes it to increase further. ^2-7

#### The Four Modes of Birth ^2-2-2-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-8]]

Among the four modes of birth, egg-birth exists among humans, nāgas, birds, and so forth, and womb-birth exists among humans, animals, and also hungry ghosts. As for heat-birth, although there are many external entities such as crops and so forth, among humans it is like the Crown-born King (Māndhātā), and it includes most insects that are born in the summer season. Miraculous birth exists among the humans of the first eon, gods, hell-beings, intermediate state beings, asuras, and also animals. As for egg-birth among humans, it is just as it is explained that during the time of the Teacher, from the upāsikā Viśākhā came many eggs, and from those came many sons. The above is the manner of generating relative bodhicitta, and within that there are the two, aspiring mind and engaging mind; I think this primarily intends the stages of accumulation and preparation. For the defining characteristic of generating the mind to be perfectly complete, merely wishing to establish others in buddhahood is not sufficient; a wish for oneself to attain buddhahood is also definitely required. Therefore Maitreya said, "Generating the mind is the wish for perfect, complete awakening for the sake of others," which explains that saying "for the sake of others" is the wish to establish others in nirvāṇa, and saying "the wish for complete awakening" means a wish for oneself to attain complete awakening is required. ^2-8

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-9]]

In order to teach that this generation of the mind must be embraced by the view realizing selflessness, one should wish to entirely establish limitless sentient beings in non-abiding nirvāṇa in that way, and generate the mind thinking, "Even if they are so established, ultimately no sentient being whatsoever will have attained perfect nirvāṇa." Regarding the occurrence of the word "sorrow" here, sorrow is the two, karma and afflictions, as well as suffering; and passing beyond (bzlas/'das) that is nirvāṇa, which is being liberated or having passed beyond karma, afflictions, and suffering. Therefore, saying "passed beyond" (bzlas/'das) comes to mean both nirvāṇa and establishing them in it. This text teaches a concordant ultimate generation of the mind for ordinary beings, and it also teaches the actual ultimate generation of the mind, which exists only in noble ones. ^2-9

#### Explaining the Object of Negation of the View ^2-2-3-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-10]]

Master Kamalaśīla explained a great deal concerning the view in this context, and since this was done for the sake of needing to know the meaning of the subsequent passages of this text being taught in conjunction with the view of emptiness, it is important; yet, fearing that it would become too much here, if I state just an illustration by summarizing it: all phenomena of affliction and purification are posited as existing merely conventionally, but when analyzed by reasoning that analyzes the ultimate, they cannot withstand analysis and their designated referents are not found; and regarding that, since the object of refutation is easy to refute if it is recognized, if I first state what the object of refutation is like: although there are generally many ways of asserting the object of refutation, here I shall explain it according to the system of the Prāsaṅgika Mādhyamaka. Furthermore, as it is stated in a sūtra, "Those are posited by the power of conception"; and in the commentary on the Catuḥśataka, "They exist only through the existence of conception," and so forth; and by the Lord in the Illumination of the Thought, "It is taught that phenomena are posited by the power of conception, so there are many statements that all phenomena are merely designated by conception and posited by the power of conception"; just as it is stated there, an example of all phenomena being designated by conception is: when a speckled rope is situated in a dim place, the mind arises in someone thinking, "This is a snake." At that time, although neither the collection of that rope nor any of its parts is a snake, the snake of thinking "This is a snake" regarding it appears as merely designated by conception. ^2-10

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-11]]

Likewise, although the thought "I" arises in dependence upon these aggregates, neither the collection, nor the continuum, nor any part of the aggregates exists to be posited as the basis of characteristics of "I," and another entity different from the aggregates cannot be apprehended as the basis of characteristics of "I" either; therefore, that "I" is merely designated in dependence upon the aggregates, but is not established by its own intrinsic nature. Thus also it is said in the Noble One's Ratnāvalī: "A person is not earth, not water, not fire, not wind, not space, not consciousness, and not all of them; what person is there other than these?" Saying "not earth" through to "not consciousness" refutes positing each individual part of the six elements of a person as the self of a person, saying "not all of them" refutes positing the collection of the six elements as the self of a person, and the last line refutes a self with an entity different from the elements. Well then, as to how a person, an individual, is posited, from that same text it is said: "Because a person is an assembly of the six elements, it is not perfectly real; just as that is so, likewise each element is also not perfectly real, because it is an assembly." For that reason, other than a person being merely designated upon the assembly of its own six elements, it is not perfectly established. It demonstrates that just as that is so, the elements are also not perfectly established, because they are designated in dependence upon the assembly of their own respective parts. ^2-11

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-12]]

Likewise, one applies this to say that all phenomena of the aggregates and so forth are also not independently established, because they are designated in dependence upon their own respective parts and collections. One's own aggregate of form is designated in dependence upon the five limbs and so forth, and the limbs too are designated in dependence upon the aggregates which possess the parts and their own directional parts, and the secondary limbs such as fingers are also designated in dependence upon their own possessors of parts and their parts, and a vase is designated upon its own lip, base, belly, and so forth, and the lip, base, and so forth are also designated in dependence upon their own parts and the vase which possesses the parts; applying this to forms and so forth, consciousnesses too are designated in dependence upon earlier and later moments and their objects and so forth, and unconditioned phenomena too are designated in dependence upon their own individual bases of designation; this has been explained extensively elsewhere. Therefore, since there is no phenomenon that is not dependently arisen and not dependently designated, existing without being dependently designated is the measure of the object of refutation that is an extreme held by imputation. Thus also it is said in the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā: "Because there is no phenomenon whatsoever that is not dependently arisen, therefore there is no phenomenon whatsoever that is not empty." In short, although the self or person is not found when its designated referent is sought, conventionally agent and action are tenable like an illusion or an emanation; likewise, it is similar for all phenomena too, as it is said in the Saṃcayagāthā: "Just as the self is, so should all sentient beings be known; ^2-12

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-13]]

just as all sentient beings are, so should all phenomena be known"; and from the Samādhirāja too it is said: "Just as you know the perception of self, likewise you should apply the mind to all." Likewise, intending that one must know that phenomena such as generosity too, other than existing as merely conventionally designated, are empty of inherent nature, it is said "one should give generosity without abiding in any entity whatsoever" and so forth. One must know this great essential point: as long as one is not freed from being bound by apprehension of true existence and has not realized the meaning of emptiness, for that long, even if directly accepted by a buddha, one is incapable of attaining liberation; because the Protector Nāgārjuna said: "Those who do not know emptiness do not resort to liberation; those deluded ones will cycle in the prison of existence of the six migrations," and because Āryadeva also explained, "There is no liberation for one who has perception of entities," and there are many others as well. ^2-13

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-14]]

As for the continuation from above: regarding the reason why one must generate the mind thinking that no truly established sentient being whatsoever attains nirvāṇa, "Why is that?"—having called to Subhūti, "if any bodhisattva engages in the perception of a sentient being as truly established as a sentient being, that bodhisattva is not to be called a bodhisattva who realizes selflessness." ^2-14

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-15]]

Why is that? If any bodhisattva engages in the perception of a sentient being as true as a sentient being, or engages in the perception as a living soul, that is, as possessing a living soul, or if the perception of a person as true, the apprehension of true existence, arises and engages, it is because that one is not suitable to be called a bodhisattva who knows the lack of true existence. Regarding what is said in the Madhyamakāvatāra that if the ultimate bodhicitta has not arisen in the mind-stream, one is not called an ultimate bodhisattva, that is the meaning of not being called an ultimate bodhisattva if one has never directly realized emptiness; whereas here, since there are bodhisattvas on the two paths of accumulation and preparation, it is explained from that perspective. The meaning of the word "sentient being" is possessing an intention that is entirely tormented in saṃsāra; and "living soul" is abiding until the completion of the homogenous similar lot of this life; and "person" is expressed by way of the mind-stream being filled with inferior latencies and falling into saṃsāra; and although "self" does not appear here, because it will appear below, "self" has the meaning of the basis for the arising of the apprehension of "I" thinking "I"—as is explained in the Annotated Commentary on the Śikṣāsamuccaya. Although those are synonyms for a person, if it is a person, it is not necessarily pervaded by possessing all those above word meanings, and between a sentient being and a person too, there is a difference in the breadth of their pervasion. ^2-15

#### Training in the Conduct of the Six Perfections ^2-2-4-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-16]]

As for the way of training in conduct after having generated bodhicitta in that way: illustrating with generosity, it shows that one trains in the other perfections as well, so first, regarding generosity: "Moreover, Subhūti, a bodhisattva should give generosity without abiding in entities"; here "entities" means the entity to be given, and it means giving generosity without abiding through clinging to true existence in the three entities of the three spheres—that, the act of giving, and to whom it is given. That is as it is said in Maitreya's Praṇidhāna: "Without abiding in any entities, may I give generosity without miserliness," and "Whoever gives, to whom it is given, and the generosity: the generosity wherein they are not apprehended in any way"; and from the Madhyamakāvatāra: "Generosity empty of given, receiver, and giver is called a supramundane perfection. When attachment to those three arises, it is taught as a mundane perfection"; thus, generosity endowed with the wisdom that directly realizes the three spheres of generosity as lacking true existence is explained as the supramundane perfection of generosity, and generosity not endowed with that is explained as the mundane perfection, the former existing in noble ones and the latter in ordinary beings. If one has not entered the path, even though one has generosity, one does not have the perfection of generosity, and it is similar for the other five too. Striving in giving generosity, for example, is explained as the meaning of "how should one abide", and not clinging to its three spheres as truly existent as the meaning of "how should one practice", and one-pointedly concentrating the mind on the awareness that realizes the lack of true existence as the meaning of "how should the mind be thoroughly controlled", and one should apply this to the other perfections as well. ^2-16

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-17]]

Not only in the three spheres of generosity, but also not abiding by clinging to any other entity whatsoever as truly existent, one should give generosity. ^2-17

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-18]]

Explaining this slightly differentiated, it is said "one should give generosity without abiding through clinging to true existence in form, and in sound, and smell, and taste, and tactile object, and phenomena too," where forms are external forms, the objects of eye-consciousness, and sounds are objects of the ear, smells are objects of the nose, tastes are objects of the tongue, tactile objects are objects of the body, and phenomena are objects of the mental consciousness, which are all phenomena however they are and however many they are. Subhūti, since all phenomena, other than existing as merely conventionally designated, do not have even an atom of inherent establishment, apprehending them as inherently established is to cling perversely, so a bodhisattva must absolutely not abide in a perception that apprehends signs, clinging to them as inherently established. "Apprehending signs" is a synonym for the apprehension of true existence; an example of the great fault of being bound by the apprehension of true existence will be explained below. ^2-18

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-19]]

Although great merit arises from generosity and so forth that is bound by the apprehension of true existence, to show that it is certain that the merit is extraordinarily great if one practices generosity and so forth by way of being free from being bound by the apprehension of true existence: "Why is that? Subhūti, if any bodhisattva gives generosity without abiding, the limit and measure of the greatness of that heap of merit is not easy for others to apprehend, because it is difficult." ^2-19

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-20]]

To show an example for that: it is like the measure of space in the ten directions, east and so forth, not being easy to apprehend; "Subhūti, likewise" and so forth summarizes the meaning shown by the example. ^2-20

#### Teaching that the Form Body is Accomplished from Generosity and so forth ^2-2-5-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-21]]

From the collection of merit of generosity and so forth, the form body of a Buddha is accomplished, and the form body is adorned with the major and minor marks. ^2-21

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-22]]

"Subhūti, what do you think of this?" means: Subhūti, how do you think, that is, consider, about this point? When asked, "The two kinds of form body of a Tathāgata are indeed endowed with the perfection of the major and minor marks, but do you think that one should view, that is, assert, [him] as a Tathāgata merely by there being the perfection of the major and minor marks?", Subhūti replied, "Bhagavān, he is not to be viewed like that." Slightly differentiating it: one does not view [him] as a Tathāgata merely by there being the perfection of the major and minor marks. "Why is that? Because whatever perfection of the major and minor marks of the form body the Tathāgata has spoken of, that itself is spoken of as existing conventionally like an illusion, but ultimately the perfection of the major and minor marks does not exist," he replied. ^2-22

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-23]]

To show that the major and minor marks of the form body are false conventional modes of being that arise from a collection of conditions, like a drawn image, and are not a true nature, the Bhagavān said to Subhūti: "As much as there is the perfection of marks, to that extent there is falsity." If one asks, "If so, what is not false?", as much as the perfections of the major and minor marks lack true existence, to that extent it is not false but true; thus, one should view a Tathāgata as not having truly established marks and signs. ^2-23

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-24]]

This clears away the two extremes regarding the form body, because since the form body and its major and minor marks exist in the manner of being empty as false conventions, it clears away deprecation, and since it is taught that there are no truly established marks and signs, it clears away the extreme of exaggeration. Of those, the former teaches the form body and the latter teaches the dharma body, and principally the essence body. ^2-24

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-25]]

As for asking, "Will there appear in the future sentient beings who believe in and are devoted to this sūtra that explains such dharma and form bodies?", Subhūti asked: "Bhagavān, in the future time, in the final five hundred years, when the holy dharma is thoroughly destroyed," and so forth; in reply, the Bhagavān saying, "Subhūti, do not speak thus," means "Do not ask out of harboring doubts about whether or not such individuals will appear in the future." ^2-25

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-26]]

"Subhūti, even in the future time when the holy dharma will be destroyed, great bodhisattvas endowed with the training in higher ethical discipline, and endowed with the qualities of the training in higher meditative concentration, and endowed with the training in higher wisdom, that is, endowed with the three trainings, will definitely appear; and it is not that those great bodhisattvas have venerated and generated roots of virtue under merely a single Buddha in the past, but I know that they have venerated and generated roots of virtue under many hundreds of thousands of Buddhas." As for what Kamalaśīla calls "the final five hundred", a collection of five hundreds is five hundred, and the "five periods of five hundred" from the explanation "since it is renowned that the Bhagavān's teaching remains for five periods of five hundred" means the teaching remains for two thousand five hundred years. Regarding the measure of the duration of the teaching, there are many different ways of explaining it in the sūtras and commentaries: there are also explanations that the Sage's teaching remains for one thousand, two thousand, two thousand five hundred, and five thousand years, but their intended meanings do not contradict one another. The way they are not contradictory is that some intend the time of fruition and practice, and some intend the time of transmission, and some are evidently spoken from the perspective of the Noble Land. Such bodhisattvas are, for example, the Six Ornaments of Jambudvīpa in the Noble Land and so forth, and in Tibet, for example, Sakya Paṇḍita, Butön, and the Lord and his spiritual sons. ^2-26

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-27]]

At that time, whatever sentient beings will find and generate even a single pure state of mind—let alone much faith through hearing and contemplation—towards the texts that teach the prajñāpāramitā sūtras such as this, those sentient beings are known and seen by the Tathāgata from right now, and those sentient beings will generate and gather an immeasurable aggregate of merit; "knowing" refers to thoughts and "seeing" refers to form and so forth. ^2-27

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-28]]

If one asks the reason, "Why is that?", it is because those bodhisattvas will not engage in manifest conceptions that truly apprehend a self, a sentient being, a living being, and an individual. The meanings of the words "self", "individual", and so forth are as explained above; Kamalaśīla [states], "Apprehending that a self exists by thinking 'I am' is the conception of a self. ^2-28

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-29]]

Apprehending that there is 'mine' is the conception of a sentient being. Thoroughly apprehending that very self for as long as a lifetime lasts is the conception of a living being. Apprehending it as transmigrating again and again into the transmigratory realms is the apprehension of an individual," he explained, and the meaning of apprehending "mine" is slightly different from the former [explanation]. The non-engagement of such coarse conceptions is intended for the occasion of having realized selflessness. ^2-29

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-30]]

Not only do they not engage through adherence to true existence as a self and so forth, they do not engage in the conception of form and so forth being true as conventional phenomena, nor do they engage through adherence to true existence in the conception of them not existing as phenomena; they will not engage in having such conceptions, nor in lacking conceptions. ^2-30

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-31]]

Alternatively, it is also suitable to explain it as meaning that the conception of adherence to true existence does not engage regarding conventional phenomena such as form and so forth, nor even regarding conventional phenomena lacking true existence; the faults that would ensue if conceptions were to engage in that way connect with [the sūtra's statements], "even if the conception of a phenomenon engages," and so forth, and "even if the conception of a phenomenon lacking a self engages," and so forth. ^2-31

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-32]]

Regarding the statement, "Furthermore, one should not wrongly grasp dharma, because one should not grasp non-dharma either," if it is explained that "dharma" means not wrongly grasping the holy dharma of scriptural transmission through adherence to true existence, and "non-dharma" means not grasping what is not the holy dharma, such as non-Buddhist texts and so forth, it appears that this requires investigation. ^2-32

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-33]]

For that reason, the Tathāgata, intending that principle, stated: "This dharma discourse of scriptural transmission is like a raft; just as one knows that a raft is not needed when one has crossed to the other side of the water, for noble ones who have reached the culmination, if even dharmas such as the collections of scriptural transmissions, which are the methods for attaining the result, are to be cast aside and abandoned at the time of attaining the result, let alone casting aside what are not holy dharmas, which is established as a basis." That means that at the time of attaining the result, since the purpose has been accomplished, there is no need for such scriptural transmissions for one's own sake, and that one does not depend on them. ^2-33

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-34]]

Furthermore, when the Bhagavān asked Subhūti, "Do you think that there is any dharma that the Tathāgata has manifestly completely awakened to as unsurpassed, perfectly complete awakening, and any dharma that he has spoken and taught?", Subhūti said in reply, "According to how I understand, that is, comprehend and know, the meaning spoken by the Bhagavān, ultimately there is no dharma whatsoever that has been awakened to and taught." ^2-34

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-35]]

If it is asked what the reason for that is: Even though it is explained that the Tathāgata manifestly realized it himself conventionally and awakened to it manifestly completely, or taught dharma conventionally, ultimately there is nothing that has been awakened to and no dharma whatsoever that has been taught by him, and their reality of emptiness of true existence cannot be grasped by a concordant conventional mind as it exists from the perspective of a noble one's meditative equipoise. It cannot be expressed by words in an unelaborated manner, because ultimately they are not dharma, and conventionally they are not non-dharma, that is, they are not non-existent; for that reason. ^2-35

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-36]]

If it is asked what the reason for that is: he said in reply, "Because noble individuals are distinguished, that is, posited, by having manifestly realized the unconditioned reality, the emptiness of inherent existence of all phenomena." These [statements] are intended for the freedom from elaboration from the perspective of the exalted wisdom of the noble ones' meditative equipoise. Although it is necessary to assert that Tathāgatas have awakened conventionally, regarding their not having awakened ultimately: as stated above, except for being merely imputed in dependence upon their own aggregates, the Tathāgata is neither each individual undefiled aggregate of theirs, nor the collection, nor anything essentially different from those, because the protector Nāgārjuna stated in the twenty-second chapter of the Fundamental [Treatise on] Wisdom: "The Tathāgata is not the aggregates. He is not other than the aggregates. The aggregates are not in him, he is not in them. The Tathāgata does not possess the aggregates. What Tathāgata exists?" and "Without depending on the aggregates, there is no Tathāgata whatsoever." Here, rendering it into the first two lines of the text is for the sake of easy understanding. Thus, although his awakening and having awakened exist conventionally, they do not exist inherently; this is merely an illustration. The way that the dharma taught by him also does not exist inherently is from the Sūtra of Inconceivable Secrets: "The Tathāgata has not spoken even a single syllable. ^2-36

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-37]]

[and] will not speak." The meaning of this is from the twenty-fifth chapter of the Fundamental [Treatise on] Wisdom: "Elaborations are pacified and peaceful, and all apprehensions are pacified; the Buddhas have not taught any dharma whatsoever, to anyone, anywhere." In the commentary on that by Je [Tsongkhapa]: "When the Buddha Bhagavān passes beyond sorrow, where all elaborations are pacified, and dwells in the manner of not abiding in signs regarding the peaceful nature, at that time, because all apprehensions that apprehend signs are pacified and unapprehended, in terms of that, the Buddhas have not taught any thoroughly afflicted or completely purified dharma whatsoever, anywhere in the realms of gods or realms of humans," and "If it is asked, 'If the Buddha did not teach dharma to anyone, how do these various conventional expressions of the scriptures manifest?', he stated, '[Thinking] "The teacher taught dharma to us" arises from the own conceptions of those to be trained who, possessing the sleep of ignorance, are like dreamers dreaming;'" and it also appears in the Noble Sūtra Requested by Gaganagañja, so it is a point that is difficult to realize. As explained here, the meaning of the statement below as well that the Buddha did not teach dharma should be known. In short, "they are not dharma" refutes the extreme of permanence, and "they are not non-dharma" refutes the extreme of annihilation; having abandoned the two extremes of superimposition and deprecation, by [the statement] "noble individuals" and so forth, it is stated that great noble individuals are conventionally posited as manifestly realizing the reality of conditioned phenomena and manifestly actualizing nirvāṇa. ^2-37

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-38]]

Thus, although all phenomena such as having awakened and having taught dharma do not exist ultimately, because they exist conventionally, a great deal of merit indeed arises from giving generosity. Showing that the merit of having engaged in the practice of listening, reflecting, and meditating upon this dharma is exceptionally greater even than that: the Bhagavān [asked] Subhūti, "What do you think of this? If some son of noble family or daughter of noble family..." As it is stated in the Treasury: "A thousand [sets] of the four continents, suns and moons, / Mount Merus, desire realm gods, / and Brahmā worlds / is asserted to be a first-order thousand. / A thousand of those is a second-order thousand, / a middle world system. / A thousand of those is a third-order thousand." ^2-38

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-39]]

Just as it appears [there], he asked whether, having filled this great trichiliocosm with the seven precious substances—gold, silver, crystal, lapis lazuli, emerald, sapphire, and red pearl—and given it as a gift to others, they would generate a great heap of merit. In reply, Subhūti stated that it would be a great heap of merit, and replied, "That heap of merit is posited as existing merely conventionally, like a dream and an illusion; however, that very heap of merit is not established as a heap ultimately, and conventionally the Tathāgata has designated and spoken of it as a 'heap of merit, a heap of merit'." This teaches the manner in which it is necessary to assert that although white and black actions, both those done previously and those to be done later, do not exist because the past has ceased and the future has not currently arisen, they must be asserted to exist in general, and the result must be asserted to arise from their very connection to the mental continuum of the agent, and so forth, making it a somewhat difficult point. The occurrence of [the word] "stanza" is intended to mean that although this is not a stanza in the Tibetan language, it is capable of being arranged as a stanza in the Sanskrit language. "Taking up" refers to holding the words in mind, and it is also permissible to apply it to having the physical volume in hand; "reciting aloud" and "perfectly explaining" refer to reciting the words and properly explaining [them]; and "teaching" is explained as properly teaching the meaning, and this is done from the perspective of what is primary. ^2-39

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-40]]

If one does such things as taking it up in that way, one will generate an incalculable and immeasurable heap of merit that is even greater than that. ^2-40

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-41]]

The reason is: the benefits of giving the gift of dharma are exceptionally greater than giving material gifts, and not only that, but the awakening of a perfectly complete Buddha is obtained and arises from this prajñāpāramitā that realizes emptiness, the meaning expressed in this text, because the Buddha Bhagavāns as well were born from having practiced this. ^2-41

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-42]]

Although it is like that conventionally, ultimately there is nothing to be obtained, because the Tathāgata has stated that "the dharmas of the Buddha", namely the ten powers of the Buddha and so forth, do not exist ultimately, except for being merely imputed conventionally; nevertheless, it means that the dharmas of the Buddha are designated as such and such merely relatively. ^2-42

#### The Eight Noble Śrāvaka Approachers and Abiders ^2-2-6-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-43]]

Teaching that even the results that the noble śrāvakas obtain are obtained having realized selflessness, and cannot be obtained without realizing it: when [the Bhagavān] asked, "Subhūti, does a Stream-Enterer think like this, clinging to it as true, 'I have obtained the result of a Stream-Enterer'?" Subhūti replied, "That is not so." ^2-43

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-44]]

As for the reason: although that Stream-Enterer ultimately has not entered into any object to be entered, because they have entered the stream of the path to liberation conventionally, they are called a 'Stream-Enterer'. Ultimately they have not entered anything, and they have not entered forms, sounds, smells, tastes, tangible objects, and dharmas having apprehended them ultimately. ^2-44

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-45]]

Quoting this context in the Great Commentary that follows, it states, "If a Stream-Enterer, having apprehended the obtainer and the result to be obtained as true, were to think, 'I have obtained stream-entry', that very thing would become their grasping at a self"; and since this teaches that grasping at the person and the result as true is grasping at a self, the first is grasping at the self of persons, and the second is grasping at the self of phenomena. ^2-45

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-46]]

The Stream-Enterer not apprehending it as true and thinking, "I have obtained the result," is done from the perspective of having refuted the object of true grasping, but it does not teach that innate grasping is also non-existent. This is just as it is stated, "By this, the latter ones are also to be understood." The meaning is that it teaches that in order to obtain the true cessation that is the result of separation for the four such as the Stream-Enterer, one must certainly realize emptiness, and it is explained that with regard to that, having realized a lack of true existence, one must refute the conceived object of true grasping. ^2-46

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-47]]

Therefore, having or not having experienced the refutation of the conceived object of true grasping is posited by way of having or not having experienced the realization of a lack of true existence; as long as, when engaging in analyzing whether any basis is truly established or not, one is unable to pass beyond clinging to it as true, for that long one is unable to refute the conceived object of true grasping, and thus one is posited as being bound by true grasping. Likewise, this is just as was stated having applied it to the three—the Once-Returner, the Non-Returner, and the Arhat—and the meaning is just as before. Because there is a pair—the enterer and the one abiding in the result—for each of the four such as the Stream-Enterer, if differentiated individually they are the eight individuals, and if each pair is combined they become the four pairs of beings. Although there are many explanations regarding these four, just as an illustration: the true cessation that is the result of the ascetic practice of a Stream-Enterer is posited by way of having abandoned the three fetters abandoned by seeing; therefore, having obtained such a result, abiding in it is the Stream-Enterer abiding in the result, and striving and entering for the sake of obtaining it is called its enterer; and the three fetters are the view of the transitory collection, holding ethical discipline and observances as supreme, and afflicted doubt. Although there is an innate view of the transitory collection, here it is the artificial one. The result of a Once-Returner is posited from the perspective of having abandoned the sixth degree of desire realm afflictions; abiding in that is its one abiding in the result, and entering by striving for the sake of obtaining it is its enterer. ^2-47

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-48]]

The result of a Non-Returner is posited from the perspective of having abandoned the ninth degree of desire realm afflictions; abiding in that and entering for its sake are its one abiding in the result and its enterer. The result of an Arhat is posited from the perspective of having abandoned the ninth degree of afflictions of the peak of existence; abiding in that result and entering for the sake of obtaining it are the Arhat abiding in the result and its enterer; and it appears that if done in detail, much would need to be explained. The meaning of a Once-Returner is that they still need to take birth one time in the desire realm, and a Non-Returner is that they do not need to take it. Furthermore, there is the way that the result of a Once-Returner is posited by way of having abandoned the five lower fetters, namely the three fetters, longing for sense pleasures, and malice, and the result of a Non-Returner is posited by way of having abandoned a portion of attachment, excitement, pride, and ignorance of the upper two realms; and these are only śrāvakas, and here in the Prāsaṅgika system they are all noble ones, whereas in the system of the Abhidharmasamuccaya there are ordinary beings among the enterers to stream-entry, but all the others are noble ones. In short, those four such as the Stream-Enterer do not cling as true to the obtainer and the result to be obtained, and although they may have innate true grasping, it is not the case that they have not refuted its conceived object, because for all of them the realization of emptiness has gone before. ^2-48

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-49]]

Then Subhūti said, 'Although I am conventionally an Arhat who has abandoned afflictions, I do not apprehend it as true, thinking "I am an Arhat,"' and 'If I were to apprehend it like that, I would also become afflicted, and thus I would not be an Arhat.' ^2-49

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-50]]

He explained, 'I am an Arhat, because the Bhagavān has conventionally prophesied of me: "The son of noble family Subhūti is the supreme of those without afflictions," but because I do not abide in anything ultimately, he does not prophesy me as "abiding without afflictions", because ultimately the object to be abided in, the act of abiding, and the abider are none of them established'; and this accords with the Prāsaṅgikas asserting true grasping as an affliction. ^2-50

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-51]]

Teaching that not only do the object to be obtained, the act of obtaining, and the obtainer not exist ultimately, but ultimately there is also no dharma to be taken up: when the Bhagavān asked Subhūti, 'Do you think there is any dharma that I, the Tathāgata, ultimately took up from the Buddha Dīpaṃkara in the past?', Subhūti replied, 'There is not.' Specifically mentioning the name of the Buddha Dīpaṃkara here is for the sake of remembering his kindness, because in the past when the Teacher became the youth Dharmamegha, in dependence on being blessed by the Buddha Dīpaṃkara he obtained the great acceptance that dharmas are non-arising, and upon actualizing the eighth ground, Dīpaṃkara prophesied to him, 'In the future time you will become the Buddha named Śākyamuni'; and this will be explained below. As for obtaining the great acceptance that dharmas are non-arising: because, having abandoned afflictions, one has obtained mastery in habituating to the non-conceptual pristine awareness that directly realizes the entire dharmatā, which is all dharmas being empty by nature, therefore, not only during the state of meditative equipoise, but also during post-meditation, all conditioned dharmas will appear exclusively as empty of true existence, like illusions. Because, through obtaining the acceptance that dharmas are non-arising, he directly saw all dharmas as lacking true existence, and saw whatever was prophesied, the result to be obtained, and becoming a Buddha all to be empty by nature, he had no clinging, thinking that he had taken up any truly established dharma from Dīpaṃkara. ^2-51

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-52]]

Furthermore, at the time of prophesying, the Buddha who is prophesied does not exist, and the person at the time of prophesying also does not exist at the time of having become a Buddha after being prophesied, but conventionally the persons from being prophesied to become a Buddha up to having become a Buddha are one continuum, and without differentiating the "I"s of past and future times, there is a general "I" that follows all the past and future "I"s, so with this in mind he prophesied: "You will become that Buddha in the future." For example, although the "I"s of past and future lives and of the early and later parts of this life are not this "I" of the present time, one is able to conventionally designate "I did it" for what they did, and "I will do it" for what they will do. Likewise, it is like when saying "I will make such and such a house, hat, clothes, shoes, and so forth", although the house and so forth do not exist at the time of speaking, one speaks thinking of what will occur in the future; so although it occurs merely conventionally, it does not arise by nature. If it were to arise by nature, one would have to assert that the house and so forth exist even at the time of thinking "I will make a house and so forth", but they do not. Intending such, the Sūtra Requested by Anavatapta states: "Whatever is born from conditions is unborn; it does not have the nature of birth. Whatever depends on conditions is explained to be empty; whoever knows emptiness is conscientious." And the previously explained reasonings establishing the lack of true existence should be applied here as well. If one understands well the way that although results arise from causes they do not arise by nature, one will understand the way that agents and actions are viable in the Mādhyamika system of emptiness of nature, and one will also find the Mādhyamika path of the union of appearance and emptiness. ^2-52

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-53]]

Teaching that in order to thus become a Buddha one needs to accomplish the pure field where one will become a Buddha: the Bhagavān said to Subhūti, "If any bodhisattva thinks and says with true grasping, 'I will ultimately accomplish the arrays of the field,' that statement speaks what is untrue." ^2-53

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-54]]

The reason for that is because the Tathāgata has taught that the perfect arrays of the field that is the place of becoming a Buddha are illusory arrays that have arisen from the gathering of multiple causes and conditions, but ultimately those arrays do not exist; however, since they exist to the perspective of an unexamined and unanalyzed mind, they are conventionally called "the arrays of the field." Not only Buddha fields, but all arrays of the environment and its inhabitants are designated upon the gathering of multiple parts, like a house and so forth. If all of them are broken down by reasoning down to the smallest particle, they will become nothing whatsoever; this is merely an illustration. ^2-54

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-55]]

Therefore, when a bodhisattva is to accomplish the array of a field, they should generate the mind without abiding with true grasping like this. ^2-55

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-56]]

They should generate the mind without abiding in anything ultimately and without abiding with true grasping in any dharma of form, sound, and so forth. ^2-56

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-57]]

Teaching that not only is the array of the outer field like that, but the array of the body of the persons who are its inhabitants is also merely a gathering of conditions and not ultimately established: when he asked, "Subhūti, for example, if a person's body became as large as the king of mountains, Sumeru, do you think that body would be large?", he replied that that body would be large; and the Tathāgata taught that that body itself exists merely as a conventionality, an aggregate of multiple gathered parts, but does not exist as an entity, i.e., an essence, that does not depend on parts; so in worldly convention that body is called "large." Therefore, that conventional body, which is not ultimately established as a body, is called a "body." By the illustration of a large body, all large and small forms should be applied similarly. Speaking like that explains the way to meditate that the entire environment and inhabitants are empty by nature. ^2-57

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-58]]

The example for teaching the benefits of meditating like that: when the Bhagavān asked Subhūti, "If there were exactly as many Gaṅgā rivers as there are grains of sand in the Gaṅgā river, would the grains of sand of those be many in number?", he replied, "If those Gaṅgā rivers themselves are many, what need is there to mention that their grains of sand are many, so there is no need to state it." Then the Bhagavān said to Subhūti, "You should generate inclination, i.e., desire, and you should comprehend that meaning." ^2-58

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-59]]

In reply to the question, "If any man or woman were to fill world systems equal in number to the grains of sand of those Gaṅgā rivers with the seven precious substances like gold, silver, and so forth as explained before, and give them as an offering to the Tathāgatas, would their merit be many?", he replied that it would be very many. Regarding what is called the "Gaṅgā river", there appear to be two ways: asserting it to be the Gaṅgā river flowing from Lake Anavatapta, and asserting it to be the great ocean; and regarding the "sand", there also appear to be two ways: taking it as the universally known sand, and taking it as the particles of water drops. ^2-59

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-60]]

Regarding the statement that the benefits of reciting this text, listening to it, thinking about it, and meditating on it are exceptionally greater than the explained example: "Whoever... from this dharma discourse, even at a minimum" means at the very least. The rest is as before. ^2-60

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-61]]

Teaching the enumeration with "Moreover, Subhūti" and so forth: not only do such benefits arise from reciting and so forth, but the location or place where one recites this text well, teaches its meaning, and practices it becomes an object of offering for others; so if that is the case, what need is there to mention that the person who takes the words of this text to mind and retains them, carries a volume of it, reads it, comprehends—i.e., knows—all its meaning, and takes the meaning to mind by practicing it properly will become endowed with supreme wonder? In that place dwells either the Teacher Buddha or a guru who is a holy being, whichever it may be. ^2-61

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-62]]

Then when Subhūti asked, "What is the name of this text?", the Bhagavān replied, "The name of this text is the prajñāpāramitā, so bear its name like that"; this designates the expressing text with the name of what is expressed, because it teaches by taking the prajñāpāramitā of the expressed meaning as what is to be expressed. By illustrating with the name of the prajñāpāramitā, this also teaches the other pāramitās. ^2-62

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-63]]

That very prajñāpāramitā which the Tathāgata taught is not ultimately established as a pāramitā; therefore, it is conventionally designated as the "prajñāpāramitā." All such explanations of not existing ultimately but existing conventionally teach the Mādhyamika path of the union of the two truths that has abandoned the two extremes; that also teaches that both being empty of nature and existing conventionally gather simultaneously upon a single basis. The meaning of the term "pāramitā" is both causing one to go to the other shore of the ocean of saṃsāra and having already gone to it; according to the former, it exists on the path of training, and the latter exists on the ground of a Buddha, which is synonymous with omniscience. Merely regarding what is expressed by the term "prajñāpāramitā", there are many, such as the prajñāpāramitā of nature, of text, of path, and of result, so although one needs to know the distinctions, they are not stated here. In short, this prajñāpāramitā—where the exalted wisdom realizing emptiness that is embraced by bodhicitta is taught as the prajñāpāramitā—is the special method that leads the first five pāramitās, such as the pāramitā of generosity, to the ground of a Buddha; this will be explained. ^2-63

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-64]]

When it is explained that the prajñāpāramitā is taught by the phrase, "That which is taught as the prajñāpāramitā here", if one thinks, "Well then, is there another dharma that was ultimately taught by the Tathāgata?", teaching that there is not: when asked, "Do you think there is any such dharma?", Subhūti offered the reply, "Any dharma whatsoever that the Tathāgata has taught does not ultimately exist", which is similar to the earlier explanation of the meaning of the statement that the Tathāgata has not taught a dharma. ^2-64

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-65]]

Then, teaching that subtle particles and world systems accumulated from subtle particles are not ultimately established: when the Bhagavān asked Subhūti, "Are however many particles of earth there are in a three-thousandfold world system many?", in reply he stated, "They are many," and "Those subtle particles of earth, whatever they are, the Tathāgata has taught that they are not ultimately established as particles; since they do not exist ultimately but exist conventionally, they are therefore called 'particles of earth'." ^2-65

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-66]]

Therefore, as for whatever world systems accumulated from subtle particles there are, that is because the Tathāgata has taught that they too are not ultimately established as world systems; therefore, they are conventionally called "world systems." ^2-66

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-67]]

As for merely a fraction of the reasoning that they are not ultimately established: world systems accumulated from subtle particles are merely designated upon the assembly of subtle particles, but there is no separate thing called a "world system" as the individual subtle particles, their collection, or an entity distinct from them; for example, just as a vase is designated upon the collection of the vase's lip, base, belly, and so forth. Does a single subtle particle have the six directions of the four cardinal directions and above and below, or not? If it does, then because it would be larger than its own six directional parts, it would lack the meaning of being "subtle"; if it does not have those six directions, then because it lacks the capacity to occupy space, it would absurdly follow that the particle is not physical and it would also become a non-entity. Therefore, since a single subtle particle cannot exist in isolation, it must depend on collecting and assembling with others, so a subtle particle is not independently established; and because it is not established, it is said that the gross particles accumulated from it are also not independently established. In short, it is explained to meditate on emptiness by understanding that subtle particles do not truly exist as a singular nature, and world systems are not truly established as the nature of a multiple collection. Master Kamalaśīla explained that the world system in this context is intended as the world of sentient beings: the impure world system that is the basis where sentient beings dwell. ^2-67

#### The Marks and Signs ^2-2-7-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-68]]

Then, as for [the teaching] that not only world systems, but also the form bodies of a Buddha and their marks and signs are not ultimately established: when the Bhagavān asked Subhūti, "Do you think that the Tathāgata should be seen—that is, apprehended—as merely a body that is the collection of those thirty-two marks of a great being of the Tathāgata?", Subhūti replied, "That is not so. The reason is that whatever thirty-two marks of a great being of the Buddha were taught by the Tathāgata to exist conventionally, those were taught as not ultimately existing as marks," which refutes the extreme of permanence of superimposition, and by the phrase, "therefore they are conventionally called 'marks of a great being'," the extreme of nihilism of deprecation is refuted; this is similar for others as well. By the force of teaching the marks, the eighty signs are also taught, because the signs are the retinue of the marks. The thirty-two marks are as taught in the Abhisamayālaṃkāra: "The marks of wheels on the hands and feet, and feet like a tortoise," and so forth; and the eighty signs are as taught by "The Sage's nails are copper-colored," and so forth. If one thinks, upon seeing that the marks and signs are clear and complete on the two form bodies of a Buddha, through attachment to true existence, "Is the Buddha posited merely by these marks and signs?", that is not so, because the two form bodies of a Buddha are called the Tathāgata by way of having actualized the dharmakāya, but they are not posited merely by way of obtaining a body possessing the marks and signs. For, as taught in the Aṣṭasāhasrikā: "By finding a form body, one is not counted as being called a Tathāgata; rather, by finding omniscience of all aspects, one is counted as being called a Tathāgata." Seeing a form body adorned with the marks and signs of a body and paying homage to it and so forth with attachment to its true existence is indeed subsumed by the collection of merit of the excellent path, but it is flawed by being bound by apprehending true existence, as will be explained in the context of "Those who see me as form," and so forth; because it is also explained in the Abhisamayālaṃkāra in the context of "Attachment to the Jinas and so forth is subtle," that such a manner has the flaw of attachment to true existence. In this context, apprehending true existence must be asserted as ignorance, and it is explained in many texts that if one accumulates virtuous karma motivated by ignorance, one accomplishes higher realms subsumed by saṃsāra, and if one accumulates non-virtuous karma, one accomplishes the bodies of bad migrations. #### The Great Benefits of Knowing and Meditating on the Empty Nature of All Phenomena ^2-2-8-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-69]]

Showing that if one knows and meditates on all phenomena as empty of nature, the benefits are great: the Bhagavān said to Subhūti that even more than whatever person were to give away their own bodies as gifts in number equal to the sand grains of the river Gaṅgā, if one were to apprehend merely a four-line stanza from this dharma discourse, and teach it to others and meditate on it, the merit is exceedingly greater. Although this also intends the arising of benefits like those stated by Āryadeva: "Those with small merit will not even have doubts about this dharma; even just having entertained a doubt will tear cyclic existence to shreds", yet in order that future disciples of inferior intellect who possess faith may generate enthusiasm for the benefits and engage in the text through hearing and contemplating, it is stated in the manner of praise that if one merely apprehends and recites a four-line stanza, such vast benefits will arise; and the actual intended meaning is that from the prajñāpāramitā of the meaning thus taught, one will completely obtain such benefits, but from merely reading the text, one will not completely obtain them, aside from a fraction of such benefits. This principle must also be known in other such contexts. ^2-69

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-70]]

When that was thus said, Subhūti, having heard this profound dharma, by the powerful force of generating wonder, shed tears from his eyes; then, that Subhūti, having wiped away his tears, said to the Bhagavān: "It is a great wonder that this dharma of the prajñāpāramitā has been spoken by all the Tathāgatas however many have come and abide." ^2-70

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-71]]

"I, Subhūti, since the time noble wisdom was born in myself, have not heard this dharma discourse; not only that, but I have never heard this before." This does not mean that Subhūti had not heard such things before, but rather it shows that such a profound dharma of the prajñāpāramitā is difficult for others to hear. ^2-71

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-72]]

As for the manner of being a great wonder: those sentient beings who, with respect to the words and meaning of this sūtra being taught, will generate the right perception free from attachment to true existence—those sentient beings, because they place predispositions for liberation and lay hold of a great cause for obtaining Buddhahood, will be endowed with a supreme great wonder. ^2-72

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-73]]

Whatever is explained as "the right perception" is because of the absence of the perception of being attached to true existence; therefore, the Tathāgata has taught that the perception which is without attachment to true existence and realizes the lack of true existence is called "the right perception". ^2-73

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-74]]

"Bhagavān, that I have generated conviction having examined and known with faith the meaning of this dharma being taught is not a great wonder for me; but in future times, those sentient beings who are convinced of the meaning of emptiness and take up this dharma and so forth, they will be endowed with a supreme great wonder." This way is a great wonder, just as the Venerable Lord Nāgārjuna said: "Whoever, having known this emptiness of phenomena, relies on karma and results—this is more wonderful than the wonderful! This is more marvelous than the marvelous!" The meaning of the rest has been explained previously, and regarding "dri ba", if it appears as "da dri", it connects with asking about another meaning, and if it appears as "ba bri", it connects with writing in letters; however, according to what appears in the Madhyāntavibhāga, "Writing letters, worshipping, giving," it is the latter. These texts teach the manner of how to abide, practice, and apprehend. ^2-74

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-75]]

In future times, for those sentient beings who engage in such a text through hearing, contemplating, and meditating, a gross perception of a self of persons will not engage; and similarly, the various perceptions of true attachment to a sentient being, a life-principle, and a person will not engage, that is, will not arise. ^2-75

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-76]]

When he asked, "The reason is that such a perception of a self and so forth engages erroneously in the object of attachment, and therefore it is itself devoid of all right perceptions, or because those perceptions are devoid of being established as perceptions ultimately," the Bhagavān said to Subhūti, "It is so," and the reason is: "In the future, those sentient beings who, when hearing the words and meaning of this sūtra being taught, are not terrified, do not tremble, and do not become exceedingly frightened in their minds, thinking, 'If everything lacks true existence in that way, actions and agents such as karma and results would be untenable and would become non-existent,' but instead believe and generate conviction, those will be endowed with a supreme wonder." If things were established truly, they would be unsuitable to change, and thus all actions and agents such as karma and results would be unsuitable to be posited and would be untenable; whereas the way in which all actions and agents and nirvāṇa and so forth are tenable with respect to what lacks true existence or is not established by its own nature is just as it is said in the Lord's Praise of Dependent Arising: "Because nature cannot be reversed, if phenomena had some nature, nirvāṇa would be impossible, and it is taught that all elaborations would be irreversible," and, "Being empty of nature and the tenability of actions and agents do not contradict each other; when one sees the reverse of that, action is impossible in what is empty." ^2-76

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-77]]

The reason for being a great wonder is: "This supreme holy prajñāpāramitā text, which teaches that all phenomena are not established by their own nature, has now been spoken by me, the Tathāgata, and because it has been spoken by measureless previous Buddha Bhagavāns." That was spoken in order to apprehend the aforementioned explanation that the phenomena of causes and results are without their own nature as a basis for belief. ^2-77

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-78]]

For that reason, it is called the holy prajñāpāramitā. ^2-78

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-79]]

Then, explaining specifically the perfection of patience: "Subhūti, whatever is called the perfection of patience, that perfection itself lacks ultimate existence, so it is unsuitable to be apprehended with attachment to true existence regarding the three spheres of by whom one is patient, how or what is endured, and toward whom one is patient. The reason is, Subhūti, at a previous time when the King of Kaliṅga generated an evil suspicion thinking that I was involved with his women, and severed my major limbs, minor limbs, and fingers with a sword, at that time, because I meditated on patience having known the three spheres of patience to lack true existence, focusing on the conventional 'I', perceptions ranging from the perception of attachment to a true self thinking 'I am' up to the perception of attachment to a true person did not arise, and at that time I had no such perception of attachment to true existence whatsoever; yet it was not that there were no other perceptions conventionally, because there were the perceptions of accepting suffering thinking 'I am patient', and of being unperturbed by harm, and the perception of certainty regarding phenomena realized as lacking true existence"—so he intended. ^2-79

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-80]]

The reason is: "If at that time I had the perception of an ultimate self thinking 'I am', and so forth, then at that time the perception of malicious intent toward others would also have arisen, but it did not arise," so it was said. ^2-80

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-81]]

Not only that, I directly knew and cognized it: in the past, during five hundred lifetimes when I became a sage called 'Preacher of Patience', when, depending on such conditions, others severed my limbs and so forth, perceptions of a self and so forth did not arise. If one thinks, 'If the "I" at the time of the limbs being severed was not the Teacher, and the Teacher was also not the "I" of that time, how is it tenable for the Teacher to speak of "my limbs"?', it is indeed true, but if the answer to this were explained in detail it would become voluminous, so in brief, it was intended in terms of being the same continuum, and the way in which this is faultless has been somewhat explained above and will be explained. The arising of malicious intent and so forth if there is attachment to a self thinking 'I am' is just as explained in the Pramāṇavārttika: 'If there is a self, one perceives an other. From the aspects of self and other come grasping and hatred; by being fully connected with these, all faults will arise.' ^2-81

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-82]]

Because I became a Buddha by the power of such patience, for that reason, bodhisattvas should completely abandon such a perception of a self, generate the mind for unsurpassed awakening, and make it increase further. Not only should they not abide through attachment to true existence in the three spheres of patience, they also should not abide in that way in forms, sounds, and so forth, and they should not abide through attachment to true existence in whatever conventional phenomena, nor should they abide through attachment to true existence in the ultimate, which is conventional phenomena's lack of true existence. ^2-82

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-83]]

The reason is: 'Whatever objects such as forms are the objects of abiding with attachment to forms, sounds, and so forth, those themselves do not abide ultimately, that is, they are not established,' so it was said. ^2-83

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-84]]

Therefore, the Tathāgata said, 'A bodhisattva should give gifts without ultimately abiding in anything'; and illustrating by that, he intended that this be applied to others such as ethical discipline as well. Bodhisattvas should completely give gifts, guard ethical discipline, and so forth in this way, without attachment to true existence. ^2-84

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-85]]

That perception of true existence as a sentient being itself also lacks perception ultimately, and those very sentient beings who were spoken of conventionally as 'whatever sentient beings' also lack existence by their own nature as sentient beings. ^2-85

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-86]]

The reason is: 'Subhūti, the Tathāgata thus explaining phenomena as empty of true existence did not speak falsely, because the Tathāgata speaks perfectly without error, speaks the truth that does not change into another aspect, and is a speaker of suchness exactly as the abiding mode of phenomena is, and is not a speaker of falsity.' ^2-86

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-87]]

'Subhūti, whatever phenomena of attainment the Tathāgata has awakened to or taught, among those, true ultimate phenomena are not established truly, and false conventional phenomena are also not established truly,' so it was said. Kamalaśīla seems to have explained that conventionalities, because they do not exist in the nature of the rational consciousness inquiring into the ultimate, are untrue and false; and this text also dispels the two extremes. ^2-87

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-88]]

Teaching that there is a difference in the function of giving and so forth due to realizing or not realizing the lack of true existence: 'It is like this: for example, just as a person endowed with eyes entering a great darkness would not clearly see any entities of forms as objects, similarly, those bodhisattvas who give gifts having fallen into the abyss of the view of manifest attachment, bound by apprehending true existence without directing their minds toward the lack of true existence, should be viewed as bodhisattvas similar to that example.' ^2-88

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-89]]

'Furthermore, it is like this: just as when dawn breaks and the sun rises, a person endowed with eyes sees various aspects of good and bad objects of form, similarly, those bodhisattvas who completely give gifts without falling into entities bound by apprehending true existence should be viewed as similar to that example.' Thus, giving bound by apprehending true existence was explained as being similar to a person having entered darkness and not seeing forms, and giving with realization of the lack of true existence was explained as being similar to someone endowed with eyes seeing forms; and furthermore, the five, giving and so forth, when not embraced by the wisdom realizing the lack of true existence, are like someone blind from birth, and the perfection of wisdom realizing the lack of true existence is like someone endowed with eyes leading the blind. The Sañcaya states: "How could ten million, one hundred billion people blind from birth, without a guide, who do not even know the way, enter the city? Without wisdom, these five blind perfections, being without a guide, are unable to touch awakening." And following what was thus stated, to teach in connection with the former that if those first five perfections are not embraced by the perfection of wisdom realizing the lack of true existence, they are like being without eyes and are unable to travel to the ground of a Buddha, and if they are embraced, they are like being endowed with eyes and are able to travel there, it states: "When they are thoroughly embraced by wisdom, then they gain eyes and obtain this name." And the glorious Candrakīrti also stated in the Madhyamakāvatāra: "Just as a single person endowed with eyes easily leads an entire assembly of blind people to their desired destination, similarly here too, intelligence takes the virtues whose eyes are impaired and goes to victoriousness itself." ^2-89

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-90]]

Then the Bhagavan, stating 'whatever individuals who in the present and future times will memorize, retain, and so forth this text, the Tathāgata knows and sees them right from now, and all those sentient beings will generate an immeasurable aggregate of merit,' is the Buddha keeping those sentient beings in mind and generating joy by saying 'the merit is great.' ^2-90

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-91]]

Furthermore, he said, 'If compared to the benefits of giving away bodies illustrated by giving the body in the morning and afternoon times and for many eons in numbers equalling the grains of sand in the river Gaṅgā, the aggregate of merit of whoever hears this text completely, is inspired by it, and does not abandon it mentally is even greater than that former one, what need is there to say that if one does things such as writing this text in letters, the merit will be greater than that?' And moreover, from taking it up through to perfectly teaching it, the latter will have greater merit, that is, benefits, than the former. ^2-91

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-92]]

The praise is: Because the meaning of this dharma discourse is profound and difficult to realize, it is inconceivable and there are no others equal to it that are comparable, and one should cognize, that is, know, that the fruition, the full ripening of having done hearing, thinking, and meditating on it, is also vast and precisely inconceivable. ^2-92

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-93]]

This dharma was not principally spoken for the sake of those who are inclined toward the inferior vehicle, but rather the Tathāgata spoke it for the purpose of sentient beings, bodhisattvas, who have entered the path of the great vehicle, the supreme vehicle and the most excellent vehicle. Both the supreme vehicle and the most excellent vehicle are synonyms for the great vehicle. ^2-93

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-94]]

Whatever such sentient beings of the great vehicle take up and retain this dharma and so forth, the Tathāgata knows and sees them right from now, and those sentient beings will become endowed with an aggregate of merit that is immeasurable, conceptually inconceivable, unequaled, measureless, and limitless, and all those sentient beings will also swiftly obtain the state of awakening of me, the Tathāgata, as if bearing it upon their shoulders. ^2-94

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-95]]

The reason is: those who are inclined toward the inferior dharma are unable to listen to this profound dharma discourse by way of hearing, thinking, and meditating on it, for those who view a self of persons and view a sentient being, a life force, and a person with an attachment to true existence, and individuals who assert them, like non-Buddhists, are unable to do things such as listen to and take up the words and meaning of this text, because there is no basis or opportunity to do so, because such sentient beings do not desire the meaning of selflessness. ^2-95

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-96]]

Furthermore, whatever place this sūtra is taught in, that place will become worthy of being venerated, paid homage to, and circumambulated by the sentient beings of the world including gods, humans, and asuras; that place will become like a stūpa. This is similar to how the root extensive, middling, and condensed Mother sūtras and their commentaries, corresponding to the Abhisamayālaṃkāra stating, 'Awakening itself is the veneration of the support,' state that the place where a bodhisattva on the path of meditation resides is a place revered by others, like a stūpa; and principally, it refers to the place where an individual resides in whose continuum the extraordinary perfection of wisdom, the meaning expressed by this text, has arisen. Although something similar to this appeared above, it was done in terms of individual bases. ^2-96

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-97]]

Furthermore, Subhūti, other than the former, whatever sentient beings, sons or daughters of noble family, who retain, memorize, and so forth this sūtra and put its meaning into practice may experience the suffering of being tormented and extremely tormented by various illnesses, fights, having their faults exposed, being bound, beaten, and so forth, yet it is not a great fault. What is the reason for that? By the power of the fruits of all whatever great non-virtuous karmas, karmas that would lead to rebirth in the three lower realms, those sentient beings committed in this and previous lifetimes ripening upon this very life and tormenting them with suffering, all those non-virtuous karmas of previous lifetimes will be exhausted, that is, purified, and they will also swiftly obtain the awakening of a Buddha. If it is explained that by the power of thoroughly engaging in the antidotes of making effort in the perfection of wisdom, one destroys apprehending a self, which is the root of sinful karmas, and obtains freedom and omniscience, what need is there to say that it overcomes karmas of rebirth in the lower realms? Such a method is explained in texts such as the Tarkajvālā, and the Mahāmokṣa also states: 'Even though there are sins for rebirth in the lower realms, they are exhausted merely by having a headache.' For example, just as even if a seed is planted, if there is absolutely no water, fertilizer, heat, moisture, and so forth, a sprout will not grow, similarly, if one is able to abandon apprehending a self, no matter what accumulated karmas there are, since there are no afflictions as companions, they are unable to issue forth a fully ripened result, just as the Pramāṇavārttika states: 'For one who has completely transcended craving for existence, other karmas are unable to project, because the cooperative conditions are exhausted.' ^2-97

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-98]]

Subhūti, I clearly know and cognize that in the past, in time before even the measureless of measureless eons, and in time before even the earliest before the Buddha Dīpaṃkara, eighty-four countless hundred thousands of ten millions of Buddhas appeared, and I venerated, served, and so forth, those, bringing them joy, without bringing them displeasure and without wearying their minds; of the two, the merit of my having done that and whatever merit there is of one who writes and so forth this sūtra in the latter time, the final five hundred years, this latter aggregate of merit is greater, such that the former aggregate of merit does not approach and does not go near even up to merely a single one out of a hundredth part, that is, when this is divided into a hundred parts, and likewise it does not bear, that is, is unable to, comparison even up to a thousandth part, a hundred thousandth part, and a number, a measure, a count, an example, a comparison, and a cause. Although all those illustrate examples, "part" means dissimilar in large or small portions and dissimilar in large or small numbers, dissimilar in example, unequal in comparison, dissimilar in reason, and "count" means unsuitable to be counted as equal. ^2-98

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-99]]

If one were to perfectly analyze and express how large an aggregate of merit they fully generate and that the aggregate of merit they will retain is like this, sentient beings of inferior intellect would not believe it, and their minds becoming maddened, that is, bewildered, their minds would become completely disturbed. ^2-99

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-100]]

Although this dharma discourse has a small text, its meaning is vast, so it is inconceivable, and the fully ripened fruition of putting its meaning into practice is also inconceivable. As for "fully ripened fruition", it does not apprehend solely the fully ripened fruition, but also includes the causally concordant fruition, the environmental fruition, the fruition of separation, and so forth. Although something like this also appeared above, it is spoken with a different contextual meaning in mind. ^2-100

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-101]]

Furthermore, Subhūti requested of the Bhagavān, asking how one who has entered the vehicle of the bodhisattvas should abide, and so forth. Although something like this occurred previously, it was done in terms of the path of accumulation and the level of engaged interest, whereas here it is spoken with the intention of engaging in that way near the attainment of the path of seeing and upon the occasion of having attained it, so the meaning is just as explained before. ^2-101

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-102]]

The conclusion summarizes that whatever dharma is the entry into the vehicle of the bodhisattvas is not truly established. ^2-102

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-103]]

Furthermore, when he asked, "Subhūti, do you think there are any dharmas by which I, the Tathāgata, previously took up whatever dharma from the Buddha Dīpaṃkara in an ultimate sense and became a Buddha?", in reply Subhūti stated, "There are not", and the Bhagavān assented to the truth of the words by saying, "It is so," and to the truth of the meaning by saying, "It is so"; there is no such dharma at all. ^2-103

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-104]]

If there had been some such dharma, the Tathāgata Dīpaṃkara would not have prophesied to me, "Young brahmin, you will become a Buddha named Śākyamuni from about this time in the future," but because there is no dharma whatsoever of fully and completely awakening as a Buddha that is truly established, at that time the Tathāgata Dīpaṃkara prophesied to me my awakening in that way. Furthermore, the dharmas of by what, what, and to what one is prophesied are merely nominal designations, merely bare names, but the manner of their lacking establishment in an ultimate sense has already been stated above. It is explained in a sūtra that it has been one countless and ninety-one eons since the appearance of the Buddha Dīpaṃkara, and also from the annotations to the Pradīpoddyotana commentary on the Guhyasamāja Root Tantra it is stated, "Later, Dīpaṃkara appeared one countless and ninety-one great eons previously." At that time, when the Teacher became the young brahmin Dharmamegha, that very Buddha placed his feet upon his body and blessed him, so having attained great endurance in the non-arising of dharmas, he was prophesied to become a Buddha; generally, attaining endurance in the non-arising of dharmas has three: small, medium, and great; the small is the endurance of the path of preparation, the medium is the path of seeing, and the great arises from the attainment of arhatship, and in terms of the Mahāyāna, it is attained from the eighth level. To attain whichever of those three endurances, because one must realize that conditioned dharmas do not arise by their own nature, one must definitely realize emptiness, and for the medium and great one must realize emptiness directly, and for the great one must also abandon afflictions. Therefore, for a direct prophecy to become a Buddha, one definitely must have previously realized emptiness, so a direct prophecy is not given to bodhisattvas who are bound by grasping at true existence, because those who are bound by grasping at true existence, adhering to true existence in the dharmas of what is to be attained and what attains, are unsuitable as vessels to be directly prophesied, because as explained before, truly established dharmas are unable to be transformed, so it is unsuitable to posit any attainer and so forth. ^2-104

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-105]]

Regarding the reason for prophesying, if one asks, "Why is that?", the expression "Tathāgata" is a synonymous designation for the primordial wisdom that comprehends the authentic suchness of all dharmas being empty of true existence, so it is because he is expressed by way of that. To summarize, the meaning of "Tathāgata" is having actualized and comprehended the suchness wherein the two obscurations are abandoned, which is the meaning of his mind having gone to or cognized suchness exactly as it is. ^2-105

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-106]]

If some sentient being were to speak in this way, saying, "The Tathāgata fully and completely awakened to awakening in an ultimate sense", those very words would be speaking falsely. ^2-106

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-107]]

The reason is because there is no dharma of becoming a Buddha in an ultimate sense whatsoever. ^2-107

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-108]]

Although something like this occurred above, it is applied to the respective contexts, so although there is repetition in the words, there is no fault of repetition in the meaning, and knowing its meaning well comes down to knowing the root meaning of lacking own-nature. Whatever dharma the Tathāgata has awakened to or taught, within that there is no true dharma of the ultimate established by its own nature, and there is no false dharma of the conventional established in that way. There also appears to be another, different way of explaining this. Because of that reason, the Tathāgata has comprehended and actualized that all dharmas subsumed by the two truths without distinction are empty of their own nature, and in terms of that, the suchness of all dharmas being empty of true existence is like the dharmakāya of a Buddha, so all dharmas are called "the dharmas of a Buddha". This is from the Abhisamayālaṃkāra: "Those have the defining characteristic of their own nature." ^2-108

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-109]]

and, from the sūtra called Ratnakoṭi, when Mañjuśrī asked, "Śāriputra, are you inclined to believe that all dharmas are equal to the supreme bull of a Buddha?", in reply, "Mañjuśrī, thus the Bhagavān has fully and completely awakened to all dharmas as isolated by their own nature; I am inclined thus", which is similar to what occurs. ^2-109

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-110]]

The meaning of the statement, "All dharmas, such as a Buddha and forms and so forth, are non-existent in an ultimate sense, therefore all dharmas are the dharmas of a Buddha," is also like that. ^2-110

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-111]]

As for teaching an example for that, he stated, "It is like a person who is endowed with a human body and has become a great body." Ācārya Kamalaśīla seems to have applied the meaning of this by applying being endowed with a body to the body of a bodhisattva and a great body to the body of a Buddha, and it should be explained like this: just as a person whose small body gradually grows and becomes large is nominally designated as a "great body" in relation to the former, I think that if one says the meaning of that example is that by gradually purifying obscurations with respect to the dharmatā of the minds of all sentient beings being empty of true existence, ultimately all obscurations are exhausted and it becomes the dharmakāya of a Buddha, it accords with his intention. Then, when Subhūti stated that although the body of such an example is not established as a body in an ultimate sense, it is posited as such conventionally, the Bhagavān assented, saying "It is so," and so forth. ^2-111

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-112]]

Furthermore, although a bodhisattva must generate the mind to establish immeasurable sentient beings in non-abiding nirvāṇa by way of conventional illusion, showing that in an ultimate sense no sentient being whatsoever is established in it, if any bodhisattva says with an adherence to true existence, thinking, "I will pass sentient beings completely beyond sorrow," that one is not called a bodhisattva who knows the meaning of selflessness. ^2-112

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-113]]

As for the reason, when he asked, "Do you think there is any dharma at all called a bodhisattva in an ultimate sense?", in reply Subhūti stated, "There is no such thing." The Bhagavān said to Subhūti, "Because of the reason of its non-existence, the Tathāgata has said that all dharmas of thorough affliction and purification are without a sentient being established as a self of persons, without a life-force, and without a person." To summarize, if one engages with adherence to true existence, thinking, "The self is what attains," and "This is the dharma to be attained," that is explained as someone with the pride of self-conceit who arrogantly assumes that what is non-existent exists, so it is unsuitable; that is, it has been explained above that if they were truly established they would be unable to be transformed, so it is unsuitable to posit what is to be attained, what attains, and so forth for any dharma. By these, the meaning of "how should one practice?", the manner of meditating on selflessness, is taught. ^2-113

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-114]]

Then, showing that not only does one not adhere to true existence regarding establishing sentient beings in nirvāṇa, but also although one adopts a Buddha field conventionally, one should not do so with adherence to true existence: "Subhūti, if any bodhisattva says with words, adhering thus, thinking, 'I will thoroughly establish in an ultimate sense the excellent arrangements of the field of the place where I will awaken as a Buddha', it is also unsuitable to express and adhere to it in that way." ^2-114

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-115]]

Regarding the reason, it is because the Tathāgata has said that other than those arrangements of the field existing conventionally as accumulated and assembled like an illusion, there are no truly established arrangements; therefore, they are nominally called "arrangements of the field". ^2-115

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-116]]

If any bodhisattva is inclined to and realizes thus, thinking, "Forms and so forth are without a self of persons and without a self of dharmas", the Tathāgata expresses that one as a "great bodhisattva who has realized selflessness". By this as well, it is explained that one must meditate on the two selflessnesses. ^2-116

#### Teaching that the Tathāgata Possesses the Five Extraordinary Eyes ^2-2-9-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-117]]

If one wonders, "How does the Tathāgata possess the knowledge that sees the meaning of selflessness?", showing that the Tathāgata possesses the five excellent eyes: when the Bhagavān asked Subhūti, "Does the Tathāgata possess the eye of flesh, the divine, the wisdom, the dharma, and the Buddha eye?", in reply Subhūti stated, "The Tathāgata possesses them." In general, although there are large and small scopes in the manner in which the five eyes see objects, if they are applied here to a Buddha: with the eye of flesh he directly sees all subtle and gross forms in the world realms, with the divine eye the series of births and deaths of sentient beings and so forth, with the wisdom eye all subtle and gross selflessness, with the dharma eye all the divisions of the conventional, and with the Buddha eye he directly sees all dharmas subsumed by the two truths exactly as they are. Herein, the eye of flesh and the divine eye are the sense faculty of the eye or the sense consciousness depending upon it, while the other three are mental consciousness. However, these are posited in terms of their respective individual objects, for it is explained that since the Buddha sees all dharmas even with sense consciousnesses such as eye consciousness, there is no difference from actual omniscience; and it is just as it is explained in the sūtra, "He sees even with the uṣṇīṣa of a Buddha" and so forth, and in the Nāmasaṃgīti, at the occasion of "Even his hair is a blazing vajra", that all parts of the Buddha's body know the objects of knowledge. Furthermore, I think this intends that the body and sense consciousnesses of a Buddha engage in the manner of being of one nature with mental consciousness, and similarly it also occurs in the fiftieth chapter of the Cakrasaṃvara Root Tantra at the occasion of, "Possessing hands and feet everywhere, possessing eyes, heads, and faces everywhere." ^2-117

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-118]]

Showing that because the Tathāgata possesses such eyes he sees all dharmas and also sees the mind streams of sentient beings: when the Bhagavān asked Subhūti, "For example, however many numerous grains of sand there are in the Gaṅgā river, if there were exactly that many Gaṅgā rivers, would those world realms be many?", in reply Subhūti stated, "Many," and so the Bhagavān said, "I thoroughly know the streams and aspects of the various, dissimilar, and diverse minds of those sentient beings existing in all those world realms." ^2-118

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-119]]

"Even so, I have explained that the mind streams of sentient beings are not established as what is called a stream in an ultimate sense; therefore, because they are non-existent in an ultimate sense, conventionally they are expressed as what is called a 'mind stream'." By this, it is taught that the five eyes and the objects of the five eyes are also merely posited conventionally, but are not established in an ultimate sense. ^2-119

#### Mind and Mental Factors ^2-2-10-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-120]]

Past minds are unapprehendable in an ultimate sense because they have passed away. Future minds are also non-existent in an ultimate sense because they are presently unproduced and have not arisen. Presently arisen minds are also non-existent in an ultimate sense because they do not exist independently. "Mind" does not mean merely the main mind, but subsumes all minds and mental factors. Among mental factors, feelings are a great basis for grasping at true existence, because when intense feelings of pleasure and pain arise, the mind grasping at a self also arises with intense force. Therefore, although there are many reasonings showing that feelings are not truly established, the root is like this statement by Śāntideva: "When there is no feeler at all, there is not any feeling either." As for that, if the self who experiences feelings does not exist by its own nature, how could the feelings arising for it exist by their own nature, since characteristics are impossible without a basis of characteristics? Feelings of pleasure and pain are experienced having arisen in the nature of meeting or not meeting with body and mind, but not by their very essence, because if either body or mind is absent, feelings are also absent, and even if the body is present, if the mind is absent, feelings are not experienced: just as feelings of pleasure and pain do not arise even if corpses, earth, stones, and so forth are destroyed. In short, all consciousnesses such as feelings also arise merely conventionally like the light of a butter lamp in dependence upon an object, but because they do not arise independently without depending on such things, they are not established by their own nature. Established by its own nature, truly established, established in an ultimate sense, established by its own essence, established by its own characteristics, independently established, genuinely established, and established without dependence are equivalent in meaning and merely synonyms. By this, the manner of how the mind is to be held is taught. ^2-120

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-121]]

If one wonders, 'If the mind stream does not exist by its own nature, won't results fail to arise from actions such as giving generosity in dependence upon the mind?', showing that although the mind stream and so forth do not exist by their own nature, conventionally results are produced in dependence upon the mind: when the Bhagavān asked Subhūti, 'If someone filled this three-thousandfold great thousandfold world realm with the previously explained seven precious things such as gold and silver and gave it as generosity to others, would they generate much merit from that?', in reply he stated, 'It would be much,' and then he validated it to Subhūti, saying, 'That is exactly so' and so forth. ^2-121

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-122]]

Those heaps of merit have arisen conventionally from a collection like an illusion, but if they were established by their own very essence without depending on such things, the Tathāgata would not have spoken of a 'heap of merit'. The reason is that he intended that the meaning of 'heap' in 'heap of merit' is the meaning of a collection, and if it were established independently, it would be established without depending upon a collection. ^2-122

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-123]]

If one wonders, 'If the heap of merit is not truly existent, then the form body of a Buddha that is born from it would also be non-existent', showing that this does not follow: when he asked, 'Do you think one should view, that is, apprehend and assert, this perfectly established form body as the Tathāgata in an ultimate sense?', in reply he stated, 'It is not so; one should not view it in that way.' The reason is that the form body is also established from a collection of causes and conditions, but is not established in an ultimate sense, which is similar to the heap of merit; conventionally it is expressed as 'established as the form body'. ^2-123

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-124]]

If one wonders, 'If a form body exists, are its marks and signs truly established?', showing that they are not: in reply to the question, 'Do you think one should view the Tathāgata by the perfection of marks and signs?', he stated, 'One should not view him in that way.' One does not view the Tathāgata with grasping at true existence by way of a perfection of marks and signs existing as truly established on the body of the Tathāgata, because those perfections of marks and signs are things that are gathered and assembled, established like an illusion; therefore, although the perfection of marks and signs is not established in an ultimate sense, conventionally it is expressed as such. Although something similar to this occurred earlier, that was from the perspective of taking it into practice from the stage of engagement by conviction, while here it is from the perspective of taking it into practice from the noble stage of pure superior resolve, as Kamalaśīla states. Likewise, the meaning of the statement that there is no Dharma taught by the Tathāgata is also as explained above. If someone asserts and says with grasping at true existence that 'The Tathāgata has taught the Dharma in an ultimate sense', that is a bondage seized by grasping at true existence that grasps at what is non-existent as existent and conceives falsely, and thus they would be deprecating me, the Tathāgata. ^2-124

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-125]]

"The reason is that what is called 'Dharma' and 'teaching the Dharma' is nothing but merely conventional, and nothing is established in an ultimate sense" – the meaning of this has already been explained, and if expressed here by way of scripture, it is as stated in a sūtra: "Whatever things are expressed by whatever names, they do not exist; that is the dharmatā of things." And also from the Ratnakoṭi-sūtra: Mañjuśrī said to the Bhagavān, "Here, whatever thing is expressed by whatever expression, as for that thing, that thing is non-existent. What is non-existent is empty." And also from the Pramāṇavārttika: "Words show what is imputed by convention; that is done for the sake of convention; at that time, there are no own characteristics." Although the meaning of these is extensive, merely to illustrate it: when things such as a vase are expressed as 'this and that', the word directly expresses the meaning generality, but the specifically characterized phenomena such as a vase do not exist independently as the direct objects of expression of the word, so they are not independently established as the basis for the engagement of the conventions of their respective names, and it is taught that the self-isolates of the objects of expression of those various words are not truly established. If one wonders, 'What invalidates them being established in that way?', there is: noble Asaṅga states in the Mahāyānasaṃgraha, "Because there is no awareness prior to the name, because of non-existence, and because of being unmixed, their nature is established as selflessness and contradicted by mixing with the self." If the meaning of this is stated in brief: words engage with various meanings by the power of convention, but not by the power of the things themselves, because for any thing such as a vase, the perception of a vase does not arise prior to its name being imputed, and a single meaning can also be expressed by various names, and multiple meanings can also be expressed by a single name. If they were to engage by the power of the things themselves, it would follow that even prior to the name being imputed, the perception thinking 'it is that' would arise, and a single meaning would follow as being multiple, and multiple meanings would also become single, and because there is the expression of their names even on a basis where a vase and so forth are non-existent, a vase and so forth would also exist there, so it would follow that no sentient being would be destitute of all things such as a vase, and so forth—there are many. ^2-125

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-126]]

Then Subhūti asked, 'In the future, will any sentient beings who hear this teaching of such a profound Dharma come to believe in it?'; although something similar to this occurred earlier as well, it is by the power of the basis and the purpose being different. ^2-126

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-127]]

The Bhagavān too, considering that the answer to this had already been given, stated: 'Those sentient beings are neither established as sentient beings in an ultimate sense, nor are they non-existent as sentient beings conventionally.' ^2-127

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-128]]

The reason is because the Tathāgata has taught that what are called 'sentient beings' are non-existent as sentient beings in an ultimate sense; they are called 'sentient beings' in a relative sense. The manner of positing sentient beings in a relative sense is as it occurs in a sūtra: 'Just as it is expressed as a chariot in dependence upon a collection of parts, likewise, in dependence upon the aggregates, they are called sentient beings relatively.' ^2-128

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-129]]

As before, in reply to the question, 'Do you think there is any Dharma that is awakening?', when he stated how there is not, he gave his approval, saying 'It is exactly so,' and so forth; concerning that, there is not even the slightest Dharma of awakening to be apprehended in an ultimate sense, but conventionally it is expressed as 'awakening to perfect enlightenment'. Kamalaśīla explains that while something similar to this occurred earlier from the perspective of the path of learning, this is intended in the sense of being non-existent in the perspective of the awareness of the Buddha that sees things as they are. Furthermore, 'Subhūti, whatever things there are, they are all equal and same in being not truly established', and 'because by the power of such sameness, there is no unequal thing whatsoever'. ^2-129

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-130]]

That sameness is sameness in the non-existence of a sentient being, the non-existence of a life-principle, and the non-existence of a personal self; although they are not established as dharmas in an ultimate sense, conventionally the Tathāgata has awakened by means of all those virtuous dharmas such as generosity, and the virtuous dharmas in the mental continuum of a Buddha are also called 'Buddha'. ^2-130

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-131]]

By means of the three beginning with 'the non-existence of a sentient being', the extreme of superimposition is refuted, and by means of 'those virtuous dharmas', and so forth, the extreme of deprecation is refuted, thereby clearing away the two extremes; even though dharmas are not established in an ultimate sense, conventionally, if some sentient being were to gather heaps of the seven kinds of precious things such as gold equal in size to all the kings of mountains, Mount Meru, in the great trichiliocosm and give them as a gift, and if some person, let alone this entire text, focusing on obtaining the result of Buddhahood, were to merely take up a single four-line stanza and correctly retain its meaning and teach it to others, the meaning of the statement that 'that former merit of generosity does not approach even a hundredth part of this latter one, up to it does not bear comparison', is as explained earlier. The intention of explaining that the benefit of listening to and contemplating this prajñāpāramitā is greater than the merit of such generosity is the intention that although the result of generosity without realizing emptiness is great, it will only result in obtaining the high status of a god or human, whereas if one listens to and contemplates prajñāpāramitā, one will obtain the definite goodness of liberation and the state of omniscience; however, I think this is intended for the occasion of the path of meditation. Showing that the Tathāgata does not possess grasping at true existence regarding sentient beings: Subhūti, if you think the Tathāgata thinks in his mind even during post-meditation, 'I shall release the sentient beings to be tamed from the bondage of grasping at true existence in an ultimate sense', Subhūti, you should not view it in that way. ^2-131

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-132]]

The reason is because in an ultimate sense there are no sentient beings whatsoever whom the Tathāgata has released from the bondage of grasping at true existence and caused to be liberated. If the Tathāgata were to have such an idea, that itself would become the fault of the Tathāgata grasping at a self, a sentient being, a person, and a life-principle as truly existent; but that is inappropriate, because the Tathāgata has no grasping at or clinging to a self, and so forth, because he has abandoned all clinging that grasps at a self. Although conventionally the Tathāgata releases sentient beings who are bound by grasping at true existence, in an ultimate sense there is no releasing them from that bondage, because if they were to exist in an ultimate sense, they would be immutable, so they would be unfit and unable to be released. ^2-132

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-133]]

'Subhūti, as for what is called grasping at a self', the Tathāgata stated that he has abandoned it and that he himself possesses no such clinging to true existence; nevertheless, childish ordinary beings who do not know the lack of true existence cling to and grasp at all dharmas as a self. Here, ordinary beings who do not know the meaning of reality are designated by the name 'childish'; since noble ones directly see the reality of dharmas, they are not called 'childish' but are called 'wise', and because such ordinary beings do not know it in that way, they are like children, so they are referred to by that name. I think it is also suitable to explain the meaning of 'that grasping is non-existent' as meaning that it does not exist in the manner of the object grasped by grasping at a self. Making the specific distinction of 'childish' is for the sake of not calling 'childish' those ordinary beings who are wise and know the meaning of selflessness. ^2-133

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-134]]

'Subhūti, regarding what are called childish ordinary beings, the Tathāgata has taught that they are not established as ordinary beings in an ultimate sense; conventionally they are expressed like that by name.' The meaning of the term 'ordinary beings' is that they are explained as such because by the power of karma they are born in various different migrations. Furthermore, he asked, 'Subhūti, do you think that one should view and consider him as the Tathāgata merely by his possessing the perfect major and minor marks?' The two 'am and mam are merely a difference in translation, but there is no difference in meaning. ^2-134

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-135]]

In reply, he stated, 'It is not so,' and so forth. Although something similar to this occurred earlier as well, it was not for the sake of primarily teaching the dharmakāya, whereas here it is asked again for the sake of teaching the dharmakāya. ^2-135

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-136]]

The Bhagavān gave his approval, saying 'It is exactly so,' and so forth; one should not view a body merely possessing the major and minor marks as the Tathāgata. He stated, 'Subhūti, if one were to view him in that way, since a wheel-turning king also has the major and minor marks, it would absurdly follow that he himself must be considered the Tathāgata, but since that is not accepted, one should not view and consider him as the Tathāgata merely by his possessing the perfect marks.' ^2-136

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-137]]

Then Subhūti stated, 'According to how I understand the meaning of what the Bhagavān has said, I do not view him in that way.' Although a wheel-turning king has the thirty-two marks, they are not as clear and are not complete, whereas a Buddha's major and minor marks are clear and complete; as it is stated, 'Although it is accepted that all wheel-turning kings have these,' and, 'The marks of the Sage are superior because they are situated in their proper places, and are clear and complete.' ^2-137

#### The Dharmakāya ^2-2-11-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-138]]

Through that, having indicated the form body, as for teaching the dharmakāya: ^2-138

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-139]]

The Bhagavān spoke in verse, stating, 'Those who see me as form,' and so forth; as for the meaning of this, the Lord himself quoted that scripture in his commentary on the Mūlamadhyamakakārikā and explained the meaning as follows: "These words indicate that because grasping at conceptualized dharmas such as form and sound as truly existent is engaging wrongly with the reality of the Buddha and abandoning it, by that one does not see the reality of the Buddha, and therefore the reality of the Buddha is to be viewed as dharmatā and the dharmakāya. That dharmakāya is not to be known by way of grasping as before, because it is impossible to know it in that way"—having taught that, as he stated, "Because the eye of the mind is impaired by conceptual proliferation, it acts as the proof for not seeing the reality of the Tathāgata." As for the meaning of the words: "Whatever sentient beings, seeing the major and minor marks and so forth of me the Tathāgata, view and see, that is, grasp at, me as form through clinging to true existence, and whatever sentient beings know me as truly existent, that is, cling to and grasp at me, through hearing my name and so forth and expressing me as sound—in short, those who are bound by grasping at true existence that clings to the Tathāgata as truly existent as a body of name and form, have engaged wrongly in viewing the dharmakāya of the Tathāgata; those beings do not see my dharmakāya, emptiness, as the Tathāgata." The reason they do not see it is that the dharmakāya of the Buddhas, the svabhāvikakāya, must be viewed as the body of dharmatā, and ^2-139

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-140]]

the dharmakāya of the guiding Buddhas, the dharmatā which is ultimate truth, is not to be known by a mind bound by grasping at true existence, because that dharmakāya cannot be completely known by such a mind, because knowing the emptiness which is the dharmakāya of the Buddha requires realizing emptiness. Illustrated by this, it is similar that if there is clinging to true existence regarding any dharma, one does not see the dharmatā of that dharma. Whenever any conventional dharma such as form is directly realized, it must be realized in a manner where it appears together with conceptual proliferation, whereas whenever the ultimate dharma, which is the dharmatā of those, is directly realized, it must be realized in a manner where conceptual proliferation is eliminated; this distinction between the respective ways of seeing the two truths is a point that is difficult to realize. Not only that, but even if whatever dharma it is, is not directly realized as lacking true existence, although conceptual proliferation appears to the appearing aspect of the mind that realizes it by way of a meaning-generality, conceptual proliferation must be eliminated in its ascertained aspect. As for summarizing the meaning of that: ^2-140

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-141]]

In that way, from the perspective of the dharmakāya, the Tathāgata has not awakened to unsurpassed enlightenment merely by possessing the perfect major and minor marks. As for refuting the view of nihilism regarding that: ^2-141

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-142]]

'Subhūti, if you think and grasp that when those who have correctly entered the vehicle of bodhisattvas realize that some dharma such as form is not established in an ultimate sense, that dharma has been destroyed by reasoning or made non-existent and designated as annihilated, you should not view and consider it in that way.' ^2-142

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-143]]

Those bodhisattvas have not destroyed or designated as annihilated any dharma whatsoever, because when it is realized in that way, that dharma is realized as lacking true existence, but that dharma is not realized as non-existent. Although those conventional dharmas do not exist for the ascertained aspect of the mind that realizes it in that way, that mind does not realize them as non-existent, because although they do not exist for the aspect of that mind, they generally exist; for example, although a vase and so forth that appears as empty of true existence does not exist for the aspect of a mind bound by grasping at true existence, it generally exists. ^2-143

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-144]]

Furthermore, than the merit of whatever person filling world systems equal in number to the sand of the river Gaṅgā with the seven kinds of precious jewels and giving them as a gift, if whatever bodhisattva directly realizes the meaning of non-self relying on this dharma-discourse, the text of the prajñāpāramitā, and obtains great forbearance towards the ultimate non-arising, that is, the dharma of non-arising, that very person will generate a much more incalculable and immeasurable heap of merit from that basis of obtaining forbearance; the meaning of 'obtaining forbearance towards the dharma of non-arising' has already been explained earlier. ^2-144

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-145]]

Furthermore, when he said, 'Subhūti, bodhisattvas should not acquire the heap of merit through clinging to true existence,' Subhūti asked, feigning the doubt that arose thinking "Do they not acquire merit conventionally?", saying, 'Bhagavān, do bodhisattvas not acquire the heap of merit?' at which the Bhagavān replied, 'Indeed, merit must be acquired conventionally, but it is not wrongly acquired by viewing merit with clinging to true existence; ^2-145

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-146]]

therefore, it is called acquiring merit conventionally,' he said; by this 'should not acquire,' the view of eternalism that clings to the heap of merit as truly established is cleared away, and by 'indeed acquiring,' the view of nihilism is cleared away. Furthermore, to Subhūti: 'If some sentient being, with clinging to true existence, asks, saying and thinking this: "Does the Tathāgata depart, that is, go elsewhere over there, or come here, or stand up, or sit on a seat, or go to sleep?", ^2-146

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-147]]

that questioner does not know the meaning of how I the Tathāgata have explained my own coming, departing, and so forth as ultimately non-existent. ^2-147

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-148]]

For what reason? ^2-148

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-149]]

'Subhūti, the Tathāgata ultimately has not departed to anywhere in any direction, and has not come here from any direction; for that reason, he is called a perfectly complete Buddha conventionally.' As for the meaning of the word 'Tathāgata,' as it was stated in a sūtra: 'The Tathāgata perfectly saw emptiness, perfectly knew it, and excellently realized it; therefore he is called omniscient'—it is by way of having directly seen emptiness, and although conventionally there is coming and departing and so forth, from the perspective of the dharmakāya, emptiness, coming and departing and so forth are ultimately non-existent; regarding how the coming and departing and so forth of the Tathāgata are in the noble Aṣṭasāhasrikā from the section on Sadāprarudita relying on Dharmodgata, the manners of Sadāprarudita asking Dharmodgata, which are explained extensively later in the avadāna of Sadāprarudita, if set out here would be feared to be too much, so to express a brief summary: in the time of summer, at the boundary of mountain and plain, near sand or pale cliffs and so forth, a mirage is indeed seen moving with a shimmering blue appearance, but that mirage water initially did not come here from anywhere, and finally will not actually go anywhere in any direction—similarly, the coming and departing and so forth of the Tathāgata is also like that; and similarly, pebbles and twigs and so forth emanated by a magician into individuals such as humans and animals, and furthermore various forms of buddhas or sentient beings appearing in a dream, and sounds arising from a lute—these initially did not arise from anywhere by their own essence. ^2-149

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-150]]

Finally, like the example of not going anywhere by their own essence, similarly, the coming and departing and so forth of the Tathāgata are also explained as being similar to that. Furthermore, in the Questions of the Old Woman, it is said: 'Not only the Tathāgata, but arising has not come from anywhere and does not go anywhere,' and so forth; at the end of stating this applied to other dharmas as well, the application with an example is: Indeed, sound arises from beating a drum; although that sound initially does not abide and does not arise by its own essence in the drum's wood, the leather, the beater's hand, the stick, and so forth, and finally does not go anywhere, when conditions gather, it must be accepted just as it is explained to arise and cease conventionally; from that very text it is also said: 'Whoever perfectly knows the manner of the drum's sound, that one perfectly knows emptiness.' Furthermore, just as it is said: 'Mañjuśrī, the nature, the entity of all dharmas, is without abiding, free from going and coming, like space'; since this is said, the dharmakāya, emptiness, is like space, meaning it cannot be pointed out as ultimately abiding here, and just as space does not go or come elsewhere, the dharmakāya, emptiness, also does not go away from here or come here from there; this is merely an illustration. ^2-150

#### Subtle Particles ^2-2-12-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-151]]

Furthermore, upon asking Subhūti, 'If some sentient being were to grind however many earth particles there are in the great three-thousandfold world system into powder in an extremely small form, for example, like a collection of extremely subtle particles, would those powdered collections be many?', in reply he stated, 'That would be many,' and: 'If a collection of particles were to ultimately exist, the Bhagavān would not have spoken conventionally of "a collection of extremely subtle particles", because if they ultimately existed, he would have had to state that they exist in that way.' ^2-151

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-152]]

As for whatever was spoken as 'a collection of extremely subtle particles' whose nature is to be ultimately non-existent, it is because the Tathāgata spoke having known that they are not ultimately established as a collection; therefore, it is called 'a collection of extremely subtle particles' conventionally. The smallest limit of all forms is the extremely subtle particle, and there is no particle smaller than that, and although there is the assertion that it is partless, since that is harmed by reasoning, it must be accepted as having parts; and having parts is established because it exists surrounded by six particles in the four directions and above and below, and if it did not exist, the manner of demonstrating the reasoning that harms that has already been merely illustrated earlier. ^2-152

#### The Composition of Gross Particles ^2-2-13-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-153]]

As for the manner in which the coarse is composed from the extremely subtle particle: seven extremely subtle particles make one subtle particle, seven of those make one minute particle, seven of those make one iron particle, seven of those make one water particle, seven of those make one rabbit particle, seven of those make one sheep particle, seven of those make one ox particle, seven of those make one sunbeam particle, seven of those make one nit particle, seven of those make one louse particle, seven of those make one finger-width, twenty-four finger-widths make one cubit, four cubits make one fathom, five hundred fathoms make one earshot, and eight earshots make one yojana; and others are also illustrated by this. ^2-153

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-154]]

Having explained that the extremely subtle particle is not established by its own nature, demonstrating that the coarse forms accumulated from them are also not established by their own nature is as follows: The Tathāgata has spoken of even that great three-thousandfold world system as not being ultimately established as a world system; therefore, it is called 'the great three-thousandfold world system' merely conventionally by name. By this, both the environment and its inhabitants are illustrated. If such a world system of environment and inhabitants were to ultimately exist, it would have to be established and apprehended as a single coarse whole without depending on being collected and gathered; and if it were established in that way, the Tathāgata would also have had to see it. Because the Tathāgata did not see such world systems of environment and inhabitants as being established as a single partless coarse whole that is not collected and gathered, regarding the statement 'whatever grasping as being established as a single partless solid coarse whole in the world was spoken of, that grasping is also not ultimately established as grasping'—the former is the apprehended object, and this is explained as demonstrating that the apprehending subject is also ultimately established. If this latter one is treated from the perspective of demonstrating the world of the inhabitants, the bodies of the sentient beings who are the inhabitants are coarse forms arising from the accumulation of extremely subtle particles; that refutes apprehending them as a single partless coarse whole by their own essence, without depending on the collecting and gathering of minute particles. Although it is easy to ascertain that the coarse forms accumulated from the extremely subtle are false things that are collected and gathered, due to it being somewhat difficult to ascertain the extremely subtle particle as false, there have also occurred assertions of it as partless, permanent, and ultimately true. ^2-154

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-155]]

Then the Bhagavān said to Subhūti: 'That very grasping as a whole exists in worldly convention and does not ultimately exist; and although that dharma which is the apprehended object is ultimately inexpressible, ordinary childish beings have grasped it by adhering to it as being ultimately expressible.' By these, both the profound and the extensive aspects have been explained. However, in order to excellently know the manner in which it is ultimately inexpressible, one appears to need to excellently know this manner in which expressing sounds do not engage with their own objects by the power of real entities, but engage by the power of intention and the power of convention. ^2-155

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-156]]

'Then, Subhūti, if some sentient being were to say this: "The Tathāgata has spoken of the view of a personal self, and the view of a sentient being, a life-principle, and a person in his own system," would that be speaking by speaking correctly, that is, excellently?'—upon asking this, in reply Subhūti said, 'That is not so.' ^2-156

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-157]]

'For what reason is that? Whatever the Tathāgata has spoken of as the view of a self by others and so forth, is the complete dismantling of the view of a self and so forth, and it is because the Tathāgata has spoken of the adhered objects of those views being non-existent and that view also being ultimately non-existent; therefore, it is called the view of a self and so forth conventionally.' ^2-157

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-158]]

Then, wishing to conclude, the Bhagavān said: 'Subhūti, here, those who have correctly entered the bodhisattva vehicle should know, view, and resolve upon all dharmas by applying them just as they were explained'; regarding this, 'knowing' is the prajñā of hearing, 'viewing' is the prajñā of reflection, and 'resolving' means one should generate the prajñā of meditation. As for 'by all means the perception of a dharma', one should not generate the perception of grasping at true existence, thinking that any dharma whatsoever is truly established, and one should not abide in the perception of truth. ^2-158

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-159]]

'For what reason is that? Subhūti, what is called the perception of a dharma, is because the Tathāgata has spoken of the object of perception and the perception itself as ultimately not existing as perception; therefore, it is called the perception of a dharma conventionally.' Regarding both 'de ci'i phyir zhe na' and 'de ci'i slad du zhe na', 'phyir' and 'slad du' indicate the logic or reason, and although their original term is identical as 'atha', the former is the Teacher's speech and has no honorific, while the latter is Subhūti's speech and is translated with an honorific. Thus, having repeatedly explained the absence of true existence, grasping at true existence is emphatically refuted. As for the manner of refuting it, it is refuted by demonstrating logical invalidation to its adhered object, just as it is apprehended by grasping at true existence, but it is not like extracting a thorn; for Āryadeva said, 'If one sees the lack of self in objects, the seed of existence will be stopped,' and the Pramāṇavārttika says, 'Without invalidating its object, one is unable to abandon it.' As for the reason for refuting it, it is because grasping at true existence is ignorance, and by its power one cycles in saṃsāra, and to abandon grasping at true existence one must realize the lack of true existence, and having realized it, through the power of meditation one will be liberated from saṃsāra and also attain the state of liberation and omniscience—he intended this, for the Saṃcayagāthā says: 'Those endowed with minds attached to saṃsāra cycle permanently; ^2-159

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-160]]

the self and the dharmas of the self are both unreal and empty, yet childish beings themselves cast knots in space. For example, due to a suspicious perception of pervasive poison, even though that poison has not gone inside, one faints. Likewise, childish beings who assert a self and what belongs to a self, through the unreal conception of perceiving that self, are born and destroyed. Just as there is grasping, thus thorough affliction is taught; not apprehending a self or what belongs to a self is spoken of as purification'; and: 'However many sentient beings there are—inferior, middling, and supreme—the Sugata has spoken of them all as arising from ignorance. When conditions gather, the machine of suffering thoroughly arises; that machine of ignorance is without exhaustion and without increase. However many gateways of the method of pristine awareness and roots there are, they all arise from the supreme prajñāpāramitā. When conditions gather, the machine of pristine awareness thoroughly arises; in the prajñāpāramitā there is no exhaustion and no increase.' In short, the prajñā that realizes selflessness is the chief of all causes that produce the attainment of liberation, because without it, it is impossible to attain the liberation of being freed from saṃsāra, for the Samādhirāja says: 'If one discriminates selflessness in dharmas, and whoever meditates having discriminated that, that is the cause of the result of attaining the liberation of nirvāṇa; whatever other causes there are, they will not bring peace.' Just as this states, there are many other sources as well, and it is extremely important. ^2-160

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-161]]

Considering it to be important in that way, the conclusion by way of demonstrating the benefits is: 'Subhūti, more than if any bodhisattva mahāsattva were to fill measureless world systems with the seven precious substances like gold, silver, pearls, and so forth, and give them as a gift, if any son of noble family or daughter of noble family, let alone completely, having written down even as little as a stanza of four lines pointing out the profound of this text of the prajñāpāramitā, memorizes it, or reads the volume, or holds the entire volume in hand, or comprehends, that is, knows all its words and meanings, or, just as they themselves know the words and meanings, correctly explains them extensively to other sentient beings as well—that very person, on the basis of having done that, would generate much more merit, meaning benefits, countless and measureless.' What is called 'merit' indicates both the accumulations of merit and pristine awareness, and from the three—generosity, ethical discipline, and patience—primarily the accumulation of merit is gathered, while from the two—concentration and prajñā—primarily the accumulation of pristine awareness is gathered, and from joyous effort, both arise. Therefore, doing hearing, reflection, and so forth on the prajñāpāramitā is primarily the gathering of the accumulation of pristine awareness. 'Well then, in the occasions of explaining the benefits in this text, how is it that what is called "heap of merit" appears so often?' That does not intend merely the accumulation of merit which is differentiated as one of the pair of the two accumulations, but refers to benefits by the name 'merit'. ^2-161

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-162]]

As for the manner of explaining this text: 'How does one correctly explain it?' The expression, explanation, and understanding are established as merely conventional, and because ultimately there are no expressed meaning, expressing words, and so forth, although in reality it is not correctly or ultimately explained, just as it is explained conventionally, here too one correctly explains the words and meanings unmistakably on a merely conventional level; for that reason, it is called 'correctly explaining'. As for understanding this well: one must well understand the manner in which expressing sounds engage with their own objects by the power of convention and terminology, and do not engage by the power of the things themselves; although this was briefly indicated above, here again the reason they do not engage by the power of the things themselves is like the fact that even one object can be designated with diverse names; for example, just as a single horse is designated with names in diverse regional languages by Indians, Chinese, Tibetans, Mongolians, and so forth, and in the Prajñāpāramitā Requested by Maitreya it is said that names are adventitiously imputed upon all dharmas such as form; thus, the engagement of names is by the power of convention, and not by the power of the things themselves. Therefore, understanding the meaning based on the name is also by the power of needing to apply convention, and if convention is not applied, one is unable to understand the meaning; for example, even if such and such names are spoken to a small child who does not know conventions, they will hear mere sounds, but they do not understand such and such meanings. Similarly, the understanding of that meaning by all others is also by the power of convention, and without convention, like a small child, they would hear mere sounds but would not understand the meaning. Dharmas cannot withstand analysis by reasoning that analyzes the ultimate, and in the perspective of the ārya's meditative equipoise all elaborations of dualistic appearance are thoroughly pacified, so being unable to express them in an elaborated manner in that perspective is even more profound than the former, for as it is said, 'Prajñāpāramitā, inexpressible, inconceivable, unutterable.' ^2-162

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^2-163]]

Then, having demonstrated that conditioned dharmas are lacking in nature and impermanent, the conclusion is: it is stated, 'A star, a visual aberration, a butter lamp...' To illustrate with the five aggregates, such as form, and so forth: For example, just as stars appear at night and do not appear during the day, similarly, conditioned things such as the aggregates appear ultimately if the darkness of ignorance is present, but when the sun of pristine awareness realizing lack of true existence has arisen, they do not appear ultimately, so they should be viewed like a star. Just as when the eye is obscured by visual aberrations like dust and so forth, objects do not appear as they are but are seen as something else, in the perspective where the eye of the mind is obscured by the visual aberration of ignorance, conditioned things also appear as something else. Just as a butter lamp quickly arises and is destroyed in dependence on a wick, similarly, conditioned things quickly arise and are destroyed in dependence on their own causes and conditions. Just as an illusion appears as something else, conditioned things appear as true in the perspective of a mistaken mind. Just as a dewdrop is quickly destroyed, conditioned things are quickly destroyed without remaining into their own second moment. Just as a water bubble suddenly arises and is destroyed from the agitation of water and so forth, conditioned things suddenly arise and are destroyed when conditions come together. Just as a dream is a mistaken appearance when tainted by sleep, conditioned things dawn as a true appearance in a perspective tainted by ignorance. Just as lightning quickly arises and is destroyed, conditioned things quickly arise and are destroyed when conditions come together. Just as clouds arise and are destroyed in the sky by the power of the intentions of nāgas and so forth, conditioned things undergo arising and destruction by the power of common and uncommon karma—by these examples as well, all conditioned things are explained as being without true existence. Those were explained by applying them to conditioned things in common; whereas in the sūtra it is said: 'Form is like a mass of foam, feeling is like a water bubble, perception is like a mirage, formations are like a plantain tree, consciousness is like an illusion, so the Kinsman of the Sun has declared'—so the protector Nāgārjuna says. Master Kamalaśīla explains the latter three by applying them to the three times in a manner different from the former, yet there is no contradiction. In short, it is said that all conditioned things as well should be viewed as impermanent and lacking in nature like those nine examples explained above. One should hold that this too demonstrates both the selflessness of persons and of dharmas. ^2-163

## The Concluding Section ^3-0

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^3-1]]

Third, the concluding sequence: After the Bhagavan had spoken those words, the elder Subhūti and those other one thousand two hundred and fifty monks, and those bodhisattvas, and the fourfold retinue of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen, and the beings of the world including gods, humans, asuras, and gandharvas, rejoiced and were delighted, and they highly praised what the Bhagavan had said. ^3-1

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^3-2]]

At the end of this, there occurs what is called the 'mantra of the essence of the Vajracchedikā'; since I have not seen this existing in the sūtras or tantras, it is certain that someone else composed and inserted it. Not only that, but the claim that 'reciting this essence once is equal to reading the Vajracchedikā ninety thousand times' is an extreme exaggeration of praise; however, it is not that one is not allowed to recite it. Thus, making an effort to compose commentaries on the intent of many texts such as this scripture was done because the Sūtra of Inconceivable Secrets says: 'The merit of bodhicitta / And whatever merit there is in upholding the true dharma / Cannot match even a sixteenth part / Of being inspired by emptiness,' thereby stating that great benefits arise from explaining and being inspired by the meaning of emptiness, and because the Sūtra Requested by the Devaputra Susthitamati says: 'All dharmas are included in two teachings: / The excellent speech and the commentaries on its intent. / By the power of those, the teaching of the Śākya as well / Will remain for a long time in this world system.' Thinking that this might be of some slight benefit to the Sage's teaching, I did this, and for no other reason. Just as the Teacher prophesied that the prajñāpāramitā would spread from the north to the northern region, in this northern direction, the Lord himself entered the tradition of illuminating the completely pure view, the essence of the prajñāpāramitā, according to the intent of the Noble Father and Son; following this, the numerous presentations of the view that I too composed commenting on the Lord's intent have spread in many directions. In particular, I composed commentaries on both the Root and the Entry, and these explanations of the intent of the Heart of Prajñā and this profound text according to the best of my intellectual ability, thinking that they might go in the direction prophesied by the Victor in that way; therefore, making an effort in this method is meaningful. ^3-2

![[bo-chone-drakpa-shedrub#^3-3]]

Let it be said: You, the twice-born bird flying in the sky of profound meaning, having vigorously flapped the wing-feathers of scripture and reasoning by the wind-force of previously accumulated good conduct and extensive hearing, have you not fallen into the abyss of meaningless, mistaken error? If there are any faulty explanations in this presentation, I confess and ask for forgiveness in the presence of the Lord of Sages himself; by your compassionate mind, having cleansed the stains of my faults, may you accept me until enlightenment. By the virtue of explaining the meaning of the most profound, profound sūtra, may I, an intelligent one who has heard many, many texts, find the basis for accomplishing the omniscient path in all my lifetimes, completely perfect the accumulations, and quickly attain perfect buddhahood! Mangalam. ^3-3
